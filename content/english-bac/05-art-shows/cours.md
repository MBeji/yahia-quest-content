# ⚔️ Art Shows — Reading the Programme, Saying What You Think

> 💡 "A leaflet never argues with you. It puts five facts on a page and lets you decide — and deciding out loud, with a reason, is the whole lesson."

A city announces its shows on a single folded sheet: a **leaflet**. Nobody reads it from the first word to the last. You hunt through it for the four or five facts you need, you choose, and then somebody asks you the hardest question of the day: _why that one?_ This lesson teaches you to do both — **read** a programme fast and safely, and **describe** what you saw and **say what you think of it** in a class discussion. On the way you will pick up the tool that makes an English description short and sharp, the **compound adjective**, learn where an English word puts its **stress**, and finish beside a river with a poem about **strings**.

## 🎫 Reading a leaflet — five questions, five lines

A leaflet answers five questions, and it answers them in that order almost every time:

**What? · When? · Where? · How much? · How do I get in?**

Here is a leaflet of that kind. Read it the way you would read it standing in the street — eyes hunting, not reading.

```
CITY ARTS CENTRE — SATURDAY

10.00 am   Holiday Fun           Studio 2                children 6–11 · £3
11.30 am   Guided Tour           Meet in the main foyer  free
12.30 pm   Lunchtime Concert     Concert Hall            £5 · refreshments served
 2.15 pm   Interactive Talk      Room 10                 free · no need to book
 7.00 pm   Youth Arts Party      Whole building          ages 12–19 · free
```

Four habits turn that block into information:

- **Read across a line, not down a column.** One line is one event, and it carries its own time, its own room and its own price.
- **A price list has categories.** _Adults £7.90 · children up to 14 £4.95 · under 3 free_ does not mean the visit costs £7.90: it means it costs £7.90 **for you**. Find your own category first.
- **A condition is as important as a price.** _ages 12–19_, _no need to book_ and _members only_ decide whether you get in at all.
- **Nothing is repeated.** If a line does not name a room, the room is the one printed at the top.

Larger programmes come in **three columns — DRAMA · MUSIC · CINEMA**. Each column is a separate list; comparing an event in one with an event in another means reading two lines that are nowhere near each other on the page.

> ⚠️ **programme** is the British spelling and **program** the American one. In the same leaflet you may also read **venue** — the place where an event is held.

## 🗣️ The words a programme uses

| Word              | What it means in a leaflet                            |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **foyer**         | the entrance hall, the large open space just inside   |
| **refreshments**  | drinks, and the light food served with them            |
| **interactive**   | you take part and communicate, you do not only watch  |
| **performance**   | a show given in front of an audience                  |
| **to book**       | to make a reservation before the day                  |
| **free**          | at no cost — you pay nothing                          |
| **guided tour**   | a visit led by somebody who explains what you see     |
| **exhibition**    | works shown to the public for a period                |
| **box office**    | the desk where tickets are sold                       |

> 🗡️ Two of these are traps for the eye. **free** does **not** mean "you may come in without booking" — it means you pay nothing, and a free event can still be fully booked. And a **foyer** is a space, never a person and never a drink: _We'll meet in the foyer and buy the refreshments there._

## 🕐 am and pm

The two letters after a time come from Latin, and they turn on **midday** (_meridiem_):

| Letters | Latin           | Meaning       | Example                            |
| ------- | --------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **am**  | ante meridiem   | before midday | _10.00 am_ — in the morning        |
| **pm**  | post meridiem   | after midday  | _7.00 pm_ — in the evening         |

So **12.30 pm** is half past twelve in the **middle of the day**, half an hour after midday — it comes **after** 11.30 am, not before. Getting that wrong is how people miss concerts.

## 🔗 Compound adjectives — describing in fewer words

English hates repeating a long relative clause. Instead it welds two or three words together with a **hyphen** and puts the result in front of the noun:

_a talk that lasts ten minutes_ → **a ten-minute talk**
_a gift whose idea comes from science_ → **a science-inspired gift**

That welded word is a **compound adjective**. It says exactly the same thing in half the space, which is why leaflets, reviews and tourist brochures are full of them.

| Pattern                       | Example                       | What it unpacks into                       |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| noun + past participle        | a **science-inspired** gift   | a gift inspired by science                 |
| noun + past participle        | **stone-walled** streets      | streets walled with stone                  |
| adverb + past participle      | **naturally produced** silk (no hyphen) | silk produced naturally          |
| adjective + noun + **-ed**    | **bright-coloured** umbrellas | umbrellas with bright colours              |
| noun + **-ing** form          | an **elephant-training** centre | a centre for training elephants          |
| number + noun (singular!)     | a **200-page** book           | a book of two hundred pages                |
| number + noun + adjective     | a **one-hundred-year-old** tree | a tree that is one hundred years old     |

> ⚠️ **Every part of the chain is hyphenated, the number included.** _a one-hundred-year-old tree_, _a twenty-five-metre pool_. Your textbook prints _a one million-year-old teak tree_ with the first hyphen missing; the full chain is the safe form to write.

The official pattern of this lesson is the first one: **noun + past participle**, as in _science-inspired_.

Three rules hold the whole family together:

1. **The hyphen joins the parts.** Without it the reader meets two separate words and stumbles.
2. **The noun inside never takes -s.** _a six-**hour** flight_, _a four-**door** car_, _a 30-**dinar** bank note_ — even though the flight lasts six hours and the note is worth thirty dinars.
3. **A compound of measurement only works in front of its noun.** _a six-hour flight_, _a 200-page book_, _a one-hundred-year-old tree_ — after the verb _be_ you must unpack them: _The flight lasts six hours._ ✓ — "The flight is six-hour." ✗ A compound that describes a **quality**, on the other hand, stays whole after _be_: _The film **is** thought-provoking._ ✓ _The umbrellas **are** bright-coloured._ ✓

> ⚠️ **An adverb in -ly takes no hyphen.** _naturally produced silk_, _freshly painted walls_, _highly praised film_ — the _-ly_ already tells the reader that the word belongs to the participle, so the hyphen has no work left to do. Your textbook prints _naturally-produced_; write it without the hyphen.

And you must be able to play the game in **both directions**:

- **Compressing** — _a park ornamented with flowers_ → _a **flower-ornamented** park._
- **Unpacking** — _a one-hundred-year-old olive tree_ → _The olive tree **is** one hundred years **old**._

> ⚠️ Age is expressed with **be**, never with **have**: _She **is** eighteen (years old)._ ✓ — "She has eighteen years." ✗ That mistake comes straight from French and survives all the way to the bac.

> ⚠️ **-ing or -ed?** The two endings say opposite things. An **elephant-training** centre trains elephants; an **elephant-trained** keeper would be a keeper trained by elephants. Ask yourself who does the action.

## 🖼️ Describing what you saw

Put the compounds to work and a description stops being a list:

_We came out into a **glass-fronted** foyer, walked down **stone-walled** streets and ate under a **tree-lined** avenue._

Notice how much is packed in. Each compound replaces a clause, and the sentence keeps moving. That is the effect examiners call **precision**: not more words, better ones.

## 💬 Expressing an opinion — the class discussion

A leaflet gives **facts**. A discussion asks for **opinions**. The difference is simple and it is worth a mark:

| |  |
| --- | --- |
| **A fact** | can be checked. _The play starts at eight._ _Stephen Frears directed the film._ |
| **An opinion** | can be disagreed with. _The play is far too long._ _The ending is the best part._ |

The exponents you need, all of them short:

| Function            | What you say                                                             |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| giving an opinion   | _In my opinion… · I think… · I find it… · To me… · It seems to me that…_ |
| supporting it       | _…because… · What I liked most was… · That is why I'd choose…_           |
| agreeing            | _So do I. · That's true. · I agree with you there._                      |
| disagreeing softly  | _I don't quite agree. · That's true, but… · I see it differently._       |
| asking for one      | _What do you think? · And you? · Do you agree?_                          |

> 🗡️ An opinion with no reason is half a turn. _"I'd choose the drum show — you play the instruments yourself."_ The dash carries the mark: opinion, then reason.

When the opinion is about a film, the review vocabulary does the judging:

| Word                     | What it says                                     |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **wit**                  | clever, quick humour                             |
| **shot**                 | one scene as the camera filmed it                |
| **adept** (at)           | skilful at something                             |
| **thought-provoking**    | it makes the audience think                      |
| **to tackle a subject**  | to deal with a difficult subject instead of avoiding it |
| **film director**        | the person who directs the film                  |
| **script writer**        | the person who writes the words                  |

## 🔊 Word stress

Every English word of two syllables or more has **one** syllable said louder, longer and clearer than the rest. Put it in the wrong place and a listener may simply not recognise the word.

| Rule                                            | Examples                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **-ion** → stress the syllable just **before** it | ex-hi-**BI**-tion · dis-**CUS**-sion · in-spi-**RA**-tion       |
| **-ic** → stress the syllable just **before** it  | dra-**MA**-tic · ar-**TIS**-tic · en-thu-si-**AS**-tic          |
| **-ity** → stress the syllable just **before** it | ac-**TIV**-i-ty · pop-u-**LAR**-i-ty                            |
| **compound noun** → stress the **first** word     | **FILM** director · **SCRIPT** writer · **BOX** office · **TICKET** office |
| **noun / verb pairs** → noun first, verb second   | a **RE**-cord / to re-**CORD** · a **PRE**-sent / to pre-**SENT** |

The words of this lesson, marked: per-**FOR**-mance · re-**FRESH**-ments · in-ter-**AC**-tive · ex-hi-**BI**-tion · **FOY**-er (British; Americans say foy-**ER**) · **CIN**-e-ma · **AU**-di-ence.

> ⚠️ In an ordinary adjective + noun phrase the noun keeps the stress (_a free per**FOR**mance_, _a crowded **FOY**er_), but a **compound noun** pulls it forward (_a **TICK**et office_). That is how the ear tells "a **BLACK** bird" (a blackbird, the bird) from "a black **BIRD**" (any bird that is black).

> ⚠️ A few words in -ic refuse the rule: **A**-ra-bic, **PO**-li-tics, **CA**-tho-lic.

## 🎼 Strings — a poem, a word, a rhyme

**James Joyce** (1882–1941) was born in Dublin and became one of the great Irish writers of the twentieth century. He took his degree in 1902 and then earned his living as a journalist, a teacher and much else, often in real financial difficulty. His last book, _Finnegans Wake_, is held by some critics to be a masterpiece; his early poems, much gentler, were collected long before it.

One of them, **"Strings in the earth and air"**, is the poem of this lesson. It is short — three stanzas — and everything in it happens in a single place: a riverbank, with trees, an instrument being played, and the figure of Love. Joyce does not put music and love side by side as two subjects; for him the music that is heard **is** the presence of love. Read the poem in your book and listen for its **tone** rather than for events, because nothing happens in it — that is the question the lesson asks you, and the answer lies in that stillness: quiet, tender, unhurried.

### The word _string_

_String_ is a word with several lives. A dictionary lists them in order:

| Sense                                   | Example                                    |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| thin cord for tying                     | _Tie the package with string._             |
| a chain, a series of things owned       | _He owns a string of shops._               |
| a queue, a line of people or things     | _A string of visitors waited under the arch._ |
| a strand of beads on a thread           | _a string of coloured beads_               |
| the wire of a musical instrument        | _The musician broke a string._             |

Choosing between them is a reading skill: **the sentence around the word decides**, never the word alone. And once you have chosen, ask a second question — is the word **literal** (a real piece of cord, a real wire) or **figurative** (a "string" of shops is not tied to anything)? In the poem the strings are the wires of an instrument, and the music they make stands for something larger. Which makes the answer a double one: the word names a real wire, so the sense is the instrument's string — but the title hangs those strings in the earth and in the air, where no instrument stands, and that makes the use **figurative**.

### Rhyme scheme

A **quatrain** is a stanza of four lines. We describe how its lines rhyme with letters: the same letter means the same rhyme sound.

| Scheme      | Name              | Which lines rhyme          |
| ----------- | ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| **a b a b** | alternate rhyme   | 1 with 3, 2 with 4         |
| **a a b b** | rhyming couplets  | 1 with 2, 3 with 4         |
| **a b b a** | enclosed rhyme    | 1 with 4, 2 with 3         |

Two rules follow, and both are tested:

- A **new sound gets a new letter.** So a second quatrain whose rhymes are all new is **c d c d**, not a second _a b a b_ — writing _a b a b_ again would claim it rhymes with the first stanza.
- **A word does not rhyme with itself.** Repeating _flute_ at the end of two lines is not a rhyme; you need _mute_, _route_, _fruit_.

> 🗡️ Your own turn: choose a tree you love and a musical instrument, and write two quatrains, **a b a b** then **c d c d**. Pick your rhyming words **first** — the poem is much easier to write backwards.

## 🎤 A voice worth a word list — Ray Charles

The lesson closes on **Ray Charles**, the American singer and pianist who mixed gospel with blues and changed popular music. His short biography carries eight words that come back in every text of praise:

| Word                | Meaning                                                    |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **inspiration**     | stimulation, motivation, encouragement                     |
| **single-handedly** | without anyone's help                                      |
| **posthumous**      | happening after a person's death                           |
| **pioneering**      | introducing ideas never used before                        |
| **encompassed**     | included                                                   |
| **stature**         | the importance and respect earned by what one has achieved |
| **spell**           | fascination, an influence you cannot resist                |
| **indisputably**    | undeniably, beyond argument                                |

> ⚠️ **spell** is another word with several lives: _to spell a word_, _a magic spell_, _a spell of hot weather_. Here it is the fascination sense.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

- _a six-**hours** flight_ ✗ → **a six-hour flight** — the noun inside a compound never takes -s.
- _The flight is six-hour._ ✗ → **The flight lasts six hours.** — unpack a compound of **measurement** after _be_ (a compound of quality, _thought-provoking_, stays whole).
- _**naturally-produced** silk_ ✗ → **naturally produced silk** — an adverb in _-ly_ never takes a hyphen.
- _She has eighteen years._ ✗ → **She is eighteen.** — age takes _be_.
- _an elephant-**trained** centre_ ✗ → **an elephant-training centre** — the centre trains, it is not trained.
- _12.30 pm is before 11.30 am_ ✗ — pm is after midday, so 12.30 pm comes half an hour after midday.
- _"Free" means I can just walk in_ ✗ — free is about **money**; booking is a separate condition.
- _A second quatrain rhyming a b a b_ ✗ → **c d c d** when the sounds are new.

> 🏆 Gate cleared, hero. You can read a programme without missing your show, build a description that says more in fewer words, and hold your ground in a discussion with an opinion and a reason.
