# 📜 Summary: The Winter's Tale (Part 1)

- **What a narrative text is for** — to **entertain**, to **tell a story**, to give a literary pleasure. It grows out of life as people live it, is built round **people** rather than facts, lets them **speak**, and uses **familiar language**. It is organised round a **plot**: not one event but a **chain of episodes** arranged by the author to hold your attention and raise the tension as the story moves on.
- **The four windows** — **setting** (where? when?) · **characters** (who, and how are they tied to one another?) · **plot** (what happens, and in what order?) · **theme** (what is it really about?). **Theme is not plot**: the events are the plot, the idea they carry is the theme — and the theme is sometimes printed plainly, as in a fable, and sometimes left **between the lines**. **Setting is not decoration**: it works on the theme and on what the reader feels.
- **Kinds of narrative** — **imaginary**, **factual**, or a **mixture of the two** (a novel built on a real ship's log, its talk on deck invented, is a mixture — no fault at all). The family is large: the marvellous (myths and legends, fairy stories, science fiction, horror), the ordinary (slices of life, personal experience, romances), a puzzle or a chase (mysteries, adventure stories), a lesson or a record (fables, ballads, historical narratives). The four that get confused: **fable** (talking animals + a moral) · **myth** (gods and beginnings) · **historical narrative** (real people, real past) · **slice of life** (one ordinary moment).
- **The five stages of a plot** — **introduction** (the balance before anything goes wrong) · **problem** (the single moment that breaks it) · **climax / crisis** (the peak of the tension) · **resolution** (how it is settled) · **conclusion** (what is left). To tell the introduction from the problem: the introduction says what **used to** happen, the problem happens **once** and is marked — _one autumn_, _one night_.
- **The tale** — a play by **Shakespeare**, retold in prose by **Charles and Mary Lamb** for readers not yet ready for the plays, with two aims: **pleasure** and a **moral lesson**. Part 1: a king who has everything grows jealous of his wife and his oldest friend on no evidence, orders the friend poisoned (the lord warns him instead and flees with him), sends the queen to prison, takes their small son from her — the boy sickens — and sends to the **oracle** for the god's answer; in prison the queen gives birth to a daughter Part 1 never names. Words: **jealousy** · **suspicion** · an **oracle** · an **heir** · **grief** · **sorrow**.
- **Reading a tale, twice** — first for the **people and the ties between them** (a story drops relationships in passing: _his queen_, _the young prince_, _a lord of the court_; invent nothing the text withholds), then for the **notes**, in two columns, _Characters | Action_. Rule: **hunt the main verbs** — a line earns its place only if it records something somebody **did**. Description, background and colour stay out.
- **`so` + adjective + `that`** — the first half says **how far** something went, the second says **what came of it**: cause and consequence in one sentence. _The bread was **so** hard **that** the children soaked it in their tea._ Joining two ideas: the first supplies the adjective, the second becomes the `that`-clause. If the first gives you a **noun**, convert it — _fear → frightened_, _shame → ashamed_, _anger → angry_, _joy → happy_, _sorrow → sad_. And the `that`-clause must be a **consequence**, not merely the next fact.
- **The other jobs of `so`** — _narrow, **so** the bus reversed_ (a plain link, no degree) · _**so** narrow!_ (an exclamation, no effect stated) · _and **so** was the bridge_ ("the same is true of"). Only `so … that` names a degree **and** its result. In writing, **never drop the `that`**.
- **Writing the summary (five lines)** — in: the situation you start from, the event that broke it, the two or three acts that followed, the point the story has reached. Out: description, dialogue, small details, and **your own opinion or invention**. Three rules: keep the **order** of the events, keep **one tense**, and make it **much shorter** than the text.
- **Linking the summary** — mostly connectors of **time**: _at first_ / _for years_ → _one day_ / _then_ → _soon afterwards_ / _after that_ → **_meanwhile_** (two things at the same time, in different places — never for two events that follow each other) → _in the end_ / _finally_; plus _as a result_ / _therefore_ where one event plainly produced the next.
- **My new words** — **to beg** (ask anxiously, and more than once — stronger than _ask_) · **evil** (deliberately cruel, and without remorse afterwards) · **to rule** (to govern and make laws) · **to send for someone** (send word that you want somebody — nothing is sent but the message) · **shamefully** (so badly that those who saw it were ashamed for him) · **unfaithfulness** (being untrue to the person you are married to).
