# 📖 The Winter's Tale (Part 1) — Reading a Tale, and Telling It Back

> 💡 "A story is a chain. Pull on any link and the whole thing moves — which is why you can hand a hundred pages back in five lines."

This is the last lesson of the unit, and it changes the exercise. Until now you have read leaflets, advertisements, e-mails and conversations — texts that inform you, sell you something or answer you. Now you read a **tale**: a text whose only business is to take you somewhere and keep you there.

Three things are asked of you. **See how a story is built**, because a reader who knows the parts stops being surprised by them and starts predicting them. **Read a real tale** and keep track of who does what. And **give it back in five lines** — the skill an examiner tests most often, and the one on which the whole unit closes. The grammar comes from the tale itself: one small pattern, **so … that**, which says how far something went and what came of it.

## 📚 1. What a narrative text is for

An advertisement wants your money. A report wants you informed. A **narrative text** wants something else: it sets out to **entertain**, to **tell a story**, and to give you the pleasure that literature gives.

Four habits follow from that purpose, and you can spot all four in the first paragraph of any tale:

- it grows out of **life as people live it** — even a story about dragons is about fear, loyalty and greed;
- it is **built round people** (or round animals and creatures given a person's mind and voice), not round facts;
- it lets its people **speak**, so dialogue runs through it;
- it uses the **familiar language** of ordinary life, not the technical language of a manual.

And it is organised round a **plot**: not one event but a **chain of episodes**, arranged by the author in the order that holds your attention and raises the tension as the story moves on. That last point is worth pausing over. The events of a tale are not laid out in the order that would explain them fastest. They are laid out in the order that makes you want the next one.

## 🧭 2. The four things a reader looks for

Every narrative can be examined through four windows. Learn the four words: an examination question will use them, and a reader who cannot name them cannot talk about a book.

| The window | The question it answers | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **setting** | Where? When? | the place and the moment — a walled town in winter, a ship in the year 1790 |
| **characters** | Who? | the people, animals or invented creatures the story follows, and what ties them to one another |
| **plot** | What happens, and in what order? | the run of events, from the calm opening to the last page |
| **theme** | What is it really about? | the idea or the message the events carry — jealousy, forgiveness, the price of a lie |

Two warnings, and they cover almost every mistake made on this lesson.

**Theme is not plot.** _A fisherman finds a ring and gives it back to its owner_ is the plot: it tells you what happened. _Honesty can cost more than it pays_ is the theme: it tells you what the happening was for. Ask yourself which of the two you could still say if the people were renamed and moved to the other side of the world — that one is the theme.

**Theme is sometimes spoken and sometimes not.** A fable prints its lesson in the last line and leaves you no work to do. Most other narratives leave it **between the lines**: no character ever pronounces it, and you build it yourself out of what the people do and what it costs them.

One more thing about **setting**: it is never decoration. Move a story from an open hillside to a locked room and the same events will frighten you instead of amusing you. Setting works on the theme, and it works on what the reader feels.

## 🗂️ 3. Kinds of narrative

A narrative may be **imaginary**, **factual**, or — very often — **a mixture of the two**. A ship's log — the book in which a captain writes the dates, the ports and the weather — never holds a word of what was said on deck; a novelist who builds a story on one has to put the words into the sailors' mouths himself, and what he produces is a mixture, which is no fault at all.

The family is large. Some kinds live on the marvellous — myths and legends, fairy stories, science fiction, horror; some on the ordinary — slices of life, personal experience, romances; some on a puzzle or a chase — mysteries, adventure stories; and some carry a lesson or a record — fables, ballads, historical narratives.

Four of them are regularly confused, so fix them now:

| Kind | Its signature |
| --- | --- |
| **fable** | short, animals who talk, and a moral lesson at the end |
| **myth** | gods and beginnings — how the world, a people or a custom came to be |
| **historical narrative** | real people and real events of the past, told as a story |
| **slice of life** | one ordinary moment of ordinary life, with no marvel in it at all |

## 🧱 4. The shape of a plot

A plot is not a heap of episodes. It has a shape, and the shape has names. Your book uses these five.

| Stage | Its work in the story |
| --- | --- |
| **introduction** | the balance before anything goes wrong: who these people are, where, when |
| **problem** | the single moment that breaks that balance and sets everything moving |
| **climax** (or **crisis**) | the peak of the tension: the story cannot stay here, and the next move decides how it ends |
| **resolution** | how the trouble is settled |
| **conclusion** | what is left when it is over |

Here is the shape at work, in five lines:

> The old dog had slept on the same step outside the grocer's shop since anyone could remember. _(introduction)_
> On a Thursday in March the shop changed hands, and the new owner nailed a board across the step. _(problem)_
> The dog waited in the road; the children of the street fed it there; two old customers stopped coming in. _(episodes of the development)_
> On the evening when the new owner found it lying in the rain on the far pavement, three of his neighbours were watching him from their doors. _(climax)_
> He took the board down before dark, and the step was a step again. _(resolution and conclusion)_

> ⚠️ **Telling the introduction from the problem.** The introduction describes what **used to** happen — repeated, stable, no date. The problem happens **once**, and the text usually marks it: _one autumn_, _one night_, _that Tuesday_. When you are asked which event sets the story moving, look for the single occasion, not for the habit.

## 🎭 5. The tale: *The Winter's Tale*, Part 1

The text of this lesson is a tale drawn from a play by **William Shakespeare**. The prose version you read was made by **Charles and Mary Lamb**, a brother and sister who retold Shakespeare's plays for readers not yet ready for the plays themselves. They stayed as close to his words as they could, and they had two aims at once: to give **pleasure** and to leave the reader a **moral lesson**. A tale is easier than a play — nobody has to imagine a stage — but the story underneath is the same story.

**What happens in Part 1**, in short. Leontes, king of Sicily, has everything a man can be given: a queen he trusts, Hermione; a small son, Mamillius; a kingdom at peace. He invites the friend of his childhood, the king of Bohemia, to stay, and when the visitor is about to leave, the queen persuades him to stay a little longer. From that harmless success something turns over in the king. With no evidence of any kind, he decides that his wife and his guest have betrayed him. He orders a lord of his court to poison the guest; the lord warns the guest instead and escapes with him across the sea. The king then sends his queen to prison and takes their little boy from her, and the child, who cannot understand what has happened to his mother, stops eating and sleeping and falls ill. Two messengers are sent to the oracle at Delphi to bring back the god's answer. In prison, the queen gives birth to a daughter — a girl Part 1 never names. There the first part stops.

Six words will help you talk about it:

| Word | What it names |
| --- | --- |
| **jealousy** | the fear that someone you love prefers another, and the anger that comes with it |
| **suspicion** | the belief that someone has done wrong, held without proof |
| **an oracle** | a holy place where a god was asked a question, and the answer that came back |
| **an heir** | the person who will receive a title, a crown or a fortune after its holder |
| **grief** | the deep pain that follows a loss |
| **sorrow** | sadness, especially long and quiet sadness |

## 📝 6. Reading a tale: who is who, and who did what

A tale is read **twice**, and the two readings do different work.

**First reading — the people and the ties between them.** A story almost never announces its relationships in a list. It drops them in passing, and you assemble them: _his queen_ tells you a marriage, _the young prince_ tells you a son and an heir, _a lord of the court_ tells you rank without family, _a lady serving the queen_ tells you service. Draw the family as a small tree if it helps, and mark each name with the **status** the text gives it. Do not invent what the text has not said: if a name is missing, the answer is that the text does not give it.

**Second reading — the notes.** Take a sheet, rule it in two columns, and fill it as you read:

| Characters | Action / event |
| --- | --- |
| the new owner | nailed a board across the step |
| the children of the street | fed the dog in the road |
| two old customers | stopped coming to the shop |

The rule for this table is short: **hunt the main verbs**. A line earns its place only if it records something somebody **did** — an act that pushes the story on. What the weather was like, how old the shop was, what colour its door was: all of that is true, all of it is in the text, and none of it belongs in the notes. Those notes are not a private habit, either. They are the raw material of the paragraph you are about to write.

## 🔗 7. Grammar: `so` + adjective + `that`

Read this sentence and watch it work in two moves:

> _The bread was **so** hard **that** the children soaked it in their tea._

The first half tells you **how far** something went — not merely hard, hard to a degree. The second half tells you **what came of it**. The pattern names a cause and its consequence in one sentence, and the book's own box records it exactly so:

> **so + adjective + that + result** — cause and consequence

Three things to hold on to.

**① Use it to join two ideas.** The first sentence supplies the adjective, the second becomes the `that`-clause:

> _The market was crowded. We lost each other in two minutes._
> → _The market was **so** crowded **that** we lost each other in two minutes._

Applied to the tale you have just read, the same move gives you sentences like this one:

> _The king was **so** jealous **that** he shut his own wife away._

**② When the first idea gives you a noun, find the adjective.** `so` will not take a noun after it, so you must convert:

> _The old man felt great shame._ + _He did not come out of his house for a week._
> ✗ "He had so shame that he did not come out of his house for a week."
> ✓ _He was **so** ashamed **that** he did not come out of his house for a week._

Keep a short list ready for the examination: _fear → frightened_, _shame → ashamed_, _anger → angry_, _joy → happy_, _sorrow → sad_, _surprise → surprised_, _tiredness → tired_.

**③ The second half must be a consequence, not simply the next fact.** This is where marks are lost:

> ✓ _The lamp was so weak that nobody could read by it._ — the weakness explains the reading.
> ✗ "The lamp was so weak that my uncle had bought it in Sfax." — true, perhaps, but no degree of weakness could ever bring it about.

### Three other jobs of the little word `so`

Only one of the four is the one you have just learned. Learn to tell them apart, because the examination mixes them.

| Sentence | What `so` is doing |
| --- | --- |
| _The road was **so** narrow **that** the bus had to reverse._ | **this lesson**: a degree, and its consequence |
| _The road was narrow, **so** the bus had to reverse._ | a plain link between two clauses — no degree is named |
| _The road was **so** narrow!_ | an exclamation: a degree, but nothing said about its effect |
| _The road was narrow, and **so** was the bridge._ | `so` meaning "the same is true of" |

In writing, **never drop the `that`**. _"The road was so narrow, the bus had to reverse"_ passes in speech; in a composition it costs you a mark, because the sentence no longer shows which half caused which.

## ✍️ 8. Writing the summary

The unit ends here: you turn your notes into a **paragraph of about five lines**.

**What goes in** — one line for the situation you started from, the event that broke it, the two or three acts that followed, and the point the story has reached. Nothing else.

**What stays out** — the descriptions, the small vivid details, the dialogue, and above all **your opinion**. A summary reports the story; it does not judge it and it does not add to it. A single invented word ("the king had always been cruel") turns a summary into something else.

**Three rules that decide the mark:**

1. **Keep the order of the events.** A summary that jumps about is unreadable.
2. **Keep one tense.** Choose the past or choose the present, then stay there to the last line.
3. **Make it much shorter than the text.** A summary nearly as long as its original has understood nothing. Five lines means five lines.

**Link the sentences.** A row of short statements is not a paragraph. Sew them together, mostly with connectors of **time**, and with a connector of **consequence** wherever one event plainly produced the next:

| Where you are | Connectors to reach for |
| --- | --- |
| the situation you begin from | _at first_ · _for years_ · _in the beginning_ |
| the event that breaks it | _one day_ · _then_ · _suddenly_ |
| what follows it | _soon afterwards_ · _after that_ · _later_ |
| something happening at the same time, elsewhere | _meanwhile_ · _in the meantime_ |
| an event produced by the one before | _as a result_ · _therefore_ · _so_ |
| the point the story has reached | _in the end_ · _finally_ · _by the close of Part 1_ |

> ⚠️ **meanwhile** is not a synonym of _then_. It says that two things were happening **at the same time in different places** — the messengers were on the road **while** the queen was in prison. Used for two events that follow each other, it is simply wrong.

## 🏷️ 9. My new words

| Word | What it means | In a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **to beg** | to ask for something anxiously, and more than once | _He begged for work at every door in the street._ |
| **evil** | deeply and deliberately cruel: the harm is meant, and no remorse follows it | _An evil counsellor whispered against the queen._ |
| **to rule** | to govern a country or a people, and make its laws | _When the old chief died, nobody knew who would rule in his place._ |
| **to send for someone** | to send word that you want somebody, so that they come to you | _The judge sent for the witness who had left the room._ |
| **shamefully** | so badly that the people who saw it were ashamed for him | _She treated the girl who had helped her shamefully, and never apologised._ |
| **unfaithfulness** | being untrue to the person you are married to | _He accused her of unfaithfulness and would hear nothing._ |

> 💡 **send for** is one of those verbs whose meaning is not the sum of its words: nothing is sent except word, and it is the person who arrives. Take care with **shamefully** too: it does not mean "in an embarrassing way". Embarrassment is a small discomfort; this word passes a judgement on what somebody did. And **to beg** is stronger than _to ask_: it belongs to a person whose only argument is his need.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The story is about a fisherman who returns a ring." (as a **theme**) | _The story is about what honesty costs._ | The events are the plot; the idea they carry is the theme |
| "The soup was such salty that nobody finished it." | _The soup was **so** salty that nobody finished it._ | `such` must be followed by a noun group (_such salty soup_), never by an adjective on its own |
| "The crowd had so anger that the gates were shut." | _The crowd was **so angry** that the gates were shut._ | `so` takes an **adjective**; turn the noun into one |
| "The waiting room was so cold, we kept our coats on." (written work) | _The waiting room was so cold **that** we kept our coats on._ | The `that` is what shows which half caused which |
| "It rained, so we stayed in." offered as this lesson's pattern | _The rain was **so** heavy **that** we stayed in._ | A plain `so` links two clauses; it names no degree |
| Notes reading "the step was of grey stone" | _the new owner nailed a board across the step_ | Notes record **acts**, not description |
| A five-line summary that retells only the opening | one line per stage, as far as the story goes | A summary must reach the end, not stop at the setting |
| "The messengers left. Meanwhile they came back." | _The messengers left. **In the end** they came back._ | **meanwhile** = at the same time, not afterwards |
| Naming the king's daughter in a summary of Part 1 | _the queen gives birth to a daughter_ | Part 1 does not name her; do not add what the text withholds |

> 🏆 Gate cleared, hero. You can take a tale apart into its four windows and its five stages, follow who did what across a court in uproar, say how far something went and what came of it in one clean sentence, and give a whole story back in five lines.
