# 📜 Summary: Virtual Schools

- **The report, in four facts** — in **January 2006**, **Shan Jayran** and her **husband John Davies** **opened First College**, an **on-line high school**; it **now has twenty-two students**. Two listening traps: *her husband John Davies* is **one** man (two founders, not three), and **twenty-two** is the number of students **at the moment of the report** — not the number who have ever enrolled.
- **The timetable** — **0900** log on to the internet and chat to classmates in the "school hall" · **0930** classes start · **1030** break · **1100** Lesson 2 · **1200** lunch · **1300** last class · **1400** "home time". Three readings the grid never spells out: **1)** the nine o'clock line — connecting to the internet — could stand on no other school's timetable; **2)** only **"school hall"** and **"home time"** wear inverted commas, because this school has no hall and nobody travels home: the words are **borrowed** from a school with walls; **3)** from **0900 to 1400 is five hours**, and the day holds **three** classes (0930, 1100, 1300).
- **The five new words** — **bullying** = frightening or hurting a weaker person · **to log on** = to connect a computer to a system · **appeal** = **what makes something attractive** · **texting** = writing on the computer · **bustle** = a crowd of people moving about in a busy way. ⚠️ The matching exercise prints **six** definitions for five words: *testing* fits none of them, so never match by elimination — check that the last definition really fits the last word.
- **Bustle blames nobody** — it names movement, not cruelty; that is what separates it from **bullying**, which is one person deliberately frightening another. Around them: **to enrol**, **bustling**, **panic attacks**, **side-effects**, **drawbacks**.
- **Enrol ≠ log on** — you **enrol once**, and your name stays on the roll; you **log on every morning**, and you are logged off the moment the connection drops. One is a decision, the other a gesture: *twenty-two have enrolled* and *twenty-two logged on this morning* are two different counts.
- **The two young people** — **Zack, 12**: **panic attacks** and **bullying**. **Natalie, 13**: **afraid of speaking in class**, and a **strict** teacher. Neither reason mentions a lesson, a subject or an examination: both name **what happens around the teaching**. A virtual school teaches nothing new — it **removes the surroundings**.
- **Benefits** — the pupils **do feel better**; two things stop happening to them (**bullying**, and the **bustling** crowd); they are **more confident**; and all they have to do is **log on** and start **texting**. **The one drawback stated aloud** — *sitting at a computer for five hours **does** have side-effects*: **side-effects** are the unwanted effects that come with something useful (aching back, tired eyes), and the **five hours** are the school's own timetable, from nine to two. Test: a **benefit** is what the pupil gains, a **drawback** what the pupil pays.
- **Agreeing and disagreeing — five openings** : _I think…_ (opens an opinion, grants nothing) · _I'm afraid I don't agree…_ (**refuses flatly**, politely) · _I see what you mean, but…_ · _You are making a good point, however…_ · _What you say is … but I still strongly believe…_ — the **last three share a hinge**: they grant the other speaker a point, then turn it round. Granting the true half is what earns you a hearing; repeating that you disagree answers nothing.
- **The emphatic form** — **do / does / did + the infinitive without _to_ → to emphasize the meaning**. Book example: **He does work hard**. **1)** the auxiliary carries the person and the tense, so the verb goes **bare**: *does work* (~~does works~~), *did enrol* (~~did enrolled~~). **2)** choose it as you would the verb's own ending: **do** for I/you/we/they, **does** for he/she/it, **did** for the past. **3)** it means **"it is true that…"** — never *more often*, *harder* or *longer*. **4)** it almost always answers a doubt somebody has just raised, and the voice **rises on the auxiliary**.
- **Silent letters** — a letter **written and never sounded** (it is still written: "autum" is wrong). The ten words: **right** (gh) · **half** (l) · **taught** (gh) · **friend** (i) · **bustle** (t) · **psychology** (p) · **knowledge** (k) · **comb** (b) · **knee** (k) · **through** (gh). Families: **kn-** loses its **k**, **-gh-** is silent in a whole tribe.
- **The hidden word** — take the silent letter of **plumber** (b), **biscuits** (u), **harmful** (r), **autumn** (n), **listen** (t), in that order: **b-u-r-n-t** → **burnt**, the past of *burn*. Get one letter wrong and the word does not come out. ⚠️ The **r** of *harmful* is silent in the **British** pronunciation the book teaches (an *r* before a consonant is not sounded), not in every accent. And the chain is a game you can build yourself (the book asks you to): *knee, autumn, friend, bustle* gives **knit** — change one word in a chain and the word at the bottom changes, so a chain is decoded, never recalled.
- **Emphasizing a word in a sentence** — same words, same order, one word pressed, and each saying answers a different question. _I read a tale last week_: press **I** → *Who read a tale last week?* · press **read** → *What did you do last week?* · press **a tale** → *What did you read?* · press **last week** → *When did you read a tale?* And with _Mary enrolled in a virtual school last summer_: **Mary** = who · **enrolled** = what did she do · **in a virtual school** = where · **last summer** = when. The machine runs **both ways**: the stressed word **is** the answer, and everything else was already in the question.
