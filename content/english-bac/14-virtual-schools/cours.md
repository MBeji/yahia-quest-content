# ⚔️ Virtual Schools — The School with No Corridor

> 💡 "Take the building away. Take the corridor, the yard, the bell, the bus. What is left is still called a school — and this lesson asks you to say why."

The last lesson counted the children who never reach a classroom. This one meets children who **left** one, and who are at school all the same: they log on at nine in the morning and they are in class by half past.

This is a **listening lesson**. Somewhere a journalist has recorded a report; you will hear it in two parts, and everything you learn about this new kind of school will come to you **through your ears**, from a voice that goes past once and does not wait. That changes the work. A page can be read again; a voice cannot. So you listen for the things a report is built out of — a date, a name, a number, a reason — and you write them down as they go by.

Then you argue. One of you will be for these schools and one against, and there is a right way to disagree with somebody without slamming a door on them. And on the way you pick up **one small grammatical trick** that this whole lesson turns on: the trick of putting the word **do** in front of a verb that did not need it, so that the sentence stops describing and starts **insisting**.

> 🗡️ What you must be able to do by the end: **work in pairs to express an opinion**, and **emphasize a word in a sentence** — with your grammar, and with your voice.

## 🏫 A high school with no address

The report opens on facts, and they are the sort of facts a listening exercise is made of: **when**, **who**, **what**, **how many**.

| The question | What the report gives                                  |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **When?**    | **January 2006**                                        |
| **Who?**     | **Shan Jayran** and her **husband**, **John Davies**     |
| **What?**    | they **opened First College**, an **on-line high school** |
| **How many?**| it **now has twenty-two students**                       |

An **on-line** school is one that exists where its pupils' computers meet, and nowhere else. There is no gate, no playground, no address a parent can drive to. What holds it together is a **connection** and a **timetable**.

> ⚠️ Two of those four facts are traps for a careless listener. *Her husband John Davies* is **one** man, named twice over — the pair who opened the college are two people, not three. And **twenty-two** is the number of students the college has **now**, at the moment the report is being made. It is not the number who have ever enrolled, nor the number who will be there next year. A figure heard on the radio always carries a date, spoken or understood; take the figure and drop the date, and you have written down something the report never said.

## ⏰ The timetable, and the two words in inverted commas

A virtual school still prints a day. Here it is, and it is worth reading line by line, because almost all of it is ordinary and one line of it could not exist anywhere else.

| Time | What happens |
| ---- | ------------- |
| **0900** | **log on** to the internet, and chat to classmates in the "school hall" |
| **0930** | classes start |
| **1030** | break |
| **1100** | Lesson 2 |
| **1200** | lunch |
| **1300** | last class |
| **1400** | "home time" |

Three things this grid tells you that nobody says out loud.

**1. One line belongs to no other school on earth.** Every school has classes, a break, a lunch and a last hour. Not one of them begins the day by asking its pupils to **connect to the internet**. That opening line is the whole difference between this school and yours, and it is printed as if it were as ordinary as the bell.

**2. Two expressions are wearing inverted commas, and only those two.** Nobody puts *break* or *lunch* in inverted commas, because a break is a break anywhere. But this school has no hall to gather in, and nobody travels home at two o'clock — they are already there. The words **"school hall"** and **"home time"** have been **borrowed** from a school with walls, and the inverted commas are the writer admitting the loan. The half hour before classes is real; the hall is not. The end of the day is real; the journey home is not.

**3. Count the hours.** From **0900** to **1400** is **five hours**, and that is five hours in the same chair, in front of the same screen. Hold on to that number: later in the lesson a voice on the report gives it back to you, and it is the one thing the report says **against** these schools.

> 🗡️ Count the classes as well. Classes start at 0930; **Lesson 2** begins at 1100; the **last class** begins at 1300. Three classes, then — and the grid never says the word *three*. That is what reading a document means: the answer is in it, but not written in it.

## 🗝️ Five words to carry away from the lesson

| Word           | What it means                                            | Seen in action                                                |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **bullying**   | frightening or hurting somebody weaker than you            | _He changed schools to escape the bullying._                    |
| **to log on**  | to connect a computer to a system                          | _She logs on at nine and the school day begins._                |
| **appeal**     | what makes something attractive                            | _The appeal of the place is that nobody stares at you._         |
| **texting**    | writing on the computer                                    | _The lesson goes on in silence: the whole class is texting._    |
| **bustle**     | a crowd of people moving about in a busy way               | _At break the corridor is all noise and bustle._                |

Two of them need a second look.

- **appeal** is a **noun of attractiveness**. It names the thing about a place, an idea or an offer that draws people towards it. Ask *what is the appeal of X?* and you are asking **what makes people want it** — never what X is asking of you.
- **bustle** carries **no blame at all**. A crowd that bustles is simply a crowd in movement: feet, voices, bags, everybody going somewhere at once. That is exactly why it is worth putting beside **bullying**, which is the opposite kind of word: bullying is one person deliberately frightening another. A corridor can be full of bustle without a single bully in it — and this lesson will show you a pupil who fled the first as much as the second.

Around those five stand the other words this report needs: **to enrol** (to put your name on the roll of a school), **bustling** (the adjective, for a place full of that movement), **panic attacks**, **side-effects**, and **drawbacks**.

> ⚠️ **Watch out for the definition that fits nobody.** Your book prints five words and **six** definitions, and one of the six belongs to none of them: *testing*. That is not a mistake in the book; it is a deliberate trap, and it is the reason you must never match by elimination alone. Match the four you are sure of, and then **check that the fifth definition truly fits the fifth word** — because one of the definitions still on the table is there for the sole purpose of being taken by a pupil in a hurry.

## 📝 Enrolling and logging on — once, and every morning

These two verbs are easy to confuse and impossible to swap, because they do not happen the same number of times.

| Verb            | How often                                    | What it does                                            |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **to enrol**    | **once**, at the start                       | puts your name on the roll: you become a student of the school |
| **to log on**   | **every morning**, and several times a day   | connects your machine so that today's lesson can reach you |

You **enrol** in September and you are a student of that college until you leave it, whether you switch your computer on today or not. You **log on** at nine, and at ten past nine you are no longer logged on if the connection drops. One is a **decision**; the other is a **gesture**. The report keeps them apart, and so must you: *twenty-two students have enrolled* and *twenty-two students logged on this morning* are two different counts, and the second is never larger than the first.

## 👦 👧 Two young people, and what they were running from

Part two of the report follows two of the twenty-two. Your book asks you for three columns — **name**, **age**, and **reason for enrolling** — and here is what the voices give you:

| Name        | Age | Why he or she enrolled                                     |
| ----------- | --- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Zack**    | 12  | he was suffering **panic attacks**, and he was being **bullied** |
| **Natalie** | 13  | she was **afraid of speaking in class**, and found her teacher **strict** |

Now look at those two reasons **together**, because that is the question the report is really putting to you.

Neither of them says a word about the lessons. Nobody here found mathematics too hard, nobody failed an examination, nobody complained of the books. What drove these two out of school was **everything that surrounds a lesson**: other children in a corridor, a knot of fear before speaking, the eye of a teacher who frightens. A **panic attack** is a sudden rush of fear that takes hold of the body — the heart races, breathing goes short — and it has nothing to do with knowing the answer.

That is the argument the report leaves in your hands, without ever stating it: a virtual school does not teach anything a school with walls does not teach. **It removes what was happening around the teaching.**

## ⚖️ Benefits and drawbacks

Weigh what the report actually gives you. On one side, what these pupils gain:

- they **feel better**, and the report insists on it;
- **two things stop happening to them**: they no longer suffer from **bullying**, and they are no longer in the middle of the **bustling** crowd of a corridor;
- they are **more confident** — and confidence is exactly what Natalie had none of;
- and the machinery is light: **all they have to do is log on and start texting**.

On the other side, the report states one **drawback** aloud, in a single sentence, and it is the sentence your grammar lesson is built on: **sitting at a computer for five hours does have side-effects.**

**Side-effects** are the unwanted effects that come with something useful — the aching back, the tired eyes, the day spent without moving. Notice how honest the number is: five hours is not an accusation invented by an enemy of these schools, it is what their **own timetable prints**, from nine to two.

> 🗡️ **Benefit or drawback?** Test it with one question: *is this something the pupil gains, or something the pupil pays?* Escaping bullying is a gain. Not having to speak in front of a class is a gain. Five hours in a chair is a **price** — and a page that lists only gains is not a report, it is an advertisement.

## 🗣️ How to disagree without slamming the door

Now you take sides. One of you is **in favour** of virtual schools, the other is **not**, and you have to talk to each other. English gives you five ready-made openings, and they are not interchangeable — each one does a different job.

| Opening                                        | What it does                                                |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _I think…_                                      | opens an opinion. It grants nothing and refuses nothing.     |
| _I'm afraid I don't agree…_                     | **refuses flatly**, politely. Nothing of yours is kept.      |
| _I see what you mean, but…_                     | **grants**, then turns.                                       |
| _You are making a good point, however…_         | **grants**, then turns.                                       |
| _What you say is … but I still strongly believe…_ | **grants**, then turns — and adds how firmly.               |

Three of the five are built on the same **hinge**: they hand your opponent something before they take the argument back. *I see what you mean* costs you nothing and buys you a hearing; the **but** or the **however** that follows is where your own argument begins. Only one of the five refuses without granting anything at all — the polite *I'm afraid* softens the refusal, but it concedes no point.

> ⚠️ The hinge is not a decoration. In a debate, the pupil who says *I see what you mean, but the pupil is alone all day* has answered the argument; the pupil who says *I don't agree, virtual schools are bad* has only repeated that he disagrees. **Grant the true half, then attack the rest.**

## ‼️ The emphatic form: do, does, did

Here is the grammar of the lesson, and you have already met it without noticing. Listen again to the sentence the report gives:

> *Sitting at a computer for five hours **does** have side-effects.*

Take that **does** out. *Sitting at a computer for five hours has side-effects.* The sentence is still perfect English, and it still means the same thing. So the word was not needed to build the sentence — **it was put there on purpose**, and the speaker's voice goes up on it when he says it aloud.

**The rule, in one line:**

> **do / does / did + the infinitive without _to_ → to emphasize the meaning**

The MY GRAMMAR box of your book prints one example and you should learn it by heart:

> **He does work hard.**

**1. The auxiliary carries everything; the verb goes bare.** Look at the example again: it is *does work*, not ~~*does works*~~. Once **does** is there, it is the one holding the third person, so the verb behind it drops its ending and stands in its plain form — the infinitive **without _to_**. The same for the past: it is *did enrol*, never ~~*did enrolled*~~.

| Plain sentence                | The same sentence, insisting          |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| He works hard.                | He **does work** hard.                 |
| The students feel better.     | The students **do feel** better.       |
| Mary enrolled last summer.    | Mary **did enrol** last summer.        |

**2. Choose the auxiliary as you would choose the verb's own ending.** *Do* for *I, you, we, they*; *does* for *he, she, it* and for any single person or thing; *did* for anything in the past, whoever the subject is. And when **did** arrives, the past leaves the verb entirely and lives in the auxiliary — that is why *enrolled* becomes *enrol* again.

**3. What it adds is not size, and not frequency.** This is where marks are lost. *He does work hard* does **not** mean that he works harder than others, nor that he works often, nor that he works longer. It means: **it is true that he works hard** — usually said to somebody who has just doubted it, or who has just said the opposite.

> 🗡️ The emphatic form almost always answers something. Somebody has said *he never works*, or *nothing happens in those schools*, or *she never went near a virtual school* — and the sentence with **do** in it is the answer. When you write one, make sure your reader can feel the doubt it is replying to.

**4. Say it, do not only write it.** On the page the word is simply there; in the mouth, the voice **rises on the auxiliary** and presses it. That is what your book asks you to notice about the speaker in the report: he raises the pitch of his voice on **does**, and everything after it is carried by that lift.

## 🔇 Letters English writes and no longer says

English keeps letters in its spelling long after it has stopped pronouncing them. Read these ten words aloud and you will hear the same thing about every one of them: **each contains at least one silent letter.**

| Word            | The letter you do not hear |
| --------------- | --------------------------- |
| **right**       | the **gh**                  |
| **half**        | the **l**                   |
| **taught**      | the **gh**                  |
| **friend**      | the **i**                   |
| **bustle**      | the **t**                   |
| **psychology**  | the **p**                   |
| **knowledge**   | the **k**                   |
| **comb**        | the **b**                   |
| **knee**        | the **k**                   |
| **through**     | the **gh**                  |

Some of them travel in families: **kn-** at the beginning of a word has lost its **k** (*knee*, *knowledge*, *know*, *knife*), and **-gh-** is silent in a whole tribe of words (*right*, *taught*, *through*, *bought*, *daughter*). Others are alone with their oddity: the **t** buried in *bustle*, the **l** in *half*, the **i** in *friend*.

**And now the game your book plays with them.** Take these five words, in this order, and pull out the silent letter of each:

| Word          | Silent letter |
| ------------- | -------------- |
| **plumber**   | **b**          |
| **biscuits**  | **u**          |
| **harmful**   | **r**          |
| **autumn**    | **n**          |
| **listen**    | **t**          |

Read the column downwards: **b – u – r – n – t**. The five letters nobody pronounces spell **burnt**, the past form of *burn*. It is a small piece of theatre, and it is also the best possible proof that you have identified the right letter in every word: get one wrong and the word does not come out.

> ⚠️ A silent letter is **written and not sounded** — that is all. It is not a letter you may leave out when you write, and it is not a spelling mistake somebody forgot to correct. *Autumn* keeps its **n** because the word had it before English stopped saying it, and a pupil who writes "autum" has not been clever, only wrong.

## 📣 Emphasizing a word in a sentence

The last thing your voice can do is choose **which word of a sentence matters**. The words stay in the same order, the sentence keeps its meaning — and yet each saying of it answers a different question.

Take one sentence and say it five times, pressing on one word each time:

> _I read a tale last week._

| Press on…       | and you have answered…            |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **I**            | Who read a tale last week?          |
| **read**         | What did you do last week?          |
| **a tale**       | What did you read?                  |
| **last week**    | When did you read a tale?           |

Nothing has been added and nothing has been taken away. The **stress alone** has told your listener which part of the sentence is the news, and the rest is treated as already known.

**Now do it with the sentence of this lesson.**

> _Mary enrolled in a virtual school last summer._

| Question                              | Press on…              |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **Who** enrolled in a virtual school? | **Mary**                |
| **What** did Mary do last summer?     | **enrolled**            |
| **Where** did Mary enrol?             | **in a virtual school** |
| **When** did Mary enrol?              | **last summer**         |

> 🗡️ The machine runs in both directions, and that is what you will be tested on. Given the question, you must know which word to press. Given the pressed word, you must know which question has just been asked — because the stressed word **is** the answer, and everything else in the sentence was in the question already.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                             | Right                                              | Why                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "He does works hard"                              | _He **does work** hard_                            | the auxiliary carries the person; the verb goes bare                 |
| "Mary did enrolled last summer"                   | _Mary **did enrol** last summer_                   | once *did* is there, the past has left the verb                      |
| "he does work hard" = he works very hard          | _= it is **true** that he works hard_              | the emphatic form insists on truth, not on quantity                  |
| "she logs on every September"                     | _she **enrolled** in September, and **logs on** every morning_ | enrolling happens once; logging on happens daily            |
| "the appeal of the school" = what it asks of you  | _= what makes it **attractive**_                   | in this lesson the word names attractiveness                         |
| "a bustling corridor is a corridor full of bullies" | _= a corridor full of **movement**_               | *bustle* describes a busy crowd and blames nobody                    |
| "the report says the school has 22 students, so 22 have enrolled" | _22 is the figure **now**_         | a heard figure always carries the moment it was counted              |
| "I don't agree" answers the argument              | _"I see what you mean, but…" answers the argument_ | granting the true half is what buys you the right to attack the rest |
| "autum", "plumer"                                 | _**autumn**, **plumber**_                          | a silent letter is not pronounced, but it is always written          |

> 🏆 You can now read a timetable that has no building behind it, weigh a benefit against a price, disagree with somebody without losing them, and make one word of a sentence carry the whole of your meaning — on the page with **do**, in the mouth with your voice.
