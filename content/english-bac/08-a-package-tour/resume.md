# 📜 Summary: A Package Tour

- **The words of a journey, in four families** — **Accommodation**: _full board_ (every meal included; _half board_ = breakfast and one other), _single room_, _camp site_, _to check in_ (report your arrival and be given your room — or, at an airport, your boarding card; the opposite is _to check out_). **Means of transport**: _coach_ (a long-distance bus), _jet_, _passenger_, _return ticket_ (there **and** back; a _single_ only takes you there). **Tourism**: _package tour_ (travel, accommodation and often meals at **one** price), _journey_, _travel agent_. **Sightseeing**: _monument_, _temple_, _palace_, _statue_. And keep the four apart: a **journey** is the going itself, a **trip** is the whole short outing, a **tour** runs through several places in a planned order, and **travel** is the general idea — uncountable, never "a travel".
- **An ad exists to persuade** — four moves: it speaks to **you**; it names what you **get** (the benefit) and not what the thing **is** (the feature); it piles the benefits into a list of _free_ and _included_; and it ends with an **instruction** — _Get your pass now_ — the one line aimed straight at the reader, a verb with nothing in front of it, a bare **imperative**. Same fact, two sentences: _The pass is valid for ten days_ informs, _Ten days, one card — you walk in wherever you like_ persuades. Nothing is invented; what changes is **who the sentence is about**.
- **Evaluating an ad — four questions** — (1) **What exactly do I get?** read the verbs: _includes_, _covers_, _gives you free admission to_. (2) **What is NOT included?** hunt for the asterisk and its words: _not included_, _extra_, _a separate ticket is required_, _supplement_, _on request_ — **the asterisk is the page's own footnote and always points upwards, at a promise, to take something back from it**. (3) **What must I still do?** book, pay a deposit, show a card, come before a date. (4) **What has it not told me at all?** the price, the dates, the size of the group — **what is missing is information too**. And remember that **a promise is not information**: "a welcome you will not find anywhere else" contains nothing you could check.
- **The words an ad makes its promises with** — **admission** = the right to enter (_free admission_ = you go in without paying) · **discount** = a **reduced** price, never nothing · **contest** = a competition you enter hoping to win · **exhibition** = works shown to the public · **souvenir** = something kept to remember a place · **several** = more than two but not very many · **concessions** = reduced prices for students, children, the retired · **benefits** = the advantages the offer brings you · **artisans** = craftsmen working by hand. Three traps: **free** is about **money** only (a free event can still be fully booked), a **discount** lowers a price without removing it, and **several** is not a figure you may write in a table.
- **Judging a package tour** — for it: one price paid once, everything arranged, a guide, and it is cheaper than the same journey alone. Against it: the programme is **fixed**, you see what the agency chose, you move at the speed of the group, and the country arrives through a window. Say it with _In my opinion… · I find it… · It's worth it because… · It's not for us: it doesn't include… · I'd rather…_ — and when you are judging **an ad**, take the reason **out of the ad**: "the concerts we came for are not included" evaluates the offer, "I don't like groups" only describes you.
- **Expressing interest — five frames, and the slot each one needs** — _X_ **sounds interesting** puts the thing **in front** (_Your group pass sounds interesting_); the four others put it **behind**: **I'm interested in** _X_, **I'd like to know more about** _X_, **Please tell me about** _X_, **I'm keen on** _X_ (a noun or an **-ing** form after _in_ and _on_). **interested** describes the person, **interesting** describes the thing — "I am interesting in…" ✗. And only two of the five actually **ask** something (_know more about_, _tell me about_); the three others merely state where you stand. **Interest is not an order**: saying you are interested commits you to nothing, while _we'll **take** twenty passes_, _we'd like to **book** twenty places_ and _please **put** twenty aside for us_ all commit you, and cost money.
- **The e-mail** — **To:** one address exactly as printed; **Subject:** what the message is about, in four to six words (_Group pass — request for information_ ✓, "Travel" ✗, and your whole first sentence ✗). Then four moves: (1) **where you saw the offer**, (2) **your interest**, in one line, (3) **your questions**, one per line and precise, (4) **the polite close** — _I look forward to hearing from you_ + _Yours faithfully,_ + your name. _Dear Sir or Madam_ goes with _Yours faithfully_; _Dear Mr Ben Salah_ with _Yours sincerely_. **Never ask what the ad has already answered** — cross out on the page every question it settles, and what is left is your e-mail. And hold the message together with its rails: _It promises… · Neither of these two promises… · as well · so_.
- **[æ] and [ə]** — **[æ]** is the wide-open vowel of _cat_ and appears **only in a stressed syllable**; **[ə]** (**schwa**) is the weak, colourless vowel of _about_, the commonest sound in English, and appears **only where there is no stress**. So: **find the stress first, and the vowel follows**. This lesson: **[æ]** in _**pack**age_, _**acc**ess_, _**cap**ital_ (first a), and in _chance_ said the American way ([tʃæns] — British English says [tʃɑːns]); **[ə]** in _festi**val**_, _**a**nnounced_, and the adjective _sepa**rate**_. **One word holds both**: _capital_ [ˈkæpɪtəl], stressed [æ] then unstressed [ə]. **One word changes with its job**: the **verb** _separate_ has three syllables [ˈsepəreɪt], the **adjective** two [ˈseprət].
- **Project Work 1 — My Festival** — Step 1: search the Web and watch **how** the information is presented, not only what it says. Step 2: choose a festival you know well and describe it so that web users want to come, with pictures of what makes it **different**. Step 3: invite your classmates to the school's Internet room and be ready to explain **how you worked**. A page that works gives the facts a reader needs (what, when, where, who may come, what it costs and who pays less) **and** the one thing that belongs to your festival and to no other — while a photograph of any cheerful crowd proves nothing, and the region's other festivals only hand your reader the door out. Describing attractively is **persuading with the truth**: you invent nothing, you choose what to show.
