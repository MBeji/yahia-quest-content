# ⚔️ First Impressions — Opening Your Toolbox for the Year

> 💡 "Before a craftsman cuts anything, he lays out his tools and learns their names."

This unit is not a lesson. It is the moment you open the box. There is no story to read, no tense to conjugate — only the instruments you will reach for from now until the exam: the books that answer your questions, the words that name what you are looking at, the partnerships that make English sound English, and the traps that spelling sets for your ear.

## 📚 Four books, four different questions

Students often say "I looked it up in the dictionary" whatever the question was. But a question has a shape, and each shape has its own book.

| You want…                                   | You open…                              | Because it gives…                              |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| the **meaning** of a word                   | a **dictionary**                       | definitions, in English or in another language |
| the **opposite** of a word                  | a **dictionary of synonyms and antonyms** | similar words, then their antonyms          |
| the **best** of twenty possible words       | a **thesaurus**                        | an exhaustive list of equivalents, by sense    |
| facts about a **subject**                   | an **encyclopedia**                    | articles on many subjects, in alphabetical order |

A **thesaurus** is not a fancy dictionary. Its own definition says it: _a collection of words arranged in lists or groups according to sense_. Look up **tale** in a dictionary and you get one line; look it up in a thesaurus and you get _account, fable, legend, narration, novel, parable, recital, story, yarn_ — and now you can choose.

A **dictionary of synonyms and antonyms** does something else again: it prints the opposites underneath. Under **talented** you read _able, gifted, clever, brilliant, adept_, and then, on its own line, _antonyms: clumsy, inept_.

> 🗡️ Other learning aids have their own jobs: a **grammar book** explains structures, a **workbook** gives you practice, a **pronunciation dictionary** shows how a word sounds, a **monolingual dictionary** stays in English while a **bilingual** one crosses into Arabic or French, and a **vocabulary list** simply remembers for you.

## 🏷️ Naming what you are looking at

Half of learning English is being able to say what kind of thing is in front of you. These are the labels you will use all year.

| Label                  | What it means                                                              |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **formal English**     | the English of essays, lectures, reports and official letters               |
| **colloquial English** | the English of everyday conversations — relaxed, spoken                     |
| **synonym**            | a word with a **similar** meaning                                          |
| **antonym**            | a word with the **opposite** meaning                                       |
| **collocation**        | two or more words that regularly **go together**                           |
| **phrasal verb**       | a verb **combined** with an adverb or a preposition, giving a new meaning   |
| **idiom**              | a group of words meaning something **different** from the individual words  |
| **proofreading**       | **revising** a piece of writing before you hand it in                      |
| **encyclopedia**       | a set of books giving information about many subjects, in alphabetical order |

> ⚠️ Do not confuse the last three of the middle block. _make a mistake_ is a **collocation** (two ordinary words that simply belong together), _give up_ is a **phrasal verb** (a verb plus a particle), and _give somebody a hand_ is an **idiom** (the meaning is nowhere in the words).

## 🔗 Collocations — the partnerships English has already chosen

Some words are friends and some are not, and no rule explains why.

- _Time **flies** / **goes by** / **wears on** / **passes**._ ✓ — these collocate.
- _Time speeds / travels / rides / moves._ ✗ — these do not; every word is correct, the combination is not.

The same happens everywhere: you can speak of an **academic year** but not a "studying year"; a discussion can be **productive** or **fruitful** but never "prolific".

**Adjectives choose their nouns.**

| Adjective | Partners it takes                |
| --------- | -------------------------------- |
| **dark**  | dark sky · dark colour · dark hair |
| **light** | light blue · light meal · light clothes |
| **new**   | a new style · a new situation · a new look |
| **first** | first aid · the first time · a first impression |

**Verbs choose their nouns too**, and _make_ and _do_ are the pair that trips everybody up.

| Verb     | Partners it takes                                                   |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **make** | make your bed · make a cake · make a phone call · make a noise · make a decision |
| **do**   | do your homework · do your room · do the washing-up · do an exercise · do your best |
| **cost** | cost a fortune                                                       |
| **last** | last week · last year · last time                                    |
| **go**   | go fast · go home                                                    |

The list never stops growing: you also **make an effort**, **make an appointment**, **do the shopping** and **do your best**.

> ⚠️ "Do a mistake" ✗ → _make a mistake_ ✓ · "Make your homework" ✗ → _do your homework_ ✓. Nothing in the meaning of _make_ and _do_ predicts this. Learn each pair as one block.

**The odd-one-out test.** Take a word, list its usual partners, and one intruder will stand out: **GROW** takes _coffee, plants, old_ — but not "grow experience" (you **gain** experience). **NEW** takes _style, situation, look_ — but not "a new someone" (you say _somebody new_).

## ⚡ Phrasal verbs — when the particle rules

A verb can combine with an adverb or a preposition to become a **phrasal verb**. Sometimes it stays literal (_we sat **down**_), very often it does not.

| Phrasal verb  | Means         | Example                                     |
| ------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **come about** | happen       | _How did this come about?_                  |
| **make up**   | invent        | _Mary made up the whole story._             |
| **give up**   | stop          | _I have given up smoking._                  |
| **call off**  | cancel        | _They called the deal off._                 |

Now watch one single verb change meaning four times, purely through its particle.

| With **put** …    | Means                              | Example                                    |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **put off**       | postpone, delay, reschedule        | _Never put off till tomorrow what you can do today._ |
| **put aside**     | save; lay down; or leave behind    | _Put aside money for a rainy day._         |
| **put away**      | save; put back in its place        | _Put the books away, please._              |
| **put forward**   | suggest, propose, submit           | _She put forward a bold theory._           |

> 🗡️ **The words that follow decide the meaning.** _Put aside money_ is to save it, _put aside the newspaper_ is to lay it down for a moment, and _put aside your fears_ is to forget them, to disregard them. Read the whole sentence before you choose a meaning — that is why a dictionary of synonyms lists a phrasal verb example by example.

> ⚠️ A phrasal verb is often separable: _They called **off** the deal._ ✓ = _They called the deal **off**._ ✓

## 🎭 Idiomatic expressions — when the words tell you nothing

Some phrases cannot be understood literally. Even if you know every single word, the meaning of the whole may still be a mystery. Those are **idiomatic expressions**, and the only way in is to meet them.

| Idiom                                  | Really means                                    |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **to burn the candle at both ends**    | to work very hard and stay up very late          |
| **to burn the midnight oil**           | the same thing — the two are equivalent          |
| **so far, so good**                    | nothing has gone wrong up to this point          |
| **you could hear a pin drop**          | the place is so quiet the tiniest sound would carry |
| **to give somebody a hand**            | to help, to assist somebody                      |
| **clean enough to eat off the floor**  | extremely, spotlessly clean                      |
| **to follow in somebody's footsteps**  | to do the same job as they did                   |
| **the life and soul of the party**     | the one who makes a gathering fun for everybody  |
| **to make it to the top**              | to reach the highest position, to succeed        |
| **a meeting of minds**                 | a close understanding between people             |
| **all of a sudden**                    | quickly and unexpectedly                         |

Arabic works exactly the same way, with images of its own — and that is a good reason to collect the pairs rather than translate word by word.

> ⚠️ _Give me a hand_ = help me. _Give somebody a big hand_ = applaud them. One small word, two different idioms.

## 🌍 One language, several Englishes

English is a mother tongue in **Great Britain, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Canada and the United States**, and an official language in many more. Three things separate the varieties.

**1. The accent** — and inside one single country there are **regional accents** too.

**2. The spelling.** Two regular changes cover most of what you will meet:

| British   | American  | Rule                              |
| --------- | --------- | --------------------------------- |
| colour, humour, honour, neighbour | color, humor, honor, neighbor | **-our → -or** |
| centre, theatre, metre, kilometre | center, theater, meter, kilometer | **-re → -er** |

**3. The vocabulary.** Same object, two names.

| British       | American     | British   | American   |
| ------------- | ------------ | --------- | ---------- |
| the ground floor | the first floor | luggage | baggage    |
| autumn        | fall         | pavement  | sidewalk   |
| queue         | line         | holidays  | vacation   |
| taxi          | cab          | rubber    | eraser     |
| flat          | apartment    | sweets    | candy      |
| rubbish       | garbage      | bill      | check      |
| lorry         | truck        | lift      | elevator   |
| biscuits      | cookies      | petrol    | gas        |
| a tin         | a can        | W.C.      | restroom   |

> 🗡️ Neither column is more correct than the other. The only rule is **consistency**: choose one variety and stay inside it for the whole text.

## 🔊 The ear against the eye

**The four faces of _ch_.** One spelling, four sounds — the letters alone will never tell you.

| Sound   | Words                                          |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **/k/** | psychology · stomach · chorus · chemistry · ache |
| **/ʃ/** | machine · moustache                            |
| **/tʃ/** | achievement · chores                          |
| **/dʒ/** | spinach                                       |

**Homophones, homographs, homonyms.** Three labels for three different accidents.

- **Homophones** — same **sound**, different spelling, different meaning: _one / won_ · _sea / see_ · _air / heir_ · _here / hear_ · _aren't / aunt_ · _way / weigh_ · _flour / flower_ · _I'll / isle_ · _higher / hire_ · _I / eye_ · _some / sum_ · _new / knew_.
- **Homographs** — same **spelling**, different meaning, and often a different pronunciation: _to **lead** a team_ against _a pipe made of **lead**_.
- **Homonyms** — same spelling **and** same sound, different meanings: the **bank** of a river against the **bank** where you keep your money.

> ⚠️ In a homophone pair only the **context** can tell you which word to write: _flour_ goes into a cake, a _flower_ grows in a garden. Your spellchecker will never catch this mistake, because both words exist.

## 📄 Reading a text before reading its subject

Long before you understand a document, you can name it. Every **text type** leaves signals, and each type belongs to a **field**.

| Signals you see                                          | Text type              | Field                  |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| an amount paid, an exchange or refund policy             | a **receipt**          | shopping, customers' rights |
| author, date, title, then place and publisher            | a **bibliography**     | books, publications    |
| praise, a slogan, an appeal to your senses               | an **advertisement**   | the product sold       |
| warnings, an expiry date, "consult your doctor"          | **instructions for use** | medicine             |
| numbered steps, a validity period                        | **instructions**       | services              |
| what the item is made of and what it does                | a **description of an item** | hygiene, technology |
| a list of substances with figures and units              | a **chemical analysis** | components            |
| what is good or bad for your health                      | **medical advice**     | diets                  |

A **bibliography** entry is worth reading closely, because its order never changes:

> **Last name, Initial of the first name. Date of publication. Title of the book or article, in italics. Place: Publisher.**

So in _Bouzid, S. 2014. Reading Skills for Secondary Classes. Tunis: Nour Publishing._, the four-figure number is always the **date of publication** — never a number of pages, never a price.

## 🔢 Reading the big figures

Each **space** — or each comma — separates a group of three digits.

- `200 000` = **two hundred thousand** = 200,000
- `200 000 000` = **two hundred million** = 200,000,000

> ⚠️ After a number, **million**, **billion** and **trillion** take **no -s** and are followed by a **plural noun**: _two hundred million **cups**_, _a billion **people**_. Without a number in front, the -s comes back: _millions of cups_.

## 🗣️ Saying it the right way, in the right register

_Cuppa_ is colloquial English for _a cup of tea_ — perfect between neighbours, impossible in an essay. And the same object can be talked about in three different ways.

| Sentence                        | What it expresses |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| _Shall we have a cuppa?_        | a **suggestion**  |
| _Would you like a cuppa?_       | a **suggestion**  |
| _I'm dying for a cuppa._        | a **desire**      |
| _Make us a cuppa, will you?_    | a **request**     |

A **suggestion** puts an idea to the other person, a **desire** says what you want without asking anyone to act, and a **request** asks somebody to do something.

## 🧭 Cultures and languages

The prefix carries the whole meaning, so reading the prefix is usually enough to read the word.

| Prefix     | Means   | On a language                | On a culture      |
| ---------- | ------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------- |
| **mono-**  | one     | **monolingual** — one language | —               |
| **bi-**    | two     | **bilingual** — two languages | **bicultural**   |
| **multi-** | many    | **multilingual** — several languages | **multicultural** |

Two more from the same family: **ethnic diversity** — people of many different races and nationalities; **lifestyles** — the way people live.

## ⏱️ One last tool: your reading rate

Time yourself on a short passage and divide by the minutes: that is your **reading rate**, in **words per minute**. At this level you should be reading at least **200 words per minute**. Slow reading is not a character trait; it is a habit, and it moves.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                     | Right                          | Why                                                  |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| "do a mistake"            | _make a mistake_               | the partnership is fixed by usage, not by logic      |
| "two hundred millions cups" | _two hundred million cups_    | after a number, **million** takes no -s              |
| "I bought a flower to bake a cake" | _I bought some flour_ | homophones sound alike; only the context spells them |
| "the theatre in Chicago" (in an American text) | _the theater_ | one variety per text, from start to finish |
