# ⚔️ Online Learning — What Three Learners Said, and the Comma That Explains

> 💡 "Nobody makes you get on a bus. Nobody makes you sit down either. This lesson is about the second half of that sentence."

The unit has shown you a school with no building. This lesson hands the argument back to the people who are actually inside one: **three learners who have followed a course through a screen, and who do not entirely agree about what it cost them.**

Two skills meet here, and the lesson runs from one straight into the other. First you **read quotations** — somebody's words, printed under somebody's name. Then you **write**: a letter to a man you have never met, telling him where you agree with him and where you do not, and saying why.

Between the reading and the writing stands one small mark of punctuation, the **comma**, with exactly two jobs to do. You will need both of them before the letter is finished.

> 🗡️ What you are learning to do, in the words the book uses for it: **read quotations**, **express an opinion**, **use commas appropriately**, **write a formal letter**, and **support your ideas with arguments**.

## 🗣️ Reading a quotation — somebody said this

A **quotation** is a piece of writing handed to you with its owner attached. That is the whole difference between a quotation and an ordinary sentence in a textbook, and it changes how you read it.

Under the three remarks this lesson opens with, one line names where they came from: they were written by **e-learners** — people following courses online — and printed in a magazine for learners of English, **ESL**, in **June 2003**.

That line does three things, and only three.

| It tells you | It does **not** tell you |
| ------------ | ----------------------- |
| **who** is speaking — learners, not teachers, not researchers | that what they say is true |
| **where** the words were printed | that the magazine agrees with them |
| **when** they were said | that the three of them agree with one another |

**A quotation is evidence of an opinion, never proof of a fact.** Quote an e-learner and you have proved that an e-learner believed something; you have not proved the thing itself. That is not a reason to distrust the three remarks — it is the reason the book asks you, after each one, whether **you** agree. A sentence you may argue with is worth more to you than a sentence you may only copy.

## ⏳ Three learners, and where they part company

Here is what the three of them say, in ordinary words.

| The remark | What it claims |
| ---------- | -------------- |
| **The first** | an online course asks for the same amount of the learner's time as a course in a classroom — *if not more* |
| **The second** | some students expect e-learning to be **easier**, because there is no journey to make |
| **The third** | online learning, like every other kind of learning, **demands a commitment of time**; and a learner who has not got enough of it can make more |

Read them side by side and the disagreement is exact. Two of the three say the time is **not** saved. The remaining one reports that some learners expect it to be.

### The mistake the second remark reports

It is worth taking apart, because it is the mistake this whole page exists to correct.

- **The fact**: a learner at home does not travel. If the bus took forty minutes each way, eighty minutes of his day have come free. That is real, and nobody disputes it.
- **The conclusion drawn from it**: therefore the course is easier.

The conclusion does not follow, and you can see why the moment you ask **what the eighty minutes were being spent on**. They were spent *getting to* the course. They were never spent *on* the course. Take them away and the journey has gone; the lessons, the reading and the work have not moved at all.

> ⚠️ **A saving outside a thing tells you nothing about the thing.** The learner has a longer evening. The course is exactly the course it was.

### Reading a hedge

The first remark ends on three small words — *if not more* — and they are not decoration. They tell you which way the writer would lean if he had to. He is saying: **as much time, and quite possibly more; certainly not less.**

Learn to see these hedges, because a quotation is short and every word in it is carrying weight. Drop those three words and you are left with a claim of exact equality that the writer never made.

### A commitment of time

The third remark uses the phrase you should carry away from this page: a **time commitment**. To commit time is to promise **hours of your week to one thing, which means promising them to nothing else.** It is not money, and it is not a promise to finish; it is a block of the week, spoken for in advance.

## 🕐 Making more time

The third learner does not stop at the demand. He answers the obvious objection — *I have no time* — with a claim that sounds impossible: a learner who is short of time **can make more of it**. The one example the book prints is **sleeping less**, and the page then breaks off into dotted lines and asks you for further examples of your own.

The objection is fair: a day has twenty-four hours and nobody has ever added a twenty-fifth. So read the phrase properly.

> **To make time is not to add hours to a day. It is to take hours that are already going somewhere and send them somewhere else.**

That is why the printed example works. Sleep is not spare; it is one of the places the hours already go. Giving up an hour of it moves that hour, at a price the learner accepts.

Test any further suggestion against that idea, and most of them fall at once:

| Suggestion | Does it make time? |
| ---------- | ------------------ |
| giving up the hour spent on the telephone every evening | **yes** — an hour that was going somewhere now goes here |
| hoping the days will feel longer once the course starts | no — nothing has been moved |
| counting how many hours the whole course will need | no — that measures the problem |
| waiting for a term with less to do | no — that postpones it |

> 🗡️ A good suggestion always names **two** things: the hours, and the place they used to go. If it names only the hours, nothing has been made.

## 🌍 Flexibility — the freedom to choose when and where

The second half of the lesson brings in a different voice: **John Bears**, author of a guide to earning degrees by distance learning. His claim is about **flexibility**, and it is a strong one: distance study offers a degree of freedom no traditional programme can, since it leaves the learner free to study **whenever and wherever** he chooses.

Two words, two freedoms, and it is worth being exact about them:

| The freedom | What the learner chooses |
| ----------- | ------------------------ |
| **whenever** | the **hour** — dawn, midnight, Sunday afternoon |
| **wherever** | the **place** — a kitchen, a bus, a town with no college in it |

**Now put that claim next to the three e-learners, and look carefully, because this is the sharpest thing on the page.**

They are not contradicting each other. **Flexibility is a claim about *when* and *where* the hours are spent. The time commitment is a claim about *how many* there are.** A learner may choose every one of his hours freely and still owe the course exactly as many as a classroom would have taken.

> ⚠️ So a pupil who answers *you are wrong, an online course takes just as much time* has not countered the claim about flexibility at all. He has answered a question nobody asked. To counter a claim you must first find out what it says.

And this is where the drawback hides. The freedom is real — and a freedom is also the removal of something that used to push. Nobody keeps a distance learner to a timetable, which means that nothing but the learner himself will put him at his desk on a wet Tuesday.

| Benefits of studying at a distance | Drawbacks |
| ---------------------------------- | --------- |
| the hour is the learner's to choose | the hours the course claims must still be found somewhere |
| the place is the learner's to choose | no timetable and no room put the learner at his desk |
| the journey to a classroom disappears | the freedom that lets the work happen also lets it stop |

**benefits** and **drawbacks** are the two words the writing task asks for by name: what is good about a thing, and what is bad about it.

## ✒️ The comma, and its two jobs

Now the punctuation, and the rule is short. Your book gives the comma **two** jobs on this page, and you are answerable for both.

> **A comma is used:**
> **1.** before **and after** a phrase **in apposition**, giving additional information about the noun it follows;
> **2.** to separate items on a list.

### Job one — the phrase in apposition

An **apposition** is the addition of a phrase to another **as an explanation**. In plain terms: you have named something, and you want to say what it is without starting a new sentence. So you drop the explanation in right there, and you fence it off with a comma at each end.

> **John Bears**, *author of Bears' Guide to Earning Degrees by Distance Learning*, argues that a learner may study when he likes.

Three things to hold on to, and each of them is a mark thrown away in an examination.

**1. The phrase stands immediately after the noun it explains.** It explains *the noun it follows* — so it follows that noun and no other. Put it at the end of the sentence and it will attach itself to whatever happens to be standing there.

**2. Two commas, not one.** The rule says *before and after*. A single comma opens the fence and never closes it, and the sentence is left with a flap hanging off it:

- ~~*John Bears, the author of a guide to distance learning argues that a learner may study when he likes.*~~ ✗ — opened, never closed.
- ~~*John Bears the author of a guide to distance learning, argues that a learner may study when he likes.*~~ ✗ — closed, never opened.

**3. "Additional" means the sentence stands without it.** Take the fenced phrase out and what is left must still be a whole sentence: *John Bears argues that a learner may study when he likes.* It is. That test is how you check a doubtful comma.

> ⚠️ **But additional never means useless, and this is the trap.** The phrase can be lifted out without breaking the grammar; it cannot be lifted out without changing what has been claimed. When one of the learners writes about the hours an online course wants and fences three words inside his sentence — *like all learning* — those three words are doing the real work of it. They say that online learning is **no exception**: it is being put in the same class as every other kind of learning, not singled out. Take them away and the claim about every other kind of learning has gone with them, and the sentence no longer answers anybody who thought this kind was special.

### Job two — items on a list

The second job is the one you already use without thinking: **the comma keeps apart the things in a list.**

> She gave up the cinema, the football and the long telephone calls.

### The same mark, both jobs, one sentence

Nothing stops a sentence from needing both, and then you must read each comma for what it is doing rather than counting them all as one kind:

> **Habib**, *the caretaker of the block*, has given up **the radio, the newspaper and the long lunches**.

A pair of commas fences the explanation of who Habib is. A single comma keeps two of the three abandoned pleasures apart. Three commas, two entirely different jobs.

## 📨 The letter — arguing with a man you have never met

The writing task is the point everything else has been aimed at. You are to write to Mr Bears about his claim, say **to what extent** you agree with it, and set out the **benefits or the drawbacks** of distance learning. The page even prints the frame for you: a greeting at the top, *Dear Mr Bears,*, an empty space of about a dozen lines, and a rule at the foot on which to write your name.

Four instructions come with it, and each one is a mark.

**1. Formal English — and here is the reason for it.** Not because the subject is serious, and not because the man is famous: **because you do not know him.** That single fact settles the register. The English you would send a stranger is not the English you would send a friend, and the greeting is where a reader sees it first: a man you have never met is *Mr Bears*, not *John*.

**2. State a view, and state it clearly.** *To what extent do you agree?* is a question, and a letter that never answers it has failed however well it is written. You may agree, disagree, or agree with a part — but you must land somewhere.

**3. Justify it with convincing arguments.** This is the instruction most often ignored, because saying a thing twice feels like supporting it. It is not.

| Not an argument | Why |
| --------------- | --- |
| *I am quite sure of this, and I would say it again tomorrow.* | insistence — the same claim, louder |
| *A course from home is, in my opinion, no easier at all.* | the claim restated in other clothes |
| *Many people would agree with me.* | a head count, not a reason |
| **Real argument**: *the lessons are the same lessons and the hours they claim are the same hours; only the journey has gone* | it gives the reader something he can check |

**4. Be tactful when you counter a point you have granted.** The instruction is exact: *be tactful if you counter his valid point of view.* **Tactful** means choosing your words so as not to wound the person you are disagreeing with — and note what it does **not** mean. It does not mean giving up the disagreement, and it does not mean saying nothing and changing the subject.

The move to learn is in two beats: **grant the point, then set something beside it — without taking the grant back.**

- ✓ *That freedom is real, and it is precisely what makes the hours so easy to let slip.*
- ✗ *That freedom is not real, since the course still has to be finished by a certain date.* — the grant has been withdrawn; you are now arguing about whether he was right at all.
- ✗ *That freedom is real, and I have nothing to set against it.* — nothing has been countered.
- ✗ *Anybody who thinks otherwise has never opened a book.* — a view has been countered and a reader has been insulted.

## 🗝️ The words of this lesson

| Word | What it means |
| ---- | ------------- |
| **an on-line learner** / **an e-learner** | somebody following a course delivered through a screen |
| **a cyber student** | the same person, named for the way he studies rather than the place |
| **e-learning** | learning carried by electronic means |
| **distance learning** | studying in which the learner and the school are not in the same place |
| **a time commitment** | hours of the week promised to one thing, and therefore to nothing else |
| **flexibility** | the freedom to choose when and where the studying happens |
| **benefits / drawbacks** | what is good about a thing / what is bad about it |
| **tactful** | careful not to wound the person you are disagreeing with |
| **an apposition** | a phrase added to another as an explanation of it |
| **a comma** | the mark that fences an apposition at both ends, and keeps items of a list apart |

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| ----- | ----- | --- |
| "no journey, so the course is easier" | *the journey has gone; the course has not moved* | the minutes saved were never spent on the course |
| "make more time" = find a twenty-fifth hour | *move hours that were already going somewhere* | a day cannot be lengthened, only re-spent |
| "flexibility means it takes less time" | *flexibility is about when and where, not how many* | the two claims answer different questions |
| "Mr Bears, the author of a guide says…" | *Mr Bears, the author of a guide, says…* | the fence needs a post at **each** end |
| "additional, so I can delete it" | *the grammar survives; the claim does not* | an apposition is removable, not idle |
| "an e-learner said it, so it is a fact" | *an e-learner said it, so somebody believed it* | a quotation proves an opinion, nothing more |
| "I am certain of it" offered as an argument | *give the reader something he can check* | insisting is not justifying |
| "you are wrong" to a man you have never met | *grant the point, then set something beside it* | tactful is countering without wounding |

> 🏆 You can now read a quotation for what it claims and what it only hedges, tell a freedom of hours from a quantity of hours, fence an explanation with a comma at each end, and disagree with a stranger in writing without either giving in or giving offence.
