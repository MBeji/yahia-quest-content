# ⚔️ Education for All — Reading the Figures Behind an Empty Desk

> 💡 "One hundred and fifteen million children were sitting in no classroom at all that year. This lesson teaches you to read that number — and to read the page that asks you to change it."

The last lesson handed you the words of the school. This one puts them to work on the question the whole unit turns on: **who is left outside the gate, and who is trying to bring them in?**

Three kinds of text stand in your way, and none of them is read like an ordinary paragraph:

- a **table of initials**, where whole institutions hide behind four letters;
- a **graph**, where the argument is in the shape of a circle and not in a sentence;
- an **advertisement**, built round a sum, that wants something from you before you reach the bottom of the page.

And one small pair of words holds the argument together: **if** and **unless**. By the end of the lesson you must be able to say the same warning twice, once with each — and to hear it at once when somebody says it wrong.

> 🗡️ What you are learning to do, in the words your book uses: **interpret a graph**, **read an ad**, and **guess the meaning of a word** you have never met before.

## 🔤 Acronyms — a name folded up

An **acronym** is a name folded up. Take the first letter of each important word of a title, print those letters together, and the title has become a short label you can put on a lorry, a letterhead or a headline.

> *World Health Organization* → **WHO**

The machine runs in reverse as well, and that is what makes it useful to you. Meet an acronym you have never seen, and **the letters are your first clue to the words they came from** — one letter, one word, in order.

Here are the eleven your book puts on the page.

| Acronym    | The name behind the letters                                          |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **UNO**    | United Nations Organization                                          |
| **UNESCO** | United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization      |
| **UNICEF** | United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund               |
| **WHO**    | World Health Organization                                            |
| **WFP**    | World Food Programme                                                 |
| **WTO**    | World Trade Organization                                             |
| **WWF**    | World Wide Fund                                                      |
| **FAO**    | Food and Agriculture Organization                                    |
| **IMF**    | International Monetary Fund                                          |
| **OPEC**   | Oil Producing and Exporting Countries                                |
| **NOW**    | National Organization of Women                                       |

Four of them open on the same letter, and the letter therefore decides nothing: **W**HO, **W**FP, **W**TO and **W**WF all begin on *World*. It is the **second** letter that separates health from food, and trade from a fund. Read the letters one by one, in order, and the four come apart without difficulty.

> ⚠️ An acronym is **not** the same thing as a shortened word. *Prof.* for *professor* is a word with its tail cut off; **WHO** is a whole name reduced to one letter for each of its words. And membership settles nothing: **UN**ESCO, **UN**ICEF and **UN**O belong to the United Nations, but WWF, OPEC and the IMF do not — nothing in the machine says a body must belong to anybody.

### Three of them your book stops to explain

For most of the eleven, the expanded name is all you are given, and the name is therefore all you may reason from: a body called *Oil Producing and Exporting Countries* deals with oil, and no page will tell you more than that. Three, however, are explained.

| Body       | Set up in | What it is there for                                                                                       |
| ---------- | --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **UNESCO** | 1945      | to work for peace and security by bringing nations together through **educational, scientific and cultural** relations |
| **UNICEF** | 1946      | to uphold **children's rights** and to provide them with food, medicines, health care, **education** and a decent living |
| **WFP**    | 1963      | to fight **hunger** in the world; its house is in Rome                                                       |

So when a district has children who are not in school, two of the eleven are the doors to knock on: **UNESCO**, which was made for education, science and culture, and **UNICEF**, which was made for children — and whose list of what it hands out has schooling on it. And when the problem is a class that arrives hungry, the **WFP** is the third.

The other eight are not unimportant; they are simply somewhere else. Money is the IMF, trade is the WTO, health is WHO, farming and food supply are the FAO, oil is OPEC. **Expand the acronym and you have already sorted the letter into the right pile.**

## 📊 Reading a graph — the title first, the slices afterwards

Your book prints a circle cut into eight, headed:

> **REGIONAL DISTRIBUTION OF CHILDREN OUT OF SCHOOL, 2001/02**

A beginner reads the biggest slice first. **Read the title and the date first**, because until you have read them the slices mean nothing at all. Those few words tell you two things and settle everything that follows:

1. **what the circle is a picture of** — here, every child of primary age in the world who was out of school, all 115 million of them;
2. **when the count was made** — the school year 2001/02, and no other.

Then, and only then, the slices.

| Region                          | Share of the world's out-of-school children | Number of children |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| **South Asia**                  | 36.7 %                                       | 42.3 million        |
| **West and Central Africa**     | 20.8 %                                       | 24.0 million        |
| **Eastern and Southern Africa** | 18.6 %                                       | 21.4 million        |
| **East Asia and the Pacific**   | 8.7 %                                        | 10.0 million        |
| **Middle East and North Africa**| 7.6 %                                        | 8.8 million         |
| **Latin America and the Caribbean** | 2.8 %                                    | 3.3 million         |
| **Central and Eastern Europe**  | 2.5 %                                        | 2.9 million         |
| **Industrialized countries**    | 2.3 %                                        | 2.6 million         |
| **the whole circle**            | **100 %**                                    | **115 million**     |

**A quick check you can always run.** The slices of a pie are the parts of one whole, so their shares have to come to a hundred. Add these eight — 36.7, 20.8, 18.6, 8.7, 7.6, 2.8, 2.5, 2.3 — and you land on 100 exactly. If your eight figures come to 94, you have missed a slice or misread one.

> ⚠️ **The trap this graph sets, and it catches almost everybody.** The word in the title is **distribution**. A distribution says **where** something is, not **how bad** things are in each place. So the 2.3 % beside the industrialized countries does **not** mean that 2.3 % of their children are out of school. It means that **2.3 % of the world's out-of-school children live there**. To say what proportion of a region's own children are missing from class, you would need a second figure the graph never gives you: how many children that region holds altogether. A region with very few children can hold a small slice and still be doing badly; a region with a quarter of the world's children could hold a large slice and be doing well.

**The two columns say the same thing twice, in two units.** 8.7 % of 115 million is 10.0 million, and that is why the two columns put the regions in exactly the same order. Which means you can fill in a missing box yourself: if Eastern and Southern Africa holds 21.4 million out of the 115 million, its share is 21.4 divided by 115, that is **18.6 %**. Copying 21.4 into the percentage column is the classic slip — a number of children and a share of a total are not the same kind of quantity, however alike the two figures look.

> 🗡️ **Where the figures come from matters.** Under the circle your book prints the report the numbers were taken from: a 2005 study by the **UNESCO Institute for Statistics** called *Children out of school: measuring exclusion from primary education*. A graph without that line is a graph nobody can check.

**One phrase to be exact about.** A child who is **out of school** is not a child who has gone home for the evening, and not a child who has been sent into the corridor. It counts every child **who is of an age to be at school and whom no school anywhere has ever registered** — the ones the system never took in, and the ones it has lost since. The report calls that being kept outside the system **exclusion**, and its whole business is measuring it.

## 🗝️ Three words to carry away

| Word           | What it means                                              | Seen in action                                                     |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **an acronym** | a name folded into the first letters of its words          | _Nobody says the whole name; everybody says the acronym._            |
| **enrolment**  | registration — the act of putting a name on a register     | _Enrolment opens on the first of September and closes a week later._ |
| **therefore**  | consequently — what follows from what has just been said    | _The nearest secondary school moved eleven kilometres further off; nine girls therefore left at fifteen._ |

**enrolment** is the moment your name goes onto the register, once a year. It is not the same as turning up day after day, which the last lesson called *attendance*, and the gap between the two is where a great many official announcements hide. A child can be enrolled in September and be missing from the room in March: **the register counts names, not faces.** So a district whose enrolment has gone from 54 % to 90 % has put many more names down — and has proved nothing whatever about who is actually sitting in the classroom. (You will meet this word spelt with two l's as well; your book's word list prints it with one.)

**therefore** joins a cause to what came out of it. It is not *however*, which turns a sentence round, and it is not *for example*, which stays on the same idea. Put *therefore* between two sentences and you have claimed that the second one **happened because of** the first.

And two more from the page of the advertisement: to **donate** is to give money to a cause; a **recreation kit** is a boxed set of things to play with; **exercise books** are the empty ones a pupil fills with his own handwriting — nothing to do with sport, and not the printed volumes a teacher sets.

## 📢 An advertisement built on a sum

Your book prints a UNICEF page. The photograph and the paragraph are where you expect them, but the thing that stops the eye in the middle of the page is not a sentence at all. It is a sum:

> **Grade 2 + $1 = Grade 3**

**What draws the reader's attention here is an equation.** That is the device, and it is worth understanding why it works. A sentence has to be read; a sum is taken in at a glance and looks as if it had been proved. Unfolded into words, this one promises that **a gift of one dollar is the whole distance between the class a child is in and the class above it** — that the gap between staying and dropping out is that small.

Below it the body of the page does the arithmetic in ordinary language: what a single dollar buys in pencils and in the empty books a child writes in, what two hundred and twenty-five buy in a recreation kit for a whole class, and where to go to give. Then a web address, and nothing else.

**The purpose of a page like that is not to inform you. It is to persuade you to donate money.** Every part of it is aimed there: the equation stops you, the figures make the sum look small, the address tells you where to send it. A page that only wanted to describe poor children would have no need of any of that.

## 🕳️ Words printed unfinished

One activity on this page prints the advertisement with holes in it: **the first letters of a word are given, and one dot is printed for every letter that has been left out.** You have to put the word back.

> *For $225 a school can buy a recreation kit con……. toys, games and sports equipment.*

Two things have to agree before you write anything in, and neither is enough on its own:

1. **the letters and the length** — what is printed at the front cannot be changed, and the dots number what has gone, to the letter;
2. **the sense the sentence needs** — of the words that fit the shape, only one fits the meaning.

In the line above, *continuing*, *contacting*, *conducting* and *containing* all begin on *con* and all run to ten letters. The shape has eliminated nothing. It is the sentence that decides: a kit is a box, and what a box does to toys and games is **contain** them.

> 🗡️ Watch the near-misses especially. *Chance* and *choice* are both six letters and both open on *ch*; but a charity that says it gives a child a **chance** is promising an opportunity the child did not have, while giving a child a **choice** would be promising him a menu. Fit the shape, then read the sentence again before you commit.

## 🔁 if + a negative = unless + a positive

Here is the structure of this lesson, in the shape your book prints it:

> **If + negative verb  =  Unless + positive verb**

The two sentences below say **exactly** the same thing:

- _**If** we **do not** give to UNICEF, children will not have a chance._
- _**Unless** we **give** to UNICEF, children will not have a chance._

Look at what moved and what did not. The *not* has gone from the verb, because **unless is carrying it**: the word already means *if not*, and once you have written it the negative is spoken for. The second half of the sentence was not touched at all.

**1. Never put a second negative after unless.** This is the mistake that costs the most marks, and it says the opposite of what you meant.

- ~~_Unless we **don't** give to UNICEF, children will not have a chance._~~ ✗ — two negatives, and the sentence now warns you against giving.

**2. Unless has a short reach.** It carries its negative inside the clause it introduces and nowhere else, so the other half of the sentence keeps whatever verb it had, positive or negative. A pupil who has just learnt that *unless* hides a *not* is tempted to change both halves; there is nothing to change in the second.

| Given                                                   | Rewritten with *if*                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _Unless the gate is unlocked, the lorry **will wait** in the lane._ | _If the gate **is not** unlocked, the lorry **will wait** in the lane._ |
| _We **shall not** finish unless you help us._            | _We **shall not** finish if you **do not** help us._               |

**3. Unless is not if.** They are opposites, not synonyms. _If the gate is locked, the yard will be empty_ and _Unless the gate is locked, the yard will be empty_ promise contrary things, and only one of them is what a caretaker would chalk on a board.

**4. Either clause may come first.** _Unless we act, they will lose the year_ and _They will lose the year unless we act_ are the same sentence; when *unless* opens, a comma separates the halves, and when it comes second there is usually none.

**5. Read it against a real case before you answer.** An *unless* sentence sets one condition and announces what happens when it is not met — and, by the same stroke, what happens when it **is**. *The coach leaves on Saturday unless twelve names are down by Friday* tells you two things at once: with eleven names the trip is off, and with twelve it happens.

> 🗡️ **This is how you hypothesize in English.** The whole argument of this lesson is built out of these sentences: _Unless these children are enrolled, the region will lose a generation._ You are not describing what is; you are saying what will follow from a condition — and that is the shape every appeal on this subject takes.

## ⛲ A fountain, and why it runs dry

Your book closes the grammar with a saying attributed to Aristotle, and it is built on exactly this word. Knowledge, it says, is a fountain; and a fountain **runs dry unless** fresh water keeps arriving.

Turn the image back into plain words and you have the argument of the whole unit: **somebody who stops learning does not simply stand still — he loses, little by little, what he already had.** The condition is continuous, which is why the sentence needs *unless* and not a simple *if*: nothing is required for the water to sink except that nobody adds any.

That is also the answer to the question the lesson keeps asking you for your own opinion — *which organisations can help? how can a child be saved?* An opinion here is worth exactly as much as the condition you can name behind it.

| Wrong                                             | Right                                             | Why                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "unless we don't help them"                      | _unless we help them_                             | the negative is already inside *unless*                                 |
| "unless" = "if"                                   | _unless_ = **if not**                             | the two announce contrary things                                        |
| "2.3 % of their children are out of school"      | _2.3 % of the world's out-of-school children live there_ | the graph shows a **distribution**, not a rate                     |
| "21.4 million, therefore 21.4 %"                 | _21.4 out of 115, therefore **18.6 %**_          | a head count and a share of a total are different quantities            |
| "exercise books" = books about sport             | _the blank books you write your work in_         | the false friend of this page                                           |
| "enrolment has risen, so the classes are full"   | _more names are on the register_                  | the register counts names, not the children present in March            |
| "WHO, WTO, WWF — all much the same"              | _health, trade, a fund_                           | four bodies share the *W*; the **second** letter separates them          |
