# ⚔️ At the Travel Agency — The Promise, and What Came of It

> 💡 "A holiday is bought twice: once from a brochure that promises, and once from a hotel that delivers. When the two do not match, only language is left."

Two months separate the two halves of this lesson. In the first, a customer sits at a counter and listens to everything a travel agent promises her. In the second, she comes back to complain — and a complaint lives or dies on the promises she can put back, word for word, in front of the person who made them. That is why the grammar of this lesson is **reported speech**: a complaint is a promise repeated aloud by somebody who did not get it.

You will learn to **say you are dissatisfied**, to **complain** without being rude, to **report what was promised**, and to hear where an English word puts its **stress** — because at a counter, a word stressed on the wrong syllable is a word nobody understands.

## 📊 1. Before the counter: why people travel

The lesson opens with your own class as its data. You ask each other eleven questions — _Why do people travel? Who do they visit? How long did you stay? What means of transport did you use?_ — and you turn the answers into four **pie charts**: _Percentage of travellers_, _Purposes_, _Duration of stay_, _Mode of transport_.

A pie chart is one circle cut into slices; every slice is a **percentage**, and all the slices together make **100 %**. Say it like this: _Forty per cent of our group travelled **for family reasons**, thirty per cent **for study**, and only ten per cent **for business**._

| Category | Words you will need |
| --- | --- |
| **Purposes** | for business · to visit relatives · for study · for medical treatment · for pleasure · on pilgrimage |
| **Duration of stay** | a weekend · a fortnight (two weeks) · a month · the whole summer |
| **Mode of transport** | by air · by coach · by train · by ferry · by car |

Then comes the second warm-up, and it is the real subject of the lesson: _What does a travel agent sell? How does the travel agent convince customers? **Should tourists always trust travel agents?**_ Hold on to that last question. The whole lesson is its answer.

## 🏷️ 2. The words you pay with

| Word | What it means | In a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **to book** | to reserve and pay for a place in advance | _She booked the holiday in April._ |
| **a booking / a reservation** | the place kept for you once you have booked | _I have a reservation in the name of Ward._ |
| **a deposit** | the first part of the price, paid to hold the booking | _They asked for a deposit of 20 %._ |
| **the balance** | the rest of the price, paid later | _The balance is due six weeks before you fly._ |
| **a refund** | your money given back to you | _She asked for a full refund._ |
| **a discount** | money taken **off** a price you are still going to pay | _Students get a 15 % discount._ |
| **a voucher** | a printed paper you exchange for a service already paid for | _Show this voucher when you check in._ |
| **a receipt** | the paper that proves you have paid | _Keep the receipt as proof of payment._ |
| **a package holiday** | flight, hotel and transfers sold together at one price | _A package holiday costs less than booking the three parts yourself._ |
| **a transfer** | the journey between the airport and the hotel | _The transfer is included; someone will meet your plane._ |
| **an excursion** | a short organised trip during a holiday | _There is an excursion to the volcano on Fridays._ |
| **airport taxes** | what an airport charges every passenger | _Airport taxes are not always included in the price._ |
| **facilities** | the equipment and services a hotel puts at your disposal | _The hotel's facilities include a lift, a laundry and a children's club._ |
| **scheduled** | planned for a fixed time, on a published timetable | _The flight is scheduled for 21.15._ |
| **superb** | excellent, magnificent | _The view from the terrace was superb._ |
| **disappointed** | sad because something was less good than you expected | _She was bitterly disappointed with the room._ |
| **an oil refinery** | the factory where crude oil is turned into fuel | _Nobody mentioned the oil refinery next to the beach._ |

Four of these are confused every year, and the confusion costs money:

- a **deposit** is what you **give**; a **refund** is what you **get back**;
- a **refund** gives you your money; a **discount** only makes the **next** price smaller;
- **to book** is the verb, **a booking** or **a reservation** the noun. ✗ "I want to reservation a room." → ✓ _I want to **book** a room._
- **scheduled** is not "expected". **Scheduled** is the time printed on the timetable, the time the flight was planned for; **expected** is the time it will really go. An airport screen that prints _scheduled 09.30 — expected 12.00_ is telling you politely that your flight is two and a half hours late.

> ⚠️ **disappointed** or **disappointing**? The person feels it, the thing causes it: _we were **disappointed**_, _the hotel was **disappointing**_. And the preposition: you are disappointed **with** or **by** a thing, disappointed **in** a person.

## 📄 3. What "included" really covers

Everything in this lesson turns on one small word. Before you sign, ask the question in full: **What exactly is included in the price?**

| Usually included in a package | Often **not** included |
| --- | --- |
| the hotel, the flights and the transfers | airport taxes |
| breakfast, sometimes half board | drinks with meals |
| the services of a local representative | excursions |
| — | travel insurance, tips |

And read the **booking conditions**, the small print at the bottom of the page. Two rules appear in almost all of them, and they are the two the customer discovers too late:

1. **A deposit is payable on booking** — the booking does not exist until it is paid.
2. **The deposit is not refundable** after a certain date. Cancel before that date and you lose nothing; cancel after it and the money stays with the agency.

## 😞 4. Saying that you are not satisfied

English grades dissatisfaction very finely, and the grade you choose says how far you are ready to go.

| Strength | What you say |
| --- | --- |
| mild | _It wasn't quite what we had expected._ · _The room was a little disappointing._ |
| clear | _I was rather disappointed with the hotel._ · _It wasn't what your brochure led us to expect._ |
| strong | _I was **very** disappointed with the whole trip._ · _The hotel **fell far short of** your description._ |
| very strong | _The service was **far from satisfactory**._ · _The week was a complete disaster._ |

Two habits of English make dissatisfaction sound different from the way it sounds in other languages.

**Understatement.** A British guest very often says less than she means, and the listener is expected to hear the rest: _The coach was **not exactly** punctual._ (= it was always late) · _The food was **nothing to write home about**._ (= it was poor) · _We were **not entirely** happy with the arrangements._ (= we were angry). A polite sentence is not a soft complaint; it is a strong complaint in a quiet voice — and the guest who says "**not exactly** what was promised" is telling you that it was nothing like it.

**Adding up.** One problem is bad luck; three problems are a case. English strings them with _and what is more_, _on top of that_, and above all **_to make matters worse_**: _Our luggage arrived a day late, and **to make matters worse**, the air conditioning broke down on the second night._

## 📣 5. Complaining — the four moves

A complaint that works is never one sentence. It has four moves, always in this order.

| Move | What it does | Exponents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Open it** | announce that this is a complaint, and name its subject | _I'd like to make a complaint about…_ · _I'm afraid there's a problem with…_ · _I'm calling about the trip I took last week._ |
| **2. State the facts** | say what actually happened, with dates and figures | _Our room was still occupied when we arrived at eleven at night._ |
| **3. Recall the promise** | quote what you were told — **this is where reported speech lives** | _You said that half board was included in the price._ · _Your brochure described the area as quiet and residential._ |
| **4. Say what you want** | ask for something precise | _I'd like the two extra dinners refunded._ · _I expect some compensation for the night we lost._ |

Move 4 decides whether you will be taken seriously. Ask for **what matches what you lost**: the price of the two nights you spent in a room you had not booked, not the price of the whole week. A claim bigger than the loss lets the agency argue about the claim instead of the loss.

**Firm is not rude.** The two are easy to tell apart: a firm complaint attacks the **problem**, a rude one attacks the **person**.

- ✓ _I'd like to make a complaint: the lift was out of order for the whole of our stay._ (firm — it names the fault and expects it to be dealt with)
- ✗ _Your hotel is falling apart and your staff lied to us about it._ (rude — and it gives the manager an excuse to answer the tone rather than the facts)
- ✗ _Sorry to bother you, it's nothing really, but the lift wasn't working._ (so soft that nothing will be done)
- ✗ _Am I supposed to believe that a working lift is an extra in your hotel?_ (an attack wearing the mask of a question)

**Speaking or writing?** At the desk you may begin with _Excuse me, I have a problem with…_. On paper the opening is fixed and you should learn it as a formula: **_I am writing to complain about…_** — followed by the facts, the promise, the request, and _I look forward to hearing from you._

## 🗣️ 6. Reporting the promise — the grammar of this lesson

You met the full rules of reported speech in the entrance check of the year. Here they come back for **one job**: repeating a promise in front of the person who made it.

### The advertisement promises

An advertisement talks to you in the **present** and in **will**. Here is the kind of line the Hotel Superb prints among its seven good reasons for a visit:

> _You'll love the food._ · _All the hotel staff are friendly._ · _You'll enjoy the scenery._

### The complaint reports it

Two things move at once, and the second is the one this lesson adds.

**The tenses step back**, exactly as they did in chapter one:

| In the advertisement | In your complaint |
| --- | --- |
| _You **will** love the food._ | _You said that we **would** love the food._ |
| _All the hotel staff **are** friendly._ | _You said that all the hotel staff **were** friendly._ |
| _The rooms **have** air conditioning._ | _You said that the rooms **had** air conditioning._ |
| _We **booked** your rooms in April._ | _You said that you **had booked** our rooms in April._ |
| _You **can** hear the sea from the terrace._ | _You said that we **could** hear the sea from the terrace._ |
| _You **must** reserve the sauna in advance._ | _You said that we **had to** reserve the sauna in advance._ |

A past that is already past goes one step further back, to the **past perfect**: a plain _we booked_ becomes _you said you **had booked**_. And the **time words travel with the tenses**, because the day of the promise is no longer the day of the complaint: _today_ → **that day**, _yesterday_ → **the day before**, _last week_ → **the week before**, _tomorrow_ → **the next day**, _this afternoon_ → **that afternoon**, _now_ → **then**.

**The people change sides.** This is the trap of the lesson. In the advertisement, **you** means the reader — the future guest. In the complaint, that reader is speaking, so the advertisement's **you** becomes **we** or **I**, and the word **you** is now free for a new job: it names **the person who made the promise**.

> _"**You**'ll love the food."_ (the hotel speaks to the guest)
> → _"**You** said that **we** would love the food, but **we** didn't; it was awful."_ (the guest speaks to the hotel)

Same two words, opposite directions. Read any complaint carefully and you can always say who is who: the **you** is the one who promised, the **we** is the one who paid.

### The pattern

Move 3 of a complaint has a shape you can rely on, and it is exactly the model the book prints:

> **You said (that) + [the promise, backshifted] + but + [what actually happened].**

The reported half alone is neutral — _You said that the rooms would be ready at noon_ is a simple report, and nothing more. It becomes a complaint only when the second half contradicts it: _**but ours was still being cleaned at four**_. Promise, then reality: that is a complaint in one sentence, and the joint is almost always **but**.

### Choosing the reporting verb

_Say_ and _tell_ report the words. The verbs below report the words **and the force behind them** — and an examiner reads them as a sign that you can do more than transform a sentence.

| Verb | When you use it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **complain that** | the speaker was expressing dissatisfaction | _He complained that nobody had warned him about the taxes._ |
| **add that** | the speaker went on to a further point | _She added that she would never travel with them again._ |
| **point out that** | the speaker brought a fact into the argument | _She pointed out that the small print said nothing about it._ |
| **promise that** | the speaker committed himself | _He promised that the balance could be paid in September._ |

Four verbs are enough, and they are the four your own book uses when it reports a complaint. Resist the longer list you may have met elsewhere: _insist_, _admit_ and _deny_ are taught in other lessons and in other constructions, and a verb used in the wrong construction costs you more marks than _say_ ever will.

### Report faithfully

A complaint stands or falls on one thing: **what you report must be what was really said.** The manager will have the brochure in front of him.

> The brochure printed: _The hotel is a short walk from the beach._
> ✓ _You said that the hotel was a short walk from the beach, and it took us forty minutes._
> ✗ _You said that the hotel was **on the beach**._ — nobody ever said that, and the whole complaint collapses with it.

Reporting more than was promised is the commonest way of losing an argument you were winning.

### Reading a report backwards

The examination often asks the opposite journey: here is the report, what were the **exact words**? Undo the two moves — bring the tense one pace forward, bring the people back to the speaker's side.

> _She said that the airport taxes **were** included._ → _"The airport taxes **are** included."_
> _He told her that a driver **would** be waiting **the next day**._ → _"A driver **will** be waiting **tomorrow**."_

Watch the middle step: a report with **were** can come from a present _are_ (backshifted) — not from a past _were_, which would have gone one step further back, to **had been**.

## 🤝 7. What the agent answers

The counter has its own language, and you need to understand it to answer it.

| The agent's move | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **apologise** | _I do apologise for the inconvenience, madam._ |
| **explain** | _Half board has never included drinks with meals._ |
| **refuse** | _I'm afraid we can't refund a trip that took place._ |
| **offer compensation** | _We can offer you a free upgrade on your next booking. Would that be acceptable?_ |

A single answer often does two of these at once — _I do apologise for the delay at the airport, **but** the taxes were listed as an extra on your booking form_ apologises for one complaint and rejects the other, and it produces a document as evidence. Listen for the **but**: everything after it is what the agency is refusing.

And you have to answer the offer:

- **accepting** — _That seems fair, thank you._ · _Yes, that would be acceptable._
- **refusing politely** — _I'm afraid that isn't good enough._ · _I'd rather have a refund._

> ⚠️ **I'm afraid** looks like an apology and is not one: it is the cushion English puts under a piece of bad news, and what follows it is a refusal. Weigh an offer before you take it — a **discount** only makes a future holiday cheaper, so it buys nothing at all for someone who has no intention of coming back, while a **refund** returns money you have already spent.

## 🔊 8. Word stress

Every English word of more than one syllable has **one** syllable said louder, longer and higher than the others. Put it in the wrong place and the word disappears: a customer who asks for a de-**POS**-it is understood; one who asks for a **DE**-po-sit is asked to repeat.

| Stress | Words of this lesson |
| --- | --- |
| **1st syllable** | **PACK**-age · **SCHED**-ule · **TRAV**-el · **IN**-ter-est-ed · **BOOK**-ing · **A**-gen-cy · **CUS**-tom-er · **LUGG**-age |
| **2nd syllable** | de-**POS**-it · re-**FIN**-er-y · in-**CLUD**-ed · ex-**CUR**-sion · su-**PERB** · com-**PLAINT** · ho-**TEL** · fa-**CIL**-i-ties |
| **3rd syllable** | res-er-**VA**-tion · rec-om-**MEND** · dis-ap-**POINT**-ed · com-pen-**SA**-tion |

Three things to notice:

1. The endings you already met in _Art Shows_ still work: **-tion** pulls the stress onto the syllable just before it (_reser**VA**tion_, _compen**SA**tion_).
2. In two-syllable words there is a **tendency**, not a law: the noun is often stressed first (_**PACK**age_, _**LUGG**age_, _**BOOK**ing_) and the verb second (_re-**LAX**_, _com-**PLAIN**_, _in-**CLUDE**_). _Ho**TEL**_ is the famous exception; you simply learn it.
3. Some words **move** between the two, exactly like _a **REC**ord_ and _to re**CORD**_: **a REfund** but **to reFUND**, **a TRANSfer** but **to transFER**, **a DIScount** but **to disCOUNT**. Your book's table sorts _discount_ among the words whose stress falls second — the pronunciation of the verb.

> 💡 Stress is part of the word, like a letter. Learn it the day you learn the word, or you will learn it twice.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "You said that the coach **will** pick us up at nine." | _You said that the coach **would** pick us up at nine._ | After a past reporting verb, **will** becomes **would** |
| "You said that **you** would love the food." | _You said that **we** would love the food._ | The advertisement's **you** is the guest, who is now the speaker |
| "You said the hotel was on the beach." (the brochure said _near_) | _You said the hotel was **near** the beach._ | Report what was promised, not what you hoped |
| "I want my deposit **discounted**." | _I want my deposit **refunded**._ | A refund gives money back; a discount only lowers a future price |
| "The flight is **expected** at 09.30 on the ticket." | _The flight is **scheduled** for 09.30._ | **scheduled** = planned; **expected** = what will really happen |
| "Your receptionist is completely useless." | _There was nobody at the desk for an hour, and I'd like to know why._ | Attack the problem, not the person |
| "I was disappointed **of** the room." | _I was disappointed **with** the room._ | disappointed **with / by** a thing, **in** a person |
| "The whole holiday was disappointed." | _The whole holiday was **disappointing**._ | **-ed** for the person, **-ing** for the cause |
| "He said me that the taxes were included." | _He **told me** that the taxes were included._ | You **tell** somebody; you **say** something **to** somebody |

> 🏆 Gate cleared, hero. You can read the small print before you sign it, say you are dissatisfied without raising your voice, and put a broken promise back in the mouth that made it. Next quest: **Put a Little Drama in Your Travel** — where the journey ends at the theatre.
