# ⚔️ Checking Year 3 Programme — The Gate Before the Final Year

> 💡 "Nobody walks into the last year with an empty pack. This chapter is the inspection: what you already carry, and whether it still holds."

You are starting the year that ends with the bac. Before a single new lesson begins, the book stops for five pages and asks one question: **is what you learnt in Year 3 still solid?** Not "do you remember the rule" — **can you use it**, in a sentence you write yourself, with a clock running.

Four things are checked here, and the whole book stands on them: the **tenses** you tell a story with, the **reported speech** you quote somebody with, the **words** you build and choose, and the **process** you write with.

## ⏳ 1. Three tenses, three jobs

### The past simple — a moment that is closed

The event happened, it finished, and the sentence says **when**.

- _The club **launched** its website three weeks ago._
- _Forty pupils **stayed** at home last Monday._

Its markers are all closed doors: **ago**, **yesterday**, **last week / month / year**, **in 2019**, **then**, **when I was fifteen**.

### The present perfect — a past that still touches now

**have / has + past participle.** The event belongs to the past, but the sentence is really about the present situation.

- _My parents **have lived** in this town **since** 2019._ (they still live there)
- _The headmaster **has never had** to shut the school **before**._ (up to today, it has never happened)
- _Attendance **has fallen** by half **over the past three weeks**._ (the three weeks include today)

Its markers all keep the door open: **since** + a starting point, **for** + a length of time, **ever / never … before**, **so far**, **already**, **yet**, **just**, **this week**, **over the past few days**.

> ⚠️ The one rule that decides almost every exercise: **a closed time marker and the present perfect cannot share a sentence.** _The school closed last Monday._ ✓ — "The school has closed last Monday." ✗

**since** and **for** are not interchangeable: **since** names the **point** where the situation started (_since 2004_, _since Monday_, _since I was a child_), **for** names its **length** (_for twenty-one years_, _for three weeks_).

### The past perfect — a past before the past

**had + past participle.** You are already in the past and you look back at something earlier.

- _When the inspector arrived, the parents **had** already **taken** their children home._

You will need it constantly in section 2, because reported speech uses it as its standard backshift.

| Tense          | Form              | Typical markers                              |
| -------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Past simple    | _closed_          | ago, yesterday, last Monday, in 2019, then   |
| Present perfect | _has closed_     | since, for, never … before, so far, this week |
| Past perfect   | _had closed_      | before that, by then, already (in a past story) |

## 🗣️ 2. Reported speech — quoting without quotation marks

A journalist writes down what the headmaster said. The quotation marks disappear, and **three things move at once**. Miss one of the three and the sentence is wrong.

### Move 1 — the tenses step one pace back

| Direct speech       | Reported after a past verb |
| ------------------- | -------------------------- |
| present simple _is_ | past simple _was_          |
| present continuous _is working_ | past continuous _was working_ |
| past simple _started_ | past perfect _had started_ |
| present perfect _has started_ | past perfect _had started_ |
| past perfect _had started_ | **no change** — _had started_ |
| _will_              | _would_                    |
| _can_               | _could_                    |
| _may_               | _might_                    |
| _must / have to_    | _had to_                   |

### Move 2 — the people move to the reporter's point of view

_I_ → **he / she** · _we_ → **they** · _my_ → **his / her** · _our_ → **their** · _you_ → the person actually meant · _your_ → **his / her / my**.

### Move 3 — the time and place words move away from the moment of speaking

_now_ → **then, at that time** · _today_ → **that day** · _yesterday_ → **the day before, the previous day** · _tomorrow_ → **the next day, the following day** · _last week_ → **the week before** · _ago_ → **before** · _here_ → **there** · _this_ → **that**.

Put the three moves together:

> **Direct:** _"We started the vaccinations yesterday, and today we are up to two hundred children,"_ the nurse said on Friday.
> **Reported:** _The nurse said that they **had started** the vaccinations **the day before** and that **that day** they **were** up to two hundred children._

### say or tell?

You **say** something (**to** somebody), you **tell** **somebody** something. The person comes straight after _tell_, never after _say_.

- _She **said that** she was tired._ · _She **said to** her father that she was tired._ · _She **told her father that** she was tired._
- ✗ "She said her father that…" · ✗ "She told to her father that…"

### A long quotation needs several introducing verbs

A speech of four sentences is not reported with four _he saids_. English shares the work out: _he **explained** that…_, _he **added** that…_, _he **admitted** that…_, _he **complained** that…_, _he **ended by saying** that…_

### Reporting a question — the question stops being one

No inversion, no auxiliary _do_, no question mark. The subject goes **back in front of** its verb.

- _"Where are the sick pupils now?"_ → _He asked **where the sick pupils were then**._
- _"How many pupils are off sick?"_ → _He asked **how many pupils were off sick**._
- _"Do you close on Fridays?"_ → _He asked **whether / if they closed on Fridays**._

> ⚠️ The commonest mistake in the whole chapter: "He asked where **were** the sick pupils." The inversion belongs to the direct question only.

### Reporting an order or a piece of advice

**tell / ask / advise + somebody + to + base verb** (and _not to_ for a negative).

- _"Close the school."_ → _The authority **told them to close** the school._
- _"You should see a doctor."_ → _She **advised him to see** a doctor._
- _"Don't come back before Monday."_ → _He **told them not to come** back before Monday._

## 🔨 3. Building words

### From a verb or an adjective to a noun

English rarely lets you keep the same form; the noun has its own shape, and the exercises live on that.

| Verb or adjective | Noun          | In a sentence                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| to advise         | **advice**    | _He followed the advice of the doctor._           |
| to break out      | **an outbreak** | _An outbreak of flu closed three schools._      |
| to enrol          | **enrolment** | _Enrolment starts in September._                  |
| to erupt          | **an eruption** | _The eruption lasted two days._                 |
| ill               | **illness**   | _Nobody expected such a long illness._            |
| anxious           | **anxiety**   | _Her anxiety grew every evening._                 |

Two of them are famous traps. **advise** (verb, with an _s_) and **advice** (noun, with a _c_) are two different words. And with a phrasal verb the noun is a **compound** in which the particle jumps in front: _to break **out**_ → _an **out**break_ (the same in _outcome_, _outlook_, _outset_).

### The prefixes

| Prefix    | It adds          | Examples                          |
| --------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **re-**   | again            | repay, reassurance                |
| **over-** | too much         | overworked, overburdened          |
| **mis-**  | badly, wrongly   | misconduct, misjudgement          |
| **well-** | well (hyphenated) | well-being, well-mannered        |
| **un-**   | not              | unreachable, unfathomable         |
| **dis-**  | not, the opposite | disability, disobedient          |
| **il-**   | not              | illegal, illiteracy               |
| **im-**   | not              | immortal, impatient               |

The four negative prefixes _il-_, _im-_, _ir-_ and _in-_ are **the same prefix**, and the **first letter of the word** chooses which one you write:

- **il-** before **l** — _illegal, illiterate, illogical_
- **im-** before **m, p, b** — _immortal, impatient, impossible_
- **ir-** before **r** — _irregular, irresponsible_
- **in-** everywhere else — _incorrect, inability_

_un-_ and _dis-_ obey no letter rule; you learn them word by word. That is why "unlegal" and "unpatient" are wrong while _unreachable_ and _unfair_ are right.

### Adjectives in **-ed** and in **-ing**

The pair comes from the same verb and they are not interchangeable.

- **-ed** says **how somebody feels**: _the children were **depressed**_, _I am **interested**_, _we were **surprised**_.
- **-ing** says **what causes the feeling**: _the situation was **depressing**_, _the book is **interesting**_, _the result was **surprising**_.

> ⚠️ _I am boring_ says that you send other people to sleep. _I am bored_ says that you are the one falling asleep.

## 🎯 4. Choosing between words that look alike

| Word            | Meaning                                   | Example                                    |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **rise**        | go up — **no object**                     | _Prices rise every winter._                |
| **raise**       | put up, collect — **needs an object**     | _They raised money for a school bus._      |
| **arise**       | come into existence                       | _A new difficulty arose._                  |
| **require**     | need as a necessary condition             | _The job requires trained staff._          |
| **acquire**     | obtain, gain                              | _She acquired a second language abroad._   |
| **request**     | ask for politely                          | _They requested a meeting._                |
| **inquire**     | ask for information                       | _He inquired about the timetable._         |

And the quantifiers: **a lot of** and **plenty of** both go with a positive sentence (_a lot of work_, _plenty of time_), while **any** belongs to a negative or a question (_I haven't got any time._ · _Have you got any brothers?_).

## 🔗 5. The linkers

| Relation        | Linkers and how they are built                                                     |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **contrast**    | _although / though_ + clause · _but_ (joins from the middle) · _However,_ (new sentence) · _in spite of_ + noun |
| **consequence** | _Therefore,_ · _As a result,_ · _so_ + clause                                       |
| **reason**      | _because / as / since_ + clause · _because of_ + noun                              |
| **addition**    | _and_ · _Moreover,_ · _In addition,_                                                |
| **alternative** | _Otherwise,_ ("if that were not the case") · _instead of_ + noun or -ing            |

Three of them cause almost all the mistakes:

- **although** opens a subordinate clause at the front of the sentence: _**Although** he worked full time, he cooked the evening meal every day._
- **however** is not a substitute for _but_: it needs a full stop or a semicolon before it and a comma after it. _He worked full time. **However,** he cooked every day._
- **therefore** announces a **result**. If the second idea contradicts the first, _therefore_ is simply the wrong linker — and your corrector has a symbol for exactly that (see section 7).

## 🗂️ 6. Lexical fields

Words travel in families. Sorting new vocabulary under a heading is what lets you guess an unknown word from its neighbours, and revise fifty words instead of fifty times one word.

| Field                    | Words from the check                                  |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Leisure**              | hiking, a resort, a buffet, dog sledge riding         |
| **Education**            | enrolment, graduation, a syllabus, misconduct         |
| **Family relationships** | parenting, a bachelor, a carer, a date                |
| **Science and Technology** | a gene, transgenic, insemination, surgery           |
| **Nature**               | an eruption, a sandstorm, a typhoon, a weed           |

A word can belong to two fields depending on the sentence — _depression_ is a state of mind, a period of economic crisis **and** a low-pressure area on a weather map. The field is decided by the **text**, never by the word alone.

## ✍️ 7. Writing as a process

An article is not written in one go from the first word to the last. The book gives you **six steps**, and you will use them eight times this year.

| Step  | What you do                                                                    |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1** | **Generating ideas** — you throw everything down in a list, a chart, a diagram |
| **2** | **Planning and organizing the ideas** — you select, you order, you make notes  |
| **3** | **Writing the first draft** — the notes become sentences joined by linkers     |
| **4** | **Sharing and responding** — a classmate reads it and marks it (peer review)   |
| **5** | **Revising and editing** — you act on the marks: content first, then spelling, punctuation, grammar, linkers |
| **6** | **Writing the final draft** — a neat copy, with its format and layout          |

The order is not decorative. You cannot organize ideas you have not generated; a classmate cannot review a draft that does not exist; and correcting the spelling of a paragraph you are about to delete is wasted work. **Content before form, always.**

### Developing notes into sentences (step 3)

Notes are telegraphic: no articles, no prepositions, no finite verb. Developing them means putting all of that back.

> **Notes:** _Media – campaign – change attitudes – parents – in favour of integration._
> **Sentence:** _A media campaign **can change** the attitudes of parents **in favour of** integration._

The single most important thing you add is a **finite verb**. _A media campaign changing the attitudes…_ is still not a sentence — it has no verb that can stand on its own.

### The correction code

Your classmate — and your teacher — write these symbols in the margin. They do not correct for you; they **name** the mistake so that you correct it yourself. Learn them now: the same code is used in every writing task of the book.

| Symbol   | What it points to                    |
| -------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **V**    | Vocabulary mistake or misused word   |
| **G**    | Grammar mistake                      |
| **S**    | Spelling mistake                     |
| **P**    | Punctuation (wrong punctuation mark) |
| **C**    | Capitalization                       |
| **W.L**  | Wrong Linker                         |
| **W.O.** | Word Order                           |
| **√**    | Something missing in the sentence    |

Two of them are constantly confused. **V** is for an ordinary word that does not exist or does not fit the meaning; **W.L** is kept for a **link word** that does not fit the relation between two ideas. And **S** is a mistake inside a word (letters), while **W.O.** is a mistake between words (their order).

### The writing task, step by step

The book asks you to write an article for your school magazine about the integration of children with special needs, as stated in the 2002 Education Act, and it walks you through five instructions: develop the notes into sentences · link them with suitable connectors · exchange your work with a classmate for peer correction with the code · revise your draft once you get it back · edit the final draft and put it in your **PORTFOLIO**.

Useful vocabulary for that article: _special needs, integration, learning requirements, special assistance, technical support, health care, diagnosing, parent counselling, guidance_.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                   | Right                                        | Why                                                    |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| "The school has closed last Monday."    | _The school closed last Monday._             | A closed time marker forbids the present perfect       |
| "He has been head since twenty years."  | _He has been head for twenty years._         | **for** = a length, **since** = a starting point       |
| "He asked where were the pupils."       | _He asked where the pupils were._            | A reported question keeps the order of a statement     |
| "She said her father that…"             | _She told her father that…_                  | You **tell** somebody, you **say** something **to** somebody |
| "He said he has lost his keys."         | _He said he **had** lost his keys._          | After a past reporting verb the tense steps back       |
| "It is unlegal."                        | _It is illegal._                             | **il-** before a word beginning with _l_               |
| "The film was very bored."              | _The film was very boring._                  | **-ing** for the cause, **-ed** for the person feeling |
| "They raised at six o'clock."           | _They rose at six o'clock._ / _They got up…_ | **raise** always needs an object                       |
| "My teacher adviced me to rest."         | _My teacher advised me to rest._             | **advice** is the noun, **advise** the verb            |
