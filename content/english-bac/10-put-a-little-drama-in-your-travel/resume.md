# 📜 Summary: Put a Little Drama in Your Travel

- **The lesson runs both ways** — reading squeezes prose into notes (a programme into a table), writing opens notes back into prose (a box of notes into a biography). No new grammar: the book's own column is empty for this lesson.
- **The four columns of a programme** — **Play** (the title) · **Troupe** or company (the people performing) · **Venue** (the exact spot, *not* the town) · **Dates** (usually a **run**, every evening between the two days, inside the festival's **season**). The playwright wrote the words; the troupe performs them tonight.
- **The words** — a **venue** (the place of a public event) · a **troupe** (actors who work together) · a **playwright** = a **dramatist** (the writer of plays) · **the Bard** (Shakespeare, and nobody else) · **choreography** (inventing the steps of a dance) · **sightseeing** (visiting what a town is known for) · **to head for / to head to** (to move in the direction of) · **on the calendar** (decided, its days now settled) · a **cybertrip** (a journey looked up and arranged from a screen) · **to stage** a play (verb) / **a stage** (the raised floor, noun).
- **A programme has an age** — the byline, the agency and above all the **date** printed above a travel article. Old pages prove that a festival exists; they never give this year's days or prices. And the article writes sentences where the programme entry writes a record with its verbs removed.
- **Four steps into a dictionary** — **1)** look for the **base form** (*staged* → stage) · **2)** decide the word's job in your sentence and pick the **noun half or the verb half** · **3)** read down the numbered senses inside that half · **4)** a fixed expression is filed **last**, behind a label such as *idm.* An entry also gives the pronunciation, the American spelling (the *-re → -er* switch of *First Impressions*: *centre* / *center*) and, in its little example, the grammar: *head **for** sth*.
- **Notes are not sentences** — they drop the subject, the verb and the small words. Give each note a **subject**, a **verb in the past**, and the articles and prepositions back.
- **Three rules for a biography** — **1)** the **past tense** the whole way through · **2)** the **years stay in line** — and when the notes carry none, their own facts still lock them in order · **3)** **add nothing the notes do not carry**: working a fact out from two dates is allowed, giving a reason or a judgement nobody wrote down is not.
- **The first line** names the person and says **why a stranger should read on**; the dates start only afterwards.
- **The eight linkers, and their claims** — **and** (both are true) · **but** (the second goes against what the first led you to expect) · **after** (once the other was over) · **next** / **then** (the very next step) · **some time later** (an unmeasured gap) · **eventually** / **in the end** (the outcome, after a long wait). A linker is a claim: **but** without a contradiction, or **because** without a cause in the notes, invents just as surely as a false date does. When you hesitate, **and** claims the least.
