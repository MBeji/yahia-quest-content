# ⚔️ Holidaying — The Summer You Used to Have

> 💡 "A holiday is a story told in two tenses: the one your parents lived, and the one you live now."

Ask a family what August looked like thirty years ago and you get the same picture: the grandparents' house, the cousins, work in the fields. Ask their children today and the picture has moved: friends, water sports, a plane ticket. This lesson gives you the tools to handle that change — **read** it in a table, **say** it with the right adjectives, **write** it with `used to` and `would`, **join** the two periods with contrast linkers — and, along the way, two habits of a good reader and a good writer: recognising a text by its format, and keeping a sentence in shape with parallelism.

## 📊 Reading a ranking table

A ranking table does not give numbers of people. It gives **positions**: 1 is the favourite, and the higher the figure, the less popular the outing. Here is a table of that kind — what two groups said they would most like to do on a sunny day.

| Summer outing    | Children's ranking | Adults' ranking |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | --------------- |
| the seaside      | 1                  | 1               |
| a sports event   | 2                  | 5               |
| visiting friends | 3=                 | 2               |
| a country walk   | 3=                 | 3               |
| a museum         | 5                  | 4               |

Two things must be read carefully. First, the sign **`=`**: `3=` is printed twice, so two outings are **equal** and **share** third place — which is why nobody is fourth in that column and the next position is fifth. Second, you must read **across**, not down: the same outing carries two positions, one per group.

Say what you see with the verbs of ranking: _the seaside **comes first** for both groups_ · _a sports event **drops from** second **to** fifth_ · _a museum **climbs** one place among adults_ · _visiting friends and a country walk **are equally popular** with children_.

> 🗡️ Before you compare, look for the **agreement**: here both groups put the seaside first. A comparison that only lists differences has read half the table.

## 🏖️ Talking about your holidays — the words that judge

Four question words open a conversation about holidays: _**Where** did you go? **When** did you leave? **How** did you travel? **What** did you do there?_ The answer becomes interesting when you **judge** the holiday, and each adjective judges something different.

| Adjective                  | What it says about the holiday          |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **exciting**               | it gave you strong, new emotions        |
| **interesting**            | you learned something                   |
| **enjoyable**              | it was simply pleasant                  |
| **relaxing**               | it rested you                           |
| **boring** / **monotonous** | nothing happened / nothing ever changed |
| **active** / **lazy**      | you moved a lot / you moved very little |
| **cheap** / **expensive**  | it cost little / it cost a lot          |
| **plain** / **original**   | ordinary / like nobody else's           |

Holidays then split into two columns. **Active holidays** hold the sporting activities — water skiing, sailing, horse riding, walking in the mountains. **Lazy holidays** hold everything that asks nothing of your body — sunbathing, sleeping late, playing cards, reading on a terrace.

> ⚠️ **lazy** here describes the **holiday**, not the person. _An enjoyable holiday can be a very lazy one._ And **monotonous** is stronger than **boring**: it says the same thing came back every single day.

## ⏳ Habit in the past (1) — `used to` + base form

> **Used to + base form is used for past habits or repeated actions in the past.**

_We **used to spend** August at my aunt's house in Tabarka._ The sentence says two things at once: it happened **again and again**, and it happens **no longer**. That double meaning is the whole point of the structure.

It also carries a **state** that was true and is true no more: _There **used to be** a small cinema behind the market._ · _My grandmother **used to have** very long hair._

Three forms to know:

| Form          | Pattern                    | Example                                             |
| ------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| affirmative   | `used to` + base form      | _People **used to** travel by train._               |
| negative      | `didn't use to` + base form | _I **didn't use to** read the newspaper._           |
| interrogative | `Did` … `use to` + base form | _**Did** you **use to** play in the street?_        |

> ⚠️ After **did** / **didn't**, `used` loses its **-d**: _didn't **use** to_, _Did you **use** to…?_ The helper already carries the past — marking it twice is the classic mistake.

> ⚠️ One finished event on a named date is **not** a habit: _In 1998 my parents **moved** to Sousse._ ✓ — "In 1998 my parents used to move to Sousse." ✗ Use the past simple.

## 🔁 Habit in the past (2) — `would` + base form, and where it stops

> **Would + base form is used for past habits or for describing typical behaviour in the past.**

_Every evening my grandmother **would sit** on the doorstep and tell us stories._ Here `would` and `used to` are interchangeable: the sentence paints a **repeated action**, a piece of typical behaviour.

**But `would` stops at states.** A state verb — _be, have, live, own, like, know_ — accepts `used to` and refuses `would`.

| Sentence                                  | `used to`             | `would`                 |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------------- |
| My uncle … own a small boat.              | used to own ✓         | would own ✗             |
| My uncle … repair his boat every winter.  | used to repair ✓      | would repair ✓          |
| We … live in Sfax.                        | used to live ✓        | would live ✗            |

> ⚠️ This is the trap of the lesson. Ask yourself one question before writing `would`: **is this an action that came back, or a situation that simply lasted?** A situation takes `used to` only.

## 🔀 Putting then and now side by side — the contrast linkers

Six linkers compare the two periods, and each one has its own place in the sentence.

| Linker                       | What follows it              | Example                                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **whereas**, **while**       | a whole clause, after a comma | _My father loved the farm, **whereas** I love the sea._          |
| **but**                      | a whole clause, after a comma | _It was cheap, **but** it was monotonous._                       |
| **however**, **in contrast** | a new sentence, then a comma | _It was cheap. **However**, it was monotonous._                  |
| **unlike**                   | a noun or a pronoun          | _**Unlike** my father, I spend my holidays abroad._              |

> ⚠️ **however** and **in contrast** cannot glue two clauses with a simple comma. Use a full stop or a semicolon: _It rained all week; **in contrast**, last year was sunny._ And never put a whole clause after **unlike**.

A model comparison sentence therefore looks like this: _Families **used to** rent the same house year after year; today, **however**, they book a different country each summer._

## ✉️ Recognising a text type from its format

Before reading a text word by word, look at its **shape**. The format alone tells you what kind of text it is, and a phrase inside it often tells you **who** is speaking to **whom**.

| Clue in the text                             | What it reveals                                  |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| a few lines on one small card, no envelope   | a postcard                                       |
| "Dear …", a signature, an address, an envelope | a personal letter                                |
| a headline, prices, an address, no reader named | an advertisement or a leaflet                    |
| "please tick the box below", "turn over"     | the text is **written**, never spoken            |
| "**our** doctors are on duty at night"       | the writer is the clinic; the reader is a patient |

> 🗡️ A possessive is a signature in disguise. _**Our** kitchen closes at ten_ can only be written by the hotel that owns the kitchen — so the readers are its guests.

## ⚖️ Parallelism — the same shape all the way through

> _The repetition of a form, a tense or a structure in a sentence is called **parallelism**._

_I came; I saw; I conquered._ Three clauses, one and the same shape. Parallelism makes a sentence easy to follow and pleasant to hear, and English expects it in every list.

- Same form in a list: _On the boat we ate, sang and slept._ ✓ — "we ate, sang and were sleeping" ✗
- Same form after `used to`: _We used to fish, mend the nets and sell the catch._
- Same form on both sides of **not only … but also**: _We came not only **to rest** but also **to discover**._ ✓ — "not only to rest but also discovering" ✗

> ⚠️ The break almost always happens on the **last** item of the list, when the writer forgets the form chosen at the start. Read your list back from the end.
