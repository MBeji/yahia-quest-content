# 📜 Summary: Holidaying

- **Reading a ranking table** — a ranking gives **positions**, not numbers of people: 1 is the favourite, and `3=` printed twice means two outings are equal and **share** third place, so nobody is fourth and the next position is fifth. Read **across** the columns (the same outing carries one rank per group), look for the **agreement** as well as the differences, and describe it with the verbs of ranking: _comes first_, _drops from second to fifth_, _climbs one place_, _are equally popular_. When the survey gives raw counts instead of positions, sort the figures from the biggest to the smallest and number them yourself.
- **Talking about your holidays** — open with _Where / When / How / What_, then judge: **exciting** (strong new emotions), **interesting** (you learned), **enjoyable** (pleasant), **relaxing** (it rested you), **boring** / **monotonous** (nothing happened / nothing ever changed), **active** / **lazy** (you moved a lot / very little), **cheap** / **expensive**, **plain** / **original**. Sporting activities make an **active holiday**, anything that asks nothing of your body a **lazy** one — and _lazy_ judges the holiday, not the person.
- **Habit in the past with `used to`** — `used to` + base form says the action came back **and** has now stopped (_We used to spend August at my aunt's house_); it also carries a **state** that is true no more (_There used to be a small cinema behind the market_). Negative **didn't use to**, question **Did you use to…?** — after _did_, `used` loses its **-d**, because the helper already carries the past.
- **`used to` is not the past simple** — one finished event on a named date takes the past simple: _In 1998 my parents **moved** to Sousse_, never "used to move".
- **Habit in the past with `would`** — `would` + base form works for **repeated actions and typical behaviour** (_Every evening my grandmother would sit on the doorstep_), but it **refuses states** — _be, live, own, know_, the verbs of feeling and opinion _like, hate, love, prefer, want_, and _have_ when it means **own**: _used to own_ ✓ / _would own_ ✗, _used to hate_ ✓ / _would hate_ ✗. (_have lunch_ names an action, so _we would have lunch_ is right.) Ask yourself: an action that came back, or a situation that simply lasted? In the negative, write **didn't use to** — _wouldn't_ can also mean **refused**.
- **The contrast linkers** — **whereas**, **while** and **but** join two clauses inside one sentence, after a comma; **however** and **in contrast** need a full stop or a semicolon before them and a comma after; **unlike** takes a **noun or a pronoun**, never a clause. Model: _Families used to rent the same house year after year; today, however, they book a different country each summer._
- **Recognising a text type** — the **format** answers first: a few lines on a card with no envelope = a postcard; "Dear …" + signature + envelope = a personal letter; a headline, prices and no named reader = an advertisement or a leaflet. A phrase inside settles the rest: "tick the box below", "turn over" or "enclosed with this letter" proves the text is **written**, and a possessive such as "**our** kitchen" names the writer and, with it, the reader.
- **Parallelism** — the repetition of the same form, tense or structure inside one sentence: _I came; I saw; I conquered._ Keep one form right through a list (_we ate, sang and slept_) and on both sides of **not only … but also** (_not only to rest but also to discover_). The break usually hides in the **last** item, so read your list back from the end.
