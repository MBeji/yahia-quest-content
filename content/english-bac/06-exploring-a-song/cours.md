# ⚔️ Exploring a Song — Immortality

> 💡 "A song is a text you cannot see. You get one hearing — so you had better know what you are listening for."

Everybody in the class likes music. Not everybody can say **why**, and that is the whole point of this lesson: turning what you hear into what you can say. You will learn to name what you like in a hit, to say **how much** you like it, to hear what a singer is feeling, and to tell the story of a song in your own words. The song of the lesson is **_Immortality_**, sung by **Celine Dion** and written by the Bee Gees — a song about what stays of us once we are gone. And because a song is heard and not read, two more things belong here: the **modals** a singer leans on to say what she can and must do, and the **vowels** that keep two words apart.

## 🎧 What there is to hear in a song

Four things — and you can like them one by one.

| What you listen to | What it is                                              |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **the singer**     | the voice, and the person behind the voice              |
| **the music**      | the instruments, the arrangement, the rhythm            |
| **the melody**     | the tune itself — what you hum once the words have gone |
| **the lyrics**     | the words that are sung                                 |

Two more words separate what a song **talks about** from what it **wants**:

- the **topic** is the story — a departure, a lost brother, a promise;
- the **message** is what the song asks you to believe or to do once it is over.

> 🗡️ To find the **message**, listen for the words that **come back**, listen to the **last lines**, and ask yourself what the singer wants. The message the lesson reads in _Immortality_ is that **we must do everything to fulfil our dreams**.

A song also carries **feelings**, and six of them are worth having ready: **sadness**, **hope**, **fear**, **determination**, **jealousy**, **pessimism**. In _Immortality_ the lesson hears **hope** and **determination**.

> 🗡️ Naming a feeling is only half the work. **Prove it** — point at the words that carry it. _She feels determination: she says she went back to the studio the morning after every refusal._ A feeling you cannot back up with a word of the song is a feeling you have invented.

> ⚠️ Before the music starts, **read the task and everything printed on the page**. You cannot search for a word you have never read, and a song will not wait for you. Then listen a first time for the whole picture, and a second time for the details.

## 💚 Saying what you like — and how much

English grades a taste as finely as a thermometer. Here is the ladder, from the coldest answer to the warmest.

| Expression                  | What it says      |
| --------------------------- | ----------------- |
| I **can't stand** it.       | I hate it.        |
| I **don't really like** it. | mild refusal      |
| I **quite like** it.        | mild approval     |
| I **like** it very much.    | plain, strong     |
| I'm **crazy about** it.     | the top of the ladder |

Three more strong ones sit beside the last rung and are used exactly like it: _I'm **fond of** old records_ · _I'm **keen on** live concerts_ · _I'm **mad about** her voice_.

> ⚠️ **of**, **on** and **about** are prepositions, so a verb after them takes the **-ing** form, never the infinitive: _I'm keen on **singing**_ ✓ — "I'm keen on to sing" ✗. After **like** and **love**, both are possible: _I love **singing**_ / _I love **to sing**_.

> ⚠️ **mad** and **crazy** lose their ordinary meaning inside these expressions: _He's crazy about motorbikes_ says he likes them enormously — there is no anger and no madness anywhere in the sentence.

> ⚠️ **quite like** is mild. Do not read it as enthusiasm because the word looks strong: _I quite like it_ is polite approval, one rung below _I like it very much_.

Four questions open a conversation about tastes, and each one calls for a different kind of answer:

| Question                            | What you answer with                             |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| What **type** of hits do you like?  | a kind of music — rap, rock, jazz, malouf        |
| What do you like **in** a hit?      | the singer, the music, the melody or the lyrics  |
| **Which song** is your favourite?   | a title                                          |
| **Why** do you like it?             | a reason — and there the ladder above comes in   |

## 🗝️ The words of the lesson

**From the singer's life story.** Celine Dion was born in Quebec, the youngest of fourteen children; she recorded a demo at twelve, and her manager mortgaged his own house to pay for her first record.

| Word                       | What it means                                                                   |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **stardom**                | the state of being famous as a singer or an actor                               |
| **superstar status**       | the level of importance of a very famous performer                              |
| **prowess**                | great skill at doing something                                                  |
| to **convene**             | to come together, to meet                                                       |
| to **mortgage** (a house)  | to give a bank the right to own your house for a time in exchange for a loan    |
| **renown**                 | fame, celebrity — the fact of being known                                       |

**From the song.**

| Word                   | What it means                                        |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **faith**              | what you believe in, and the trust you put in it     |
| **fate**               | what is meant to happen to you, whatever you do      |
| **immortality**        | the state of living for ever                         |
| **memory**             | what you keep of what is gone                        |
| **sorrow**             | deep sadness, especially after a loss                |
| **soul**               | the part of a person that is not the body            |
| **eternity**           | time without end                                     |
| to **fulfil** (a dream) | to make it come true                                 |

> ⚠️ **faith** and **fate** are separated by one single sound — and by everything else. Faith is what you **choose** to believe; fate is what you **cannot** choose. (British spelling **fulfil**, American **fulfill**; both give _fulfilling_.)

> 🗡️ **prowess** and **renown** are not the same thing at all: prowess is what you can **do**, renown is what people **know**. A singer can have the first for years without the second.

## 🔑 Modals: `can`, `can't`, `must`, `mustn't`

Three rules of form that never change, whichever modal you use:

1. the **base form** follows — no _to_, no _-ing_: _She **can sing** in four languages._
2. **no -s** in the third person: _He **can** play_ ✓ — "He cans play" ✗
3. **no do / does / did**: the question inverts (_**Can** I…?_, _**Must** we…?_) and the negative simply adds _not_ (_cannot / can't_, _must not / mustn't_).

Then each modal does its own work:

| Modal       | What it says                       | Example                                              |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **can**     | ability                            | She **can** hold a note for ten seconds.             |
| **can**     | possibility                        | You **can** hear the bass from the street.           |
| **can**     | permission — usually as a question | **Can** I borrow your headphones for a minute?       |
| **can't**   | inability, impossibility           | I **can't** hear the words: the music is too loud.   |
| **can't**   | permission refused                 | You **can't** film during the concert.               |
| **must**    | obligation, necessity              | We **must** leave now to hear the first song.        |
| **mustn't** | prohibition                        | You **mustn't** use a flash in the hall.             |

> ⚠️ **mustn't never means "it isn't necessary".** _You mustn't shout_ **forbids** you to shout. When shouting is merely unnecessary, English says _You **don't have to** shout, I can hear you._ Turning a ban into a "no need" is the mistake of this lesson.

> 🗡️ One rule, two ways of writing it. An **obligation** turns into a **prohibition** provided you forbid the **opposite** action: _You **must** show your ticket at the door_ = _You **mustn't** go in without your ticket._ Check the turn by asking what actually gets banned.

Put together, they make a role play — here, the box office of a concert hall:

> — Good evening. **Can** I have two tickets for the nine o'clock show, please?
> — You **can**, but you **must** pay in cash: the card machine is down.
> — Fine. **Can** we choose our seats?
> — Not tonight, I'm afraid. And you **mustn't** bring any drinks into the hall.

## 🔊 Minimal pairs, and two vowels

> Two words make a **minimal pair** when **one single sound** separates them and everything else is identical: _them_ / _then_ · _faith_ / _fate_ · _love_ / _luck_ · _storm_ / _store_.

That one sound carries the whole meaning, so the ear gets no second chance — which is exactly why a listening exercise puts such pairs side by side and asks you to circle the one you heard.

> ⚠️ Words that merely **sound close** are not minimal pairs. _history_ / _memory_ differ from their very first sound; _destination_ / _destiny_ part company after _desti-_; _may_ / _might_ change the vowel **and** add a final sound. Count the differences before you use the term.

The two vowels the lesson sorts are `[ou]` as in **home** (the dictionaries write /əʊ/) and `[ei]` as in **name** (/eɪ/). Neither has one spelling — that is the whole difficulty.

| Sound                | Usual spellings   | Words                                                       |
| -------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| `[ou]` as in **home** | o · oa · ow · o…e | s**o**, **o**nly, w**o**n't, r**oa**d, gr**ow**, foll**ow**, h**o**pe, s**ou**l |
| `[ei]` as in **name** | a…e · ai · ay · ei | f**a**te, m**a**kes, f**ai**th, w**ai**t, s**ay**, m**ay**, **ei**ght, w**ei**gh |

> ⚠️ The letters lie twice here. **ow** is `[ou]` in _grow_ and _follow_, but /aʊ/ in _now_, _how_, _down_. And **ea** goes three ways: /iː/ in _sea_ and _dream_, `[ei]` in _great_, _break_ and _steak_, and the short /e/ of _red_ in _bread_ and _head_. Read the letters as a **group**, never one by one — and when a group can go several ways, learn the word, not the rule.

Last exercise of the ear: **same sound or different?** _through_ – _choose_ (**same**, /uː/) · _journey_ – _eternity_ (**same**, the vowel of _her_) · _wind_ – _wild_ (**different**) · _vision_ – _joker_ (**different**).

## 🪄 Metaphor — and saying it again in your own words

> A **metaphor** speaks of one thing **as if it were another**, so that the two share their qualities and the description hits harder.

_Her **sunny** smile_ · _a **mountain** of homework_ · _time **flies**_. Nothing is compared with _like_ or _as_: the image simply takes the place of the thing. In _Immortality_, eternity is spoken of as a road that can be travelled — nobody packs a bag for it, so the journey is no journey at all: it stands for a life that does not stop at death. **That figure is a metaphor.**

To **paraphrase** is to say the same thing in your own words. Three moves, in this order:

1. replace the **image** by what it means;
2. replace words by **synonyms** — _renown_ → _fame_, _sorrow_ → _deep sadness_;
3. change the **structure** — a noun becomes a verb, a long phrase becomes a short clause.

_The whole family convened in front of the television set._ → **Everybody in the family came together in front of the television.** Same meaning, not one word borrowed.

> ⚠️ A paraphrase keeps **all** of the meaning and adds **nothing**. Drop "the whole family" and you have summarised; add "to watch the final" and you have invented. Say it again, say it all, say it differently — that is the test.

> 🏆 Gate cleared, hero. You can now say how much you like a song, hear the one sound that separates two words, and read a ban for what it is. Next quest: **Walking Tour** — where the language leaves the studio and takes to the streets.
