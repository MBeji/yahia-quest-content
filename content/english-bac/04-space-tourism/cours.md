# ⚔️ Space Tourism — The Holiday That Leaves the Planet

> 💡 "A tourist is somebody who pays to be somewhere else. The only question left is how far else can be."

The last lesson left you on a beach. This one puts a paying passenger in a spacesuit. In November 2006 a news report described a **face-off** between two companies — Space Adventures, in Virginia, and Virgin Galactic, in Britain — each trying to fly the **first Chinese space tourist**: one client, Jiang Feng, had paid about 100,000 dollars, another, whose name was never given, about 200,000. Their flights were **suborbital**: two and a half hours, up to the edge of space and back, from a new **spaceport** in New Mexico. Four passengers had already gone much further — into **orbit**, since 2001, for some twenty million dollars each.

That story gives this lesson everything it needs: **words** for a journey nobody used to imagine, a habit of reading called **using the definition**, the art of **saying a proverb in your own words** — and two pieces of grammar, the **verb + -ing** and the **negative prefixes**, plus the single trap the whole lesson is built around: `stop + -ing` against `stop + to`.

## 🚀 The words of space travel

Start with the word **space** itself. It builds a small family, and each member names something different.

| Word              | What it names                                                  |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a **spaceship**   | the craft itself                                                |
| a **shuttle**     | a craft that goes up and comes back, again and again            |
| a **spaceport**   | the **place** the craft takes off from and returns to           |
| space **travel**  | the activity in general — uncountable, no article                |
| a space **trip**  | one journey there and back                                      |
| a space **flight** | one crossing, one time in the air                              |

Two adjectives sort the journeys. A **suborbital** flight climbs to the edge of space, touches weightlessness for a few minutes and comes down — it never goes round the Earth. An **orbital** trip does go round, which is why it costs a hundred times more and lasts days instead of hours.

Then the eight words the article brought with it.

| Word              | Meaning                                                      |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| a **billionaire** | somebody who owns more than a thousand million dollars       |
| **well-to-do**    | rich, comfortably off                                        |
| a **face-off**    | a fight, an open argument between two sides                  |
| a **foothold**    | a first strong position from which you can make progress     |
| a **deal**        | an agreement, usually about money                            |
| **emerging**      | appearing, beginning to be known                             |
| **a couple of**   | two — informally, two or three                               |
| a **spaceport**   | the starting point of space flights                          |

> 🗡️ **foothold** and **face-off** are images before they are words. Nobody puts a foot anywhere and nobody stands face to face — a company *gains a foothold* in a market the way a climber gains one on a rock: it is the first hold that lets you go higher.

## 📖 Reading a definition

You will meet unknown words all year, and half of them will come with a definition attached — in a dictionary, in a glossary, in the margin of an exam paper. A definition is not a sentence like the others: it is **built**, and its first words already tell you a great deal.

> **A definition names the family first, then what makes this word different from the rest of the family.**

_billionaire (noun) — **a person who** owns more than a thousand million dollars._

The family is **a person**. Everything after "who" is what separates a billionaire from a millionaire, a client or a passenger. So the first two or three words answer the biggest question of all: **what kind of thing is this?**

| The definition opens with… | The word names…            | Example                          |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| _a place where…_           | a place                    | spaceport, terminal              |
| _a person who…_            | a person                   | billionaire, developer           |
| _the act of…_ , _the fact of…_ | an action or a state   | a face-off, an agreement         |
| _a position…_ , _an agreement…_ | an abstract thing     | a foothold, a deal               |

This gives you two moves, and they run in opposite directions.

- **From the definition to the word.** You are told "a place where travellers wait before a journey"; you look at four words and keep only the one that can be a place. A vehicle is out before you have thought about it.
- **From the word to the sentence.** You are told that a face-off is **a fight** between two sides; then _the face-off between the two companies lasted a year_ ✓, but "he put the face-off in his pocket" ✗ — an argument is not an object.

> ⚠️ A word often has more than one sense, and only the **surrounding sentence** chooses between them. In _they signed a deal_, a deal is an agreement; in _a great deal of money_, it is a quantity. Read the definition **against** the sentence, never on its own.

## 🗣️ Saying it in your own words — proverbs and idioms

To **paraphrase** is to say the same thing with other words. Not to translate, not to shorten, not to comment: to **keep the meaning and drop the shape**.

An **idiom** is a group of words whose meaning cannot be built from the words themselves.

| Idiom                | It does **not** mean          | It means                                          |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **to see the world** | to look at a map              | to travel widely, country after country           |
| **to do the sights** | to do some work or shopping   | to visit the famous places of a city              |

A **proverb** goes one step further: it hides its idea inside an **image**, and paraphrasing it means saying the idea and **letting the image go**.

| Proverb                                                            | The image           | The idea                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _He that travels far knows much._                                  | distance = knowledge | travelling teaches you more than staying at home                     |
| _He who does not travel does not know the value of men._           | judging people      | seeing the same few faces all your life leaves you unable to judge what people are worth |
| _The world is a book and those who do not travel read only a page._ | world = book        | one page tells you almost nothing about a book, and one country almost nothing about the Earth |

> ⚠️ The classic failure is to **keep the image and lose the idea**. "You should read one page a day of a good book" has borrowed the words of the proverb about the book and understood nothing of it. Ask yourself: *what is this really about?* Travel — not reading.

The same habit works on any expression an examiner underlines: _another first in the annals of spaceflight_ → something that happens for the very first time · _a mystery client_ → a customer whose name is not given · _to the edge of space_ → as far as the border of space, not beyond it.

## ➕ Verb + -ing — the verbs that never take "to"

> **Some verbs are followed directly by another verb in the -ing form. No _to_, no bare form: the -ing form is the name of the action.**

_The company **will begin taking** on passengers next year._ · _They **enjoyed watching** the launch._

Here are the eighteen of this lesson. Learn them in families rather than one by one.

| Family                | Verbs                                      | Example                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| liking and disliking  | enjoy, dislike, mind, miss                 | _Would you **mind closing** the door?_                       |
| saying                | admit, deny, suggest                       | _He **denied knowing** the price of the ticket._             |
| thinking              | consider, imagine, regret                  | _They are **considering selling** the shuttle._              |
| time                  | postpone, delay, finish, stop              | _They have **postponed opening** the new spaceport._         |
| keeping away from     | avoid, risk                                | _She **avoided answering** the journalist._                  |
| doing                 | involve, practise                          | _The trip **involves crossing** two deserts._                |

Eight of them are worth a line of their own, because their meaning decides which one you need.

- **admit** = say that you did it · **deny** = say that you did not
- **postpone** / **delay** = move the action to a later date
- **regret** = be sorry about something you have already done
- **miss** = feel the absence of something you no longer do
- **mind** = object to, find unpleasant
- **practise** = repeat an action in order to get better at it
- **involve** = have as a necessary part
- **risk** = accept the danger of

> ⚠️ **suggest** is the great trap of the list. "They suggested to visit the spaceport" ✗ — in English, _suggest_ takes the -ing form and nothing else: _suggested visiting_ ✓. The same goes for _avoid_, _deny_ and _mind_.

> 🗡️ A **preposition** obeys the same law: after _in_, _of_, _before_, _without_ and their friends, a verb wears -ing. _interested **in flying**_ · _instead **of paying**_ · _without **saying** a word_.

## 🔀 Verbs that accept both forms

A second, smaller group takes **either** the -ing form **or** _to_ + infinitive, and the sentence means the same either way.

| Verb                        | With -ing                             | With _to_                            |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| begin, start, continue      | _The countdown continued running._    | _The countdown continued to run._    |
| like, love, hate, prefer    | _We love scuba diving._               | _We love to scuba dive._             |
| can't stand, can't bear     | _He can't stand working late._        | _He can't stand to work late._       |
| cease, neglect, propose     | _They ceased providing the service._  | _They ceased to provide the service._ |

With these, choosing one form or the other is a matter of style, not of meaning. Which makes the next section all the more dangerous.

## ⛔ The trap of the lesson — `stop + -ing` against `stop + to`

**Stop** belongs to neither group. It accepts both forms, and the two sentences tell **opposite stories**.

| Sentence                                  | What the -ing / the _to_ does        | What happened                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| _The engineer **stopped talking** to the crew._ | -ing **names the activity that ends** | he was talking; he is talking no more                 |
| _The engineer **stopped to talk** to the crew._ | _to_ means **in order to**           | he was doing something else; he broke off so that he could talk |

Three questions settle any sentence of this kind:

1. **What was the person doing before?** With `stopped filming`, he was filming. With `stopped to film`, he was doing something else — walking, working, driving.
2. **What is he doing now?** With `stopped filming`, anything but filming. With `stopped to film`, he is filming.
3. **How many actions are there?** `stopped filming` names **one** — the one that ends. `stopped to film` names **two** — the interruption, then its purpose.

> ⚠️ Everything rests on the little word **to**. After _stop_, it does not belong to the verb: it means **in order to**, and it announces the **reason** for stopping. That is why the second action has not happened yet at the moment of stopping — it is about to.

> 🗡️ Test yourself on a pair before you write one: _She stopped eating_ (the meal is over) · _She stopped to eat_ (the meal is beginning).

## ➖ Negative prefixes

A **prefix** is a small piece added in **front** of a word to change its meaning. The negative prefixes turn a word into its opposite without touching the word itself.

| Prefix    | Example                          | Where it is used                                     |
| --------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **un-**   | uncertain, unpaid, unwelcome, unfit | the commonest of all, on adjectives and participles |
| **in-**   | inaccurate, ineffective          | on many adjectives of Latin origin                   |
| **im-**   | impatient, immature, imperfect   | before **m**, **p**, **b**                           |
| **il-**   | illogical, illegal, illiterate   | before **l**                                         |
| **ir-**   | irregular, irresistible          | before **r**                                         |
| **dis-**  | dishonest, disobey, discontinue  | often on **verbs** — it undoes the action            |
| **ab-**   | abnormal                         | away from what is normal                             |
| **non-**  | non-living, non-smoker, non-violent | absence, not opposite — a neutral word            |

Three things to hold on to.

**First, a real rule exists — but it covers only three of them.** `il-`, `im-` and `ir-` are the same prefix as `in-`, and it is the **first letter of the word** that chooses the spelling: **l** calls `il-`, **r** calls `ir-`, and **m**, **p** or **b** call `im-`. Nothing is dropped when they meet, which is why the word ends up with a double letter: _ir_ + _responsible_ = **irresponsible**, _il_ + _logical_ = **illogical**, _im_ + _possible_ = **impossible**.

**Second, between `un-` and `in-` there is no rule at all.** Each word keeps the prefix it has always had, and the ear is a bad guide: **unclear** but **inexpensive**, **unpleasant** but **inconvenient**, **unfit** but **ineffective**. When you hesitate, the dictionary decides.

**Third, a prefix is not only for adjectives.** It goes on a **verb** (_obey_ → **disobey**, _continue_ → **discontinue**), on a **noun** (_balance_ → **imbalance**, _smoker_ → **non-smoker**) and on a participle used as an adjective (_paid_ → **unpaid**, _planned_ → **unplanned**, _inhabited_ → **uninhabited**).

> ⚠️ **non-** is not a simple synonym of **un-**. _Non-living_ says a thing is **outside** the category of living things; _unliving_ would try to say it is the opposite of alive, which is not the same idea. Keep **non-** for an absence — a **non-smoker** is not the enemy of tobacco, only somebody who does not smoke.

## 🔢 Reading the figures

The article is full of numbers, and English writes two marks that many languages write the other way round.

| Mark        | Its job                        | Example       | Said aloud                                   |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | ------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| the **dot** | the **decimal point**          | 2.5 · 36.8    | two **point** five · thirty-six point eight  |
| the **comma** | separates the **thousands**  | 3,000,000     | three million                                |

So a **1.5-hour visit** lasts one hour and a half — one hour and thirty minutes, not one hour and five. And 1,795,489 is read _one million, seven hundred and ninety-five thousand, four hundred and eighty-nine_: the commas only cut the figure into groups of three so the eye can find its way.

> ⚠️ In French and in Arabic the two marks trade places. Reading "2,5" as an English decimal, or "3.000.000" as an English number, is the mistake to watch for in an exam text.

## 🔊 Pronunciation — the sound [ei]

[ei] is the sound of the **letter A** when you say the alphabet: _spAce_. It is a **double** vowel — it starts on one sound and slides towards the [i] of _see_ — and English writes it in at least five ways.

| Spelling      | Words                                    |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **a … e**     | space, place, take, safe, name           |
| **-ai-**      | paid, aim, main, remain                  |
| **-ay-**      | day, pay, away, delay                    |
| **-ei-**      | eight, weight, veil                      |
| **-ea-**      | great, break — only these two families   |
| **a** + **-tion**, **-ency** | station, nation, agency   |

And one word that belongs to no pattern: **they**.

> ⚠️ The letters lie more often than they tell the truth. The same **-ea-** gives [i:] in _reach_ and _sea_, and [e] in _bread_ and _head_. The same **-i…e** that looks so much like **-a…e** gives [ai] in _price_, _mile_ and _flight_. Say the word aloud and listen: does the vowel end like the letter **y** in _say_? Then it is [ei].
