# ⚔️ Past Tenses — Journey Through Time

> 💡 "Three tools for the past: the **simple past** tells a finished story, the **present perfect** brings the past into today, the **past progressive** shows an action while it was happening."

## 🏰 The simple past — a finished action

Think of your first morning at your new school. You woke up early, you walked into a new building and you met your new classmates. All of it happened, and all of it is over.

When you tell that story in English, the verbs cannot stay in the present. Which tense tells an action that is **completed** in the past?

::: definition
The **simple past** expresses a **completed action in the past**.

It often comes with an **indicator of the past**: _yesterday_, _last year_, _last month_, _two years ago_.

:::

| Present (every day)            | Simple past (finished)                    |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| All pupils **take** a test.    | All pupils **took** a test **last year**. |
| She **walks** to school.       | She **walked** to school **yesterday**.   |
| They **visit** their teachers. | They **visited** them **two months ago**. |

Read the table line by line: the only thing that changes is the verb, and the indicator on the right tells you why — the time is over.

::: exemple
Salma writes one sentence about her first day. Choose the tense, step by step:

1. Find the time: _last September_ → the moment is **finished**.
2. A finished moment asks for the **simple past** → _walk_ becomes _walked_.
3. The sentence: _Last September, Salma **walked** into her new school._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Yesterday I **visit** my grandmother._ ✗ — the word _yesterday_ is there, but the verb stayed in the present, as it often does in speech. The indicator of the past needs a past verb: _Yesterday I **visited** my grandmother._ ✓

::: propriete
A finished action in the past → **simple past**. Look for the indicators: _yesterday_, _last_ + time (_last week_, _last year_), time + _ago_ (_three days ago_), a past year (_in 2019_).

Edge case: the indicator is not always written. In a story that is clearly over (_My first day at school was fun._), the context alone asks for the simple past.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Two weeks ago, my class ___ (visit) the Bardo Museum."

---

_Two weeks ago, my class **visited** the Bardo Museum._ — _two weeks ago_ is an indicator of the past: the visit is finished, so the verb takes the **simple past** (_visit_ + _ed_).

:::

::: retenir
Simple past = a **completed** action in the past, often with _yesterday_, _last…_ or _… ago_.

:::

## ⚡ Regular and irregular verbs

Ali keeps a diary in English, and his teacher found these words in it: _I **haved** breakfast, I **eated** nothing, I **taked** the bus._ Each one looks logical: Ali added _-ed_ everywhere.

So why did the teacher underline all three? Which verbs take _-ed_, and which do not?

::: definition
A **regular verb** makes its simple past with **verb + ed**: _walk → walked_, _help → helped_.

An **irregular verb** does **not** follow the _-ed_ form: it has its own past form, to learn by heart: _write → wrote_, _go → went_.

:::

| Base verb | Simple past | Base verb | Simple past |
| --------- | ----------- | --------- | ----------- |
| have      | had         | take      | took        |
| eat       | ate         | see       | saw         |
| go        | went        | say       | said        |
| get       | got         | think     | thought     |
| wake up   | woke up     | know      | knew        |
| meet      | met         | leave     | left        |
| buy       | bought      | forget    | forgot      |
| begin     | began       | shake     | shook       |

Every verb of this table is irregular: none of them ends in _-ed_, and that is exactly why each one must be learned.

::: exemple
Correct Ali's sentence, one verb at a time: _I haved breakfast and I taked the bus, and I walkd to school._

1. _have_ is irregular → not _haved_ but **had**.
2. _take_ is irregular → not _taked_ but **took**.
3. _walk_ is regular → it takes the full ending **-ed**: **walked**, not _walkd_.

The sentence: _I **had** breakfast and I **took** the bus, and I **walked** to school._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I **eated** couscous._ ✗ — adding _-ed_ to every verb feels like a safe rule, but _eat_ is irregular. Its past is **ate**: _I **ate** couscous._ ✓

::: propriete
To write the simple past of a verb:

- irregular verb → its own form (_go → went_, _buy → bought_);
- regular verb → **verb + ed** (_start → started_);
- regular verb ending in **-e** → only **-d** (_change → changed_, _smile → smiled_, never _changeed_).

Edge case: **be** has two past forms — **was** with _I, he, she, it_, **were** with _you, we, they_.

:::

::: verifie
Write the simple past of the three verbs: _I (get) up late, I (miss) the bus and I (arrive) late._

---

_I **got** up late, I **missed** the bus and I **arrived** late._ — _get_ is irregular (**got**); _miss_ is regular (**missed**); _arrive_ ends in _-e_, so it only takes **-d** (**arrived**).

:::

::: retenir
Regular verbs: **verb + ed** (only **-d** after an **e**); irregular verbs have their own past form: **go → went**, **eat → ate**, **take → took**.

:::

## 🛡️ Questions and negatives with did

Your friend missed the first day. She wants to know about the new teacher, and you want to tell her that you did not meet him either.

In English, neither the question nor the answer works with the verb alone. What small word carries the past in a question and in a negative?

::: definition
**Question**: **Did** + subject + **base verb** + ?

**Negative**: subject + **did not (didn't)** + **base verb**.

:::

| Affirmative               | Question                          | Negative                         |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| She **met** the teacher.  | **Did** she **meet** the teacher? | She **didn't meet** the teacher. |
| They **passed** the test. | **Did** they **pass** the test?   | They **didn't pass** the test.   |

Compare the columns: the past moves from the verb to **did**, and the verb goes back to its base form — _met_ becomes _meet_, _passed_ becomes _pass_.

::: exemple
Turn _You met the new teacher._ into a question, step by step:

1. Put **Did** at the start → it carries the past for the whole sentence.
2. Then the subject → _Did you…_
3. The verb goes back to its **base form**, because the past is already in _did_ → _meet_, not _met_.

The question: _**Did** you **meet** the new teacher?_ And the negative answer: _I **didn't meet** him._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I **didn't prepared** my lesson._ ✗ — the past is marked twice, once in _did_ and once in _-ed_. English marks it only once: _I **didn't prepare** my lesson._ ✓

::: methode
To make a simple past question or negative:

1. Write **did** (question: before the subject; negative: _did not_ after it).
2. Put the verb in its **base form** — with a regular verb **and** with an irregular one (_Did you go?_, never _Did you went?_).

Edge case: with **be** there is no _did_ — _Was she late? / She wasn't late._

:::

::: verifie
Make the sentence negative: "Ali heard the alarm."

---

_Ali **didn't hear** the alarm._ — **didn't** carries the past, so the irregular verb goes back to its base form: _hear_, not _heard_.

:::

::: retenir
Question: **Did** + subject + base verb? — Negative: **didn't** + base verb. The past lives in _did_, never twice.

:::

## 🧪 The present perfect — the past that matters now

Three tourists talk about Tunisia. One says she has seen many countries in her life. Another says that the country has changed a lot. A third tells you the news: a new hotel has opened in the town.

None of them says **when** it happened — only that it is true **now**. Which tense speaks about the past without a finished time?

::: definition
The **present perfect** talks about past actions at an **indefinite time** — no exact time is given.

Form: **have / has** + **past participle**.

:::

| Subject             | have / has | past participle |
| ------------------- | ---------- | --------------- |
| I / you / we / they | **have**   | visited, eaten  |
| he / she / it       | **has**    | visited, eaten  |

For a regular verb, the past participle is the **-ed** form (_visit → visited_). An irregular verb often has a third form: _eat → ate → **eaten**_, _go → went → **gone**_, _write → wrote → **written**_, _see → saw → **seen**_, _take → took → **taken**_, _know → knew → **known**_, _do → did → **done**_. Some keep the past form: _send → sent → **sent**_, _make → made → **made**_. And a few go back to the base form: _come → came → **come**_, _become → became → **become**_.

::: exemple
Say that the tourist lived this experience, step by step: _she / visit / many countries_.

1. No time is given, and it is her life up to now → **present perfect**.
2. _she_ → **has** (not _have_).
3. _visit_ is regular → past participle **visited**.

The sentence: _She **has visited** many countries._ With a question about experience, add **ever**: _**Have** you **ever eaten** couscous?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _He **has went** to the beach._ ✗ — after _have / has_ comes the **past participle**, not the simple past. _go_ has a third form: _He **has gone** to the beach._ ✓

::: propriete
The present perfect is used for:

1. an **experience** (in a life, up to now) → _Have you ever stayed in a hotel?_
2. a **change** between the past and now → _The town **has become** bigger._
3. a piece of **news** → _The school **has decided** to open a library._

Edge case: with a regular verb the simple past and the participle look the same (_visited_). Only **have / has** tells you it is the present perfect.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "My uncle ___ (write) a book about Djerba." (No time is given — it is news.)

---

_My uncle **has written** a book about Djerba._ — no time is given, so it is the **present perfect**: _he_ takes **has**, and _write_ uses its past participle **written**, not _wrote_.

:::

::: retenir
Present perfect = **have / has + past participle**, for an experience, a change or a piece of news, with **no finished time**.

:::

## 🌟 Just, already, recently, lately

Sally's friend asks her to send an e-mail. Sally answers that it is done — a minute ago. Her brother adds that he has done his homework before dinner, earlier than planned.

Both want to say **how recent** or **how early** the action is, without giving a time. Which words do that, and where do they go?

::: definition
Four words often go with the present perfect: **just** (a very short time ago), **already** (earlier than expected), **recently** and **lately** (not long ago, in the last days or weeks).

:::

| Word         | Place in the sentence               | Example                                    |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **just**     | between have/has and the participle | Sally **has just sent** the e-mail.        |
| **already**  | between have/has and the participle | He **has already done** his homework.      |
| **recently** | after have/has, or at the end       | They **have recently visited** Tabarka.    |
| **lately**   | at the end                          | We **have seen** many tourists **lately**. |

The words of the first column never change the form: it stays **have / has + past participle**; they only say how close to now the action is.

::: exemple
Build the sentence with _just_: _Sally / send / an e-mail_.

1. _just_ says the action is very recent, with no time → **present perfect**.
2. _Sally_ = _she_ → **has**; _send_ → past participle **sent**.
3. _just_ goes **between** _has_ and the participle → _Sally **has just sent** an e-mail._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _She **just has sent** the e-mail._ ✗ — the word is put before the auxiliary, as with a simple verb. With the present perfect it goes between the two parts: _She **has just sent** the e-mail._ ✓

::: propriete
_just_ and _already_ sit **inside** the verb (have / has + **just / already** + participle); _lately_ closes the sentence.

Edge case: _recently_ can take either place — _They have **recently** visited Tabarka_ and _They have visited Tabarka **recently**_ are both correct.

:::

::: verifie
Put the words in order: _already / the film / have / seen / we_.

---

_We **have already seen** the film._ — _already_ goes **between** _have_ and the past participle _seen_, and the tense stays the present perfect.

:::

::: retenir
**just** and **already** go between have/has and the participle; **lately** goes at the end; **recently** at either place.

:::

## ⚔️ Present perfect or simple past?

_Tunisia has become a big tourist country._ — _Tunisia became independent in 1956._ Both sentences talk about the past, and both use the verb _become_.

Yet the first uses _has become_ and the second _became_. What decides between the two tenses?

::: definition
The present perfect is **never** used with a **finished time**. When the time of the action is given and over, use the **simple past**.

:::

| No finished time → present perfect | Finished time → simple past             |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| I **have visited** Carthage.       | I **visited** Carthage **last summer**. |
| She **has just left**.             | She **left** **ten minutes ago**.       |
| Have you **ever eaten** snails?    | Did you **eat** snails **yesterday**?   |

The left column gives no time at all; the right column gives a finished time — and that time word is what forces the simple past.

::: exemple
Choose the tense: _I (see) that film last week._

1. Look for a time: **last week** is there.
2. _last week_ is a **finished** time → the present perfect is impossible.
3. Simple past of the irregular verb _see_ → **saw**: _I **saw** that film last week._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I **have visited** Tabarka last year._ ✗ — the visit feels close and important, so the present perfect tempts you. But _last year_ is a finished time: _I **visited** Tabarka last year._ ✓

::: methode
To choose between the two tenses, find the time of the action:

1. a **finished time** is given (_yesterday_, _last…_, _… ago_, _in 1956_) → **simple past**;
2. **no time**, or _ever_, _just_, _already_, _recently_, _lately_ → **present perfect**.

Edge case: a question with _When…?_ asks for a finished time, so it takes the simple past — _When **did** you **visit** Carthage?_, never _When have you visited…?_

:::

::: verifie
Choose: "She ___ (win) a prize in 2022." / "She ___ (win) many prizes."

---

_She **won** a prize in 2022._ — _in 2022_ is a finished time, so **simple past**. _She **has won** many prizes._ — no time is given (her life up to now), so **present perfect**.

:::

::: retenir
**Finished time** (yesterday, last…, … ago, in 1956) → simple past; **no finished time** → present perfect.

:::

## 🎬 The past progressive — an action in progress

Last year, a group of pupils prepared a project together. At four o'clock, one was reading the subject, two were drawing a poster, and the teacher was helping them.

You are not telling what they finished — you are describing the scene **while it was going on**. Which tense shows an action in progress in the past?

::: definition
The **past progressive** (or past continuous) describes a **past action in progress**.

Form: **to be in the past (was / were)** + **verb + ing**.

:::

| Subject           | was / were | verb + ing           |
| ----------------- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| I / he / she / it | **was**    | reading the subject. |
| we / you / they   | **were**   | drawing a poster.    |

The only choice is in the middle column: **was** for one person (and _I_), **were** for _you_ and for several people; the _-ing_ verb stays the same for everybody.

::: exemple
Describe the teacher at four o'clock: _the teacher / help / the pupils_.

1. The help was **in progress** at that moment → **past progressive**.
2. _the teacher_ = _he_ or _she_ → **was**.
3. _help_ + **ing** → _helping_: _At four o'clock, the teacher **was helping** the pupils._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _The pupils **was studying** together._ ✗ — _was_ sounds right after a noun, but _the pupils_ are several people, like _they_. Say: _The pupils **were studying** together._ ✓

::: propriete
Past progressive = **was / were + verb + ing**: **was** with _I, he, she, it_ (and one person or thing), **were** with _you, we, they_ (and several).

Edge case: **you** always takes **were**, even for one person — _You **were** working hard._

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "At 8 p.m. yesterday, my brothers ___ (watch) a match."

---

_At 8 p.m. yesterday, my brothers **were watching** a match._ — the action was in progress at that moment, so **past progressive**; _my brothers_ are several (_they_), so **were** + _watching_.

:::

::: retenir
Past progressive = **was / were + verb + ing**, for an action **in progress** at a moment in the past.

:::

## 🚀 Going further (beyond the 9th-grade book)

This part is **not** in the 9th-grade programme: read it for curiosity, it is never tested in this chapter.

In stories you will often meet the two past tenses in one sentence, joined by **when** or **while**. The past progressive gives the long action in the background, and the simple past gives the short action that happens in the middle of it:

- _I **was reading** **when** the phone **rang**._
- _**While** we **were playing**, it **started** to rain._

You will study these sentences in secondary school.
