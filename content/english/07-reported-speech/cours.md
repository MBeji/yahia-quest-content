# 🧱 Word Building & Linking Words — The Forge of Sentences

> 💡 "Words are your weapons. This chapter teaches you to forge new ones from old ones — and to link them into sentences that hit hard."

## ❓ Question words — asking for the missing part

A new pupil, Omar, joins your class. You want to know everything about his family: who his brother is, where he lives, why he moved to your town. You already know the answers are short — _in Sousse_, _because his father works there_.

But the question itself: which small word opens it, and in what order do the other words come?

::: definition
A **question word** (a _wh-word_) opens a question and asks for **one missing part** of the answer.

:::

| The missing part is… | Question word | Example question               |
| -------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------ |
| a person             | **who**       | _Who is your English teacher?_ |
| a thing              | **what**      | _What are you reading?_        |
| a time               | **when**      | _When does the bus leave?_     |
| a place              | **where**     | _Where does your uncle work?_  |
| a manner (how?)      | **how**       | _How does Sara speak English?_ |
| a cause, a reason    | **why**       | _Why did you stay at home?_    |

Read the table from left to right: first find **what is missing**, then the word follows from it.

::: exemple
Answer: _Omar lives **in Sousse**._ Ask the question about the bold part, step by step:

1. _in Sousse_ is a **place** → the question word is **where**.
2. The verb _lives_ is in the simple present with _he_, so the helper is **does** → _Where does…_
3. The subject comes after the helper, and the verb goes back to its base form (the _-s_ now sits on _does_) → _Where does Omar **live**?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Where Omar lives?_ ✗ or _Where does Omar lives?_ ✗ — the first forgets the helper, because in speech we often ask with the voice only; the second keeps the _-s_ twice. Correct: _Where **does** Omar **live**?_ ✓

::: propriete
**Question word + helper (do / does / did) + subject + base verb?** — _Why did you stay at home?_

Edge case: when the verb is **be** (_is, are, was_), or when a helper is already there (_is reading_), you need no _do_: _Who **is** Mr Slim?_ — _What **is** Helen reading?_

:::

::: verifie
Answer: _Salma walked to school **because the bus was late**._ Ask about the bold part.

---

_**Why** did Salma walk to school?_ — the bold part is a **reason**, so the word is **why**; _walked_ is in the past, so the helper is **did**, and the verb goes back to its base form _walk_.

:::

::: retenir
Who (person), what (thing), when (time), where (place), how (manner), why (reason) + do / does / did + subject + base verb.

:::

## 🔑 Whose is it? — possessive adjectives, possessive pronouns and 's

The bell rings and a pencil case stays on the desk. The teacher lifts it: _Is this pencil case yours?_ — _No, it isn't mine. It's Sami's._

Three ways to name the owner, in one short dialogue: _yours_, _mine_, _Sami's_. When do you use which?

::: definition
A **possessive adjective** (my, your…) comes **before a noun**. A **possessive pronoun** (mine, yours…) **replaces** the noun and stands alone. The **genitive 's** gives the owner's **name**: _Sami's_.

:::

| Person | Possessive adjective | Possessive pronoun |
| ------ | -------------------- | ------------------ |
| I      | my book              | mine               |
| you    | your book            | yours              |
| he     | his book             | his                |
| she    | her book             | hers               |
| we     | our book             | ours               |
| they   | their book           | theirs             |
| it     | its name             | —                  |

With a name, the genitive works both ways: _This is **Sami's** pencil case_ → _This pencil case is **Sami's**._

::: exemple
The pencil case belongs to you. Say it in two ways, step by step:

1. Before the noun, you need the adjective → _This is **my** pencil case._
2. To avoid saying _pencil case_ twice, the noun disappears and the pronoun replaces it → _This pencil case is **mine**._
3. For Sami, the owner is a **name**, so add **'s** → _It's **Sami's**._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _This book is **my**._ ✗ — _my_ always needs its noun. Alone, say _mine_: _This book is **mine**._ ✓ And _hers, yours, ours, theirs_ never take an apostrophe (_her's_ ✗).

::: propriete
Adjective + noun (_our car_) · pronoun alone (_ours_) · name + **'s** (_my cousin's schoolbag_).

Edge case: a plural noun ending in **-s** only takes the apostrophe: _my teachers'_ advice (several teachers). And **its** is only an adjective: _The cat drinks **its** milk._ — never a pronoun alone, and never with an apostrophe (_it's_ means _it is_).

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Are these socks ___ (you)? — No, they are ___ (she). Look, they have ___ (Lina) name on them."

---

_Are these socks **yours**? — No, they are **hers**. Look, they have **Lina's** name on them._ — _yours_ and _hers_ stand alone without a noun, so they are pronouns; before the noun _name_, the owner Lina takes **'s**.

:::

::: retenir
my / your / his / her / its / our / their + noun · mine / yours / his / hers / ours / theirs alone · name + 's.

:::

## 🪞 Reflexive pronouns — when the action comes back

Every morning, Jamila stands for a long time in front of the mirror. She looks at her face, then at her hair — in fact, she looks at **Jamila**.

How do you say that the person who looks and the person you see are the **same person**?

::: definition
A **reflexive pronoun** ends in **-self** (singular) or **-selves** (plural). It refers back to the **subject** of the sentence.

:::

| Subject | Reflexive pronoun     |
| ------- | --------------------- |
| I       | myself                |
| you     | yourself / yourselves |
| he      | himself               |
| she     | herself               |
| it      | itself                |
| we      | ourselves             |
| they    | themselves            |

::: exemple
Build the sentence about Jamila, step by step:

1. The subject is _Jamila_, and the person she looks at is Jamila too → the object is the **same person** as the subject.
2. Jamila is _she_, so the reflexive pronoun is **herself**.
3. The sentence: _Jamila looks at **herself** in the mirror._ (_Jamila looks at her_ means another girl.)

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _He hurt **him** when he fell._ ✗ — it means he hurt another boy. When the subject and the object are the same person, use **-self**: _He hurt **himself**._ ✓ Also: _hisself_ and _theirselves_ ✗ do not exist — only **himself** and **themselves**.

::: propriete
Subject = object → reflexive pronoun: _We should only blame **ourselves**._

It also means **"with no help"**: _I made this box **myself**._ — _They did the project **by themselves**._

Edge case: **you** has two forms — **yourself** for one person, **yourselves** for several.

:::

::: verifie
The teacher speaks to the whole class: "Children, do the exercise ___, without your parents."

---

_Children, do the exercise **yourselves**._ — the teacher speaks to **several** pupils, so _you_ is plural and takes **-selves**; the pronoun means "with no help".

:::

::: retenir
myself, yourself / yourselves, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves: the action comes back to the subject — or means "with no help".

:::

## 👠 Compound adjectives — two words, one precise description

Your sister calls from her room: _Bring my shoes, please!_ You bring the first pair you see. _No, not these! The shoes with the high heels!_

Her description is long. English can pack it into **one** adjective — but how?

::: definition
A **compound adjective** joins two words with a **hyphen** (-). The first part gives more information about the second, so the description is more precise.

:::

| Pattern                      | Example      | It means…       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------ | --------------- |
| adjective - noun + **ed**    | high-heeled  | with high heels |
| adjective - verb + **ing**   | hard-working | who works hard  |
| adverb - **past participle** | well-made    | made well       |

::: exemple
Pack "a girl with long hair" into one adjective, step by step:

1. The description is _long_ (adjective) + _hair_ (noun) → pattern **adjective - noun + ed**.
2. Add **-ed** to the noun → _haired_.
3. Join with a hyphen and put it before the noun → _a **long-haired** girl_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _a long-hair girl_ ✗ — the noun part needs its **-ed**: _a **long-haired** girl_ ✓. And the order never follows your own language: _a hair-long girl_ ✗.

::: propriete
The compound adjective goes **before** the noun, and its parts never take a plural.

Edge case: a **number + noun** also makes a compound adjective, and the noun stays **singular**: a coin worth five dinars is _a **five-dinar** coin_ (not _five-dinars_).

:::

::: verifie
Pack each description into one adjective: "a pupil who works hard" — "a shirt with long sleeves".

---

_a **hard-working** pupil_ (adjective _hard_ + verb + **ing**) and _a **long-sleeved** shirt_ (adjective _long_ + noun _sleeve_ + **ed**) — each time, a hyphen joins the two parts, placed before the noun.

:::

::: retenir
adjective-noun**ed** (high-heeled) · adjective-verb**ing** (hard-working) · adverb-past participle (well-made) · number-noun, singular (a ten-dinar note).

:::

## 🏷️ Compound nouns — naming things with two words

In the garden, your grandfather points at his tools: a pot for flowers, a can for watering, a machine for cutting the lawn. Each one has a name made of **two words**.

Which word comes first, and which one names the thing itself?

::: definition
A **compound noun** is one noun made of two words. The **second** word names the thing; the **first** says what kind.

:::

| Pattern             | Examples                    |
| ------------------- | --------------------------- |
| noun + noun         | flower pot, school bus      |
| noun + **-er** noun | taxi driver, lawn mower     |
| gerund + noun       | driving licence, frying pan |
| noun + gerund       | wind surfing                |

::: exemple
Name "a person who plays tennis", step by step:

1. The thing itself is a person who plays → _player_ (verb + **-er**): it goes **second**.
2. The kind of player is _tennis_ → it goes **first**.
3. The compound noun: _a **tennis player**._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _a driver taxi_ ✗ or _a bus school_ ✗ — the order follows your own language, where the main word comes first. In English the main word is **last**: _a **taxi driver**_ ✓, _a **school bus**_ ✓.

::: propriete
First word = what kind, last word = the thing: a _school bus_ is a bus; a _bus school_ would be a school.

Edge case: in **gerund + noun**, the _-ing_ word tells what the thing is **for**: a _frying pan_ is a pan for frying, a _swimming pool_ is a pool for swimming.

:::

::: verifie
Name with a compound noun: "a pan you use to fry eggs" and "a brush for your teeth".

---

_a **frying pan**_ (gerund + noun: the pan is for frying) and _a **toothbrush**_ (noun + noun: the thing is a brush, _tooth_ says what kind) — each time the main word comes last.

:::

::: retenir
Main word last, kind first: flower pot, taxi driver, driving licence, wind surfing.

:::

## 🏊 The gerund — a verb that works as a noun

Your friend asks what you do on Sundays. You answer: _I like to swim._ Then you say: _Swimming is my favourite sport._

In the second sentence, _swimming_ is the **subject** — a verb doing the job of a noun. When does a verb take this **-ing** form?

::: definition
The **gerund** is the form **verb + ing** used as a noun: _swimming_, _reading_, _shopping_.

:::

| The gerund is used…                          | Example                          |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| as the subject                               | _**Skiing** can be dangerous._   |
| after like, enjoy, finish, hate, dislike     | _She **enjoys cooking**._        |
| after a preposition (before, after, at, by…) | _**After eating**, we went out._ |
| in a compound noun                           | _a **swimming** pool_            |

::: exemple
Complete: "Before ___ (go) out, Mona finished ___ (write) her homework."

1. _before_ is a **preposition** → it takes the gerund: _going_.
2. _finish_ is one of the verbs followed by the gerund → _writing_.
3. Spelling: _write_ ends in a silent **e**, which drops → _writing_, not _writeing_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Before **to go** out…_ ✗ and _I enjoy **to swim**._ ✗ — both copy the structure of French (_avant de partir_) or Arabic. In English, a preposition and _enjoy_ take the gerund: _Before **going** out…_ ✓, _I enjoy **swimming**._ ✓

::: methode
Spelling of the -ing form:

- a short verb ending in one vowel + one consonant → **double** the consonant: shop → shopping, swim → swimming (also _begin → beginning_, stressed at the end);
- a silent **e** at the end → **drop** it: shine → shining, smoke → smoking;
- all other verbs → **no change**: wait → waiting, think → thinking, do → doing, fly → flying.

Edge case: two vowels before the last consonant → no doubling (_rain → raining_, _wait → waiting_); and **w**, **x**, **y** are never doubled (_play → playing_).

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "___ (run) is good for your health, and my brother hates ___ (stop) before the end."

---

_**Running** is good for your health, and my brother hates **stopping** before the end._ — _running_ is the subject and _stopping_ follows _hate_, so both are gerunds; _run_ and _stop_ end with one vowel + one consonant, so the consonant doubles.

:::

::: retenir
Gerund = verb + ing: subject, after like / enjoy / finish / hate / dislike, after a preposition — double, drop the e, or no change.

:::

## 🔗 Cause and consequence — because, so, therefore, as a result, that's why

Two facts about your town: _Factories release fumes into the air._ — _The air is polluted._ They are true, but written like this they are two separate pieces.

One fact **causes** the other. Which small word shows it — and which fact does it stand in front of?

::: definition
A **linker** connects two parts of a sentence. **Because** introduces the **cause**. **So**, **therefore**, **as a result** and **that's why** introduce the **consequence**.

:::

| Linker          | It introduces… | Example                                                       |
| --------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **because**     | the cause      | _The air is polluted **because** factories release fumes._    |
| **so**          | the result     | _Ships spill oil, **so** fish die._                           |
| **therefore**   | the result     | _The water is dirty. **Therefore**, it is not safe._          |
| **as a result** | the result     | _Farmers use pesticides. **As a result**, food is unsafe._    |
| **that's why**  | the result     | _School is our second home. **That's why** we keep it clean._ |

::: exemple
Join: _It didn't rain._ + _The trees in the garden died._ — step by step:

1. Find the cause: no rain made the trees die → _It didn't rain_ is the **cause**, _the trees died_ is the **consequence**.
2. To start with the cause, choose a consequence linker → _It didn't rain, **so** the trees died._
3. To start with the consequence, put **because** before the cause → _The trees died **because** it didn't rain._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _**Because** I was ill, **so** I stayed at home._ ✗ — Arabic can put a word for _because_ and a word for _so_ in the same sentence, but English uses **one** linker only: _I was ill, **so** I stayed at home._ ✓ or _I stayed at home **because** I was ill._ ✓

::: propriete
because + **cause** · so / therefore / as a result / that's why + **consequence** — one linker per link.

Edge case: _It didn't rain **because** the trees died_ is grammatical but **false**: _because_ always stands in front of the cause, so check the meaning, not only the form.

:::

::: verifie
Join with a consequence linker: _He ate too many sweets._ + _He got a toothache._

---

_He ate too many sweets. **As a result**, he got a toothache._ (or _so_, _therefore_, _that's why_) — the sweets are the **cause**, the toothache the **consequence**, so the linker stands in front of the consequence. With _because_, reverse it: _He got a toothache **because** he ate too many sweets._

:::

::: retenir
because → the cause · so, therefore, as a result, that's why → the consequence — never two linkers for one link.

:::

## ⚖️ Contrast — while and whereas

Two pets live in your street: a big dog and an old cat. Dog bites happen more often; cat bites are rarer, but more serious.

The two facts point in **opposite** directions. Which word joins them and shows the contrast?

::: definition
**While** and **whereas** express **contrast**: they join two ideas that are **opposites**.

:::

| Linker      | Where it goes                              | Example                                                     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **whereas** | between the two ideas                      | _My father is lenient **whereas** my mother is severe._     |
| **while**   | between them, or at the start with a comma | _**While** bulldogs are dangerous, tortoises are peaceful._ |

::: exemple
Join: _my brother — quick-tempered_ / _my sister — patient_, step by step:

1. _quick-tempered_ and _patient_ are **opposites** → this is a contrast, so use **whereas** or **while**.
2. With **whereas**, keep the order and put it in the middle → _My brother is quick-tempered **whereas** my sister is patient._
3. With **while** at the start, add a comma after the first idea → _**While** my brother is quick-tempered, my sister is patient._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _My brother is kind **whereas** my sister is kind too._ ✗ — the two ideas are the **same**, so there is no contrast. Use **and**: _My brother is kind **and** my sister is kind too._ ✓

::: propriete
while / whereas + two **opposite** ideas — check the meaning first, then place the linker.

Edge case: a **gerund** can be the subject of each idea: _**While** obeying your parents is useful, disobeying them causes problems._

:::

::: verifie
Join with **while** at the start: _Playing video games from time to time — relaxing_ / _addiction to them — harmful_.

---

_**While** playing video games from time to time is relaxing, addiction to them is harmful._ — _relaxing_ and _harmful_ are opposites, so **while** fits; at the start of the sentence, a comma separates the two ideas.

:::

::: retenir
while / whereas join two opposite ideas — while can open the sentence, followed by a comma.

:::
