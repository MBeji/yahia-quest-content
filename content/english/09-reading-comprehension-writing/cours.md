# 📖 Reading Comprehension & Writing — Read Like a Detective, Write Like a Guide

> 💡 "Every lesson of your book has the same two gates: a text to understand and a few lines to write. Learn the moves once, and every exam text opens for you."

## 🏰 Reading for gist — what is the whole text about?

Your teacher gives you a short article at the start of the lesson on pollution. It speaks about a beach after a summer weekend, about bottles and bags on the sand, and about the visitors who leave them there.

The bell rings in five minutes, and the first question is: _What is the text about?_ You have no time to read every word twice. So how do you find the idea of the whole text?

::: definition
The **gist** is the general idea of the whole text, said in one short sentence. **Reading for gist** means reading fast — the title, the first and the last sentences, the words that come back — to find that idea before any detail.

:::

> _On Monday morning, the beach of our town looks sad. Plastic bottles, bags and cans lie on the sand. Seagulls pick at the rubbish, and every wave brings more of it. Most of this waste comes from visitors who leave it behind after their picnics. A clean beach is everybody's job, but on Monday nobody seems to remember it._

| Proposed idea                                   | Does every sentence support it?      |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Seagulls pick at the rubbish on the beach.      | No — it is one sentence: a detail    |
| The beach is a nice place for a summer picnic.  | No — the text never says it          |
| Visitors leave waste on the beach and spoil it. | Yes — every sentence is part of it ✓ |

Read the right-hand column: only the last idea gets a "yes". That is the test of a gist — the whole text stands behind it, not one line.

::: exemple
Find the gist of the beach text, step by step:

1. Read the first and the last sentences → the beach "looks sad" and "nobody seems to remember" to clean it: the text is about a **problem**.
2. Look for the words that come back → _bottles, bags, cans, rubbish, waste_ all name the same thing: the problem is **waste**.
3. Look for the cause → "visitors who leave it behind": the waste comes from **people**.
4. Say it in one sentence → _Visitors leave waste on the beach and spoil it._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "The text is about seagulls that eat rubbish." ✗ — it tempts you because the sentence is true and easy to remember. But a true detail is not the gist: it covers one sentence, not the text. Test every proposal with one question: _does every sentence support it?_ ✓

::: methode To find the gist

1. Read the title, the first and the last sentences.
2. Note the words that come back: they show the topic.
3. Build one short sentence that the **whole** text supports.

Edge case: the gist is often written **nowhere** in the text — no sentence gives it to you, and you build it from the clues, as with the beach. The same fast look also gives the **type of text**: a letter, an interview, a dialogue, a newspaper article.

:::

::: verifie
_Every Saturday, Amine and his friends visit the old people's home near their school. They read the newspaper to the residents, play cards with them and help the nurses at lunch. The residents wait for Saturday all week._ What is the gist?

---

**A group of teenagers regularly helps and cheers up old people.** Every sentence supports it: the visits, the three activities, the residents' long wait. "Amine plays cards" or "the nurses need help" are true, but each one is only a detail of one sentence.

:::

::: retenir
The **gist** is the idea the whole text supports — never a single detail, even a true one.

:::

## 🔍 Scanning — finding one precise detail

Rim's club has a short text in the school magazine. Your activity book asks you to correct a false statement: _The club started in 2014._

You do not need the whole text again: you need one number. How can you find it in ten seconds?

> _Rim, 15, lives in Sfax. At the age of twelve, she joined "Green Hands". Two biology teachers started this club in 2016. Today the club has forty members. Every month, they plant trees in the schoolyards of the town, and in spring they clean the beach._

::: definition
**Scanning** is moving your eyes fast over a text to find **one precise piece of information** — a name, a number, a date, a place — without reading the rest.

:::

| The question asks…       | You scan for…              | In Rim's text |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | ------------- |
| When did the club start? | a date — a year            | 2016          |
| How many members?        | a number                   | forty         |
| Where does Rim live?     | a place — a capital letter | Sfax          |
| How often do they plant? | a frequency                | every month   |

The question word tells you the **shape** of the answer before you look: _when_ → a date, _how many_ → a number, _where_ → a place with a capital letter.

::: exemple
Correct the false statement _The club started in 2014_, step by step:

1. The key word is _started_, and the answer is a year → scan for a four-figure number.
2. Only one year appears, 2016 → read its **whole** sentence: "Two biology teachers started this club in 2016".
3. Correct with the text's own words → _The club started in 2016, not in 2014._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "The club has twelve members." ✗ — your eyes stop on the first number they meet, and _twelve_ comes first. Read the whole sentence around it: "At the age of twelve, she joined" — twelve is **Rim's age**. The club has **forty** members. ✓

::: methode To scan

1. Find the key word of the question and decide the shape of the answer.
2. Move fast through the text until you meet that shape.
3. Read the whole sentence, then answer with the text's words.

Edge case: the text does not always repeat the question's word. For _How often do they plant trees?_ no sentence says "often": you scan for the **idea** — a frequency — and find _every month_.

:::

::: verifie
Correct the false statement: _The members clean the beach in summer._

---

**They clean the beach in spring.** The key idea is a season, so you scan for one: the only season in the text is in "in spring they clean the beach". You answer with the text's own words, not with a guess about summer.

:::

::: retenir
**Scan**: key word → shape of the answer → the whole sentence → the text's own words.

:::

## 📊 Information transfer — from the text to a table, and back

Three people talk about their free time in your book, and under their words stands an empty table: _Name, Age, Pastime_.

Three people, nine empty boxes. How do you fill them without copying the whole text?

> **Hedi, 16**: "I spend my Sunday afternoons playing football at the stadium. It is my passion."
>
> **Leila, 41**: "After work, I read novels on my balcony. It relaxes me."
>
> **Walid, 67**: "I don't use the internet. I prefer the radio and my newspaper."

::: definition
**Information transfer** is moving facts from a text into a table — or from a table into a text — with each fact in the box of its category, in a few words.

:::

| Name  | Age | Pastime                                   |
| ----- | --- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Hedi  | 16  | playing football at the stadium           |
| Leila | 41  | reading novels                            |
| Walid | 67  | listening to the radio, reading the paper |

Each column is a question you ask every speaker: _who? how old? what for fun?_ Each box keeps only the answer — a name, a number, an _-ing_ form.

::: exemple
Fill Walid's row, step by step:

1. **Name** → _Walid_, the name before his words.
2. **Age** → _67_, the number next to his name.
3. **Pastime** → he prefers "the radio and my newspaper": a pastime is an activity, so write it as an _-ing_ form → _listening to the radio, reading the paper_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: writing _He doesn't use the internet_ in Walid's Pastime box. ✗ — it tempts you because it is true and it is in his words. But it says what he does **not** do: the box asks for his pastime. Each box takes only the answer to its own column. ✓

::: propriete
One fact per box, short, in the right column — a name, a number, a noun or an _-ing_ form, never a whole sentence.

Edge case: the transfer also works **backwards**. To write a **biography** from a table of notes, each row becomes one sentence in the **simple past**, in the order of the years: _2015 — join the Red Crescent_ → _In 2015, Sami joined the Red Crescent._

:::

::: verifie
Turn this row of Sami's notes into a sentence: _2018 — organise a blood donation day at his school_.

---

**In 2018, Sami organised a blood donation day at his school.** 2018 is a finished time, so the verb takes the **simple past** (a regular verb: _organise_ + _d_), and _in_ + the year opens the sentence.

:::

::: retenir
Text → table: one short fact per box. Table → text: one sentence per row, simple past, in time order.

:::

## 🔮 Inference — reading between the lines

Your book tells the story of a boy's first day at a new school. Read it once, slowly:

> _On the first morning at his new school, Yassine held his mother's hand very tightly at the gate. He did not say a word. He looked at the big building for a long time. Then he asked: "Can we come back tomorrow instead?"_

The text never uses the word _afraid_, and yet every reader feels it. So how can you say something the writer never wrote, and still be sure you are right?

::: definition
An **inference** is a conclusion the text does not state but its **clues** prove. It answers questions such as _How does he feel?_, _Who are the speakers?_, _Where are they?_

:::

| Clue in the text                     | What it shows                |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| holds his mother's hand very tightly | he needs comfort             |
| does not say a word                  | he is tense, not joyful      |
| "Can we come back tomorrow instead?" | he wants to avoid the school |

Each line of the table is one clue; the inference is the point that **all** the clues share: Yassine is nervous about his new school.

::: exemple
How does Yassine feel? Step by step:

1. Collect the clues → the tight hand, the silence, the wish to come back another day.
2. Test each possible answer against them → _happy_? no clue shows joy. _Nervous_? every clue fits.
3. Answer and prove → _He was nervous: he held his mother's hand tightly and asked to come back another day._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "Yassine is ill." ✗ — it tempts you because a child who wants to go home is sometimes sick. But no clue in the text speaks of pain or fever: that is a guess, not an inference. Keep only what a clue proves. ✓

::: propriete
An inference = the clues + logic. If you cannot point to the clue, it is not an inference.

Edge case: in a dialogue you infer the **situation** — who speaks, and where — from key words. _"Window seat or aisle seat, sir?" — "Aisle, please." — "Your flight boards at gate 4."_ → an airport, a passenger and an airline employee — and no line says "airport".

:::

::: verifie
_A: "Good afternoon, Hotel Carthage, reception." B: "Good afternoon. I'd like a room for two nights, please. I arrive on Friday."_ Who are the speakers, and are they face to face?

---

**A hotel receptionist and a future guest, on the phone.** "Hotel Carthage, reception" is how a receptionist answers a call, and "I arrive on Friday" proves the guest is not at the hotel yet. No line says "phone": the clues prove it.

:::

::: retenir
**Inference**: the text does not say it, the clues prove it — always point to the clue.

:::

## 🧭 Reference words — who is "he", what is "it"?

The story of a cartoon, told in five sentences, comes with a question under it:

> _A young turtle, Tiki, does not listen to his mother's warnings and swims far from the beach. Soon, a fisherman's net catches him. His mother and her friend Kiko, an old crab, start looking for him. The two friends cross the whole bay. Meanwhile, Tiki meets other prisoners in the net, and they plan to escape together._

Your book asks: _What does "him" refer to in the second sentence?_ Two male nouns stand before it — Tiki and the fisherman. Which one does the writer mean, and how can you be sure?

::: definition
A **reference word** points back to a person, a thing or an idea already named: the pronouns (_he, she, it, they, him, them, his_), words like _this, that_, a phrase like _the two friends_, and the relative pronouns _who, which, that_.

:::

| Reference word       | Points back to               | Why                                    |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| him (sentence 2)     | Tiki                         | one male — the one who swims too far   |
| the two friends      | Tiki's mother and Kiko       | two people, called friends just before |
| they (last sentence) | Tiki and the other prisoners | a group — the ones inside the net      |

Each reference word **agrees** with what it replaces: one or many, a person or a thing. Then put the noun back: the sentence must still make sense.

::: exemple
What does _him_ refer to in "a fisherman's net catches him"? Step by step:

1. _him_ is one male → the candidates are _Tiki_ and _the fisherman_.
2. Put each one back → "a fisherman's net catches **the fisherman**" is strange: a fisherman does not catch himself. "A fisherman's net catches **Tiki**" is natural.
3. Check with the story → Tiki swims far from the beach, so he is the one in danger → _him_ = **Tiki**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "_him_ = the fisherman." ✗ — it tempts you because _fisherman_ is the nearest male noun. The nearest noun is only a first guess: put it back in the sentence, and the meaning decides. ✓

::: propriete
A reference word agrees in number (_it_ / _they_) and in kind (_he, she_ for people, _it_ for things), and it must make sense in place of the word it replaces.

Edge case: _this_, _that_ or _that's why_ often point to a whole **idea**, not to one noun: _Tiki does not listen to his mother. That's why he is in danger._ → _that_ = the fact that he does not listen.

:::

::: verifie
_Nadia's parents gave her a puppy for her birthday. She takes care of it every day, and it follows her everywhere. This makes her parents proud._ What do "it" and "This" refer to?

---

**it = the puppy; This = the fact that Nadia takes care of the puppy every day.** _it_ is one animal that follows her, so it replaces the puppy. _This_ makes the parents proud, and no single noun can do that: it points to the whole idea before it.

:::

::: retenir
Find the word it replaces, check number and kind, put it back — the meaning decides.

:::

## 📚 Word meaning from context

A short text about safety at home warns parents about pets:

> _A dog that spends all day on a chain often feels lonely, and this isolation can make it aggressive. Bites usually occur in the family home, so parents should supervise their young children near any dog._

Your book asks: _Find in the text a word that means "happen"._ You know the word _happen_; the text has several hard words. Where do you start looking?

::: definition
**Guessing meaning from context** means using the words around a word — its sentence, the words that explain it, the kind of word it is — to find its meaning, or to find its synonym in the text.

:::

| Meaning asked | Kind of word | Word in the text | Clue around it                          |
| ------------- | ------------ | ---------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| loneliness    | a noun       | isolation        | "this isolation" repeats "feels lonely" |
| happen        | a verb       | occur            | bites happen in the home                |
| watch         | a verb       | supervise        | parents watch children near a dog       |

The second column is the key: a noun replaces a noun, a verb replaces a verb. Then the clue around the word confirms the meaning.

::: exemple
Find the word that means _happen_, step by step:

1. _happen_ is a verb → list the verbs of the text: _spends, feels, can make, occur, should supervise_.
2. Put _happen_ in their place → "Bites usually **happen** in the family home" makes sense; "parents should **happen** their children" does not.
3. Answer → **occur**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: for "loneliness", answering _lonely_. ✗ — it tempts you because the two words share the same root. But _lonely_ is an adjective, and the question asks for a noun: the right word is **isolation**. ✓

::: propriete
Look for the same kind of word, then test it: it must replace the meaning without breaking the sentence.

Edge cases: a contrast word gives the **opposite** — _Our old flat was tiny, whereas the new one is spacious_ → _spacious_ means big. The **parts** of a word help too: _tire + less + ly_ → _tirelessly_ means without getting tired. And an expression can mean more than its words: _the hand that holds it_ means the person who uses it.

:::

::: verifie
_The rules of the park are strict: visitors must not feed the animals, and offenders pay a fine of fifty dinars._ What does "offenders" most probably mean?

---

**People who break the rules.** It is a noun for people (they pay a fine), and it comes right after the rules: the ones who pay are the visitors who do not respect them.

:::

::: retenir
Same kind of word + the clue around it + the replacement test = the meaning.

:::

## ✍️ The guided paragraph — one idea, three parts

Your teacher writes on the board: _Is team sport good for teenagers? Write a paragraph of five or six lines._ Ideas come fast: fitness, friends, your brother's video games, a match last Saturday.

You cannot write them all. Which ideas do you keep, and in what order?

::: definition
A **paragraph** develops **one main idea** in three parts: the **topic sentence** announces it, the **supporting sentences** prove it with reasons or examples, and the **closing sentence** sums it up. In an **opinion** paragraph, the topic sentence starts with _I think that_, _I believe that_ or _In my opinion_.

:::

| Part                 | Sentence                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| topic sentence       | In my opinion, team sport is very good for teenagers.                     |
| supporting sentences | It keeps them fit. Players learn to cooperate, because nobody wins alone. |
| closing sentence     | That's why I think that every teenager should join a team.                |

The first line gives the idea, the middle lines prove it, the last line brings the reader back to it.

::: exemple
Build the paragraph, step by step:

1. Choose the topic sentence → _In my opinion, team sport is very good for teenagers_, not _I scored a goal last Saturday_: a topic sentence must cover the whole paragraph, not one event.
2. Keep only the ideas that prove it → fitness and cooperation stay; your brother's video games go, because they prove nothing about team sport.
3. Close on the idea → _That's why I think that every teenager should join a team._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "Team sport keeps you fit. **My brother prefers video games.** Players learn to cooperate." ✗ — the middle sentence tempts you because it speaks about free time too. But it does not support the topic sentence: it is **irrelevant** and breaks the unity. Remove it. ✓

::: methode To check a paragraph

1. Underline the topic sentence: is it general enough to cover everything?
2. For each other sentence, ask: _does it support the topic sentence?_ If not, remove it.
3. Check that the last sentence brings the reader back to the idea.

Edge case: to put mixed sentences in order, start with the most **general** one — that is the topic sentence, even if the book lists it last. Then follow the linkers: _First_ comes before _Then_, and _As a result_ or _That's why_ come after what they sum up.

:::

::: verifie
_Reading is a useful habit. It enriches your vocabulary. The library closes at five. It also helps you relax after school._ Which sentence breaks the unity, and what could the closing sentence be?

---

**"The library closes at five" breaks the unity**: it gives a timetable, and it proves nothing about reading being useful. A closing sentence brings the reader back to the idea: _That's why I believe that everyone should read a little every day._

:::

::: retenir
One idea: the **topic sentence** announces it, **supporting sentences** prove it, the **closing sentence** sums it up.

:::

## 🔗 Linking ideas — cause, consequence, contrast, order

Your draft about pollution has two short sentences: _Farmers spray pesticides on vegetables. Many people get stomachaches._

The reader sees two facts, but not the link between them. Which small word turns the two facts into one idea?

::: definition
A **linker** (a linking word) shows the relation between two ideas: a cause, a consequence, a contrast, an addition or an order in time.

:::

| Relation    | Linkers                                | Example                                             |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| cause       | because                                | We stayed in because it was raining.                |
| consequence | so, therefore, as a result, that's why | It was raining, so we stayed in.                    |
| contrast    | but, while, whereas                    | Salah is talkative, whereas his twin is quiet.      |
| addition    | and, also                              | Walking is free. It is also good for the heart.     |
| order       | first, then, finally                   | First, sort the rubbish. Then, take it to the bins. |

The linker is the arrow between the two ideas: _because_ points back to the cause, _so_ points forward to the consequence.

::: exemple
Join _Farmers spray pesticides on vegetables_ and _Many people get stomachaches_, step by step:

1. Which fact comes first in real life? → the spraying is the **cause**, the stomachaches are the **consequence**.
2. A consequence linker goes before the consequence → _Farmers spray pesticides on vegetables. As a result, many people get stomachaches._
3. Or put the consequence first and open the cause with **because** → _Many people get stomachaches because farmers spray pesticides on vegetables._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "Many people get stomachaches, **so** farmers spray pesticides." ✗ — it tempts you because _so_ and _because_ both "explain". But _so_ opens the **consequence**: this sentence says the stomachaches cause the spraying. Keep **because** for the cause and **so** for the consequence. ✓

::: propriete
**because** + the cause · **so, therefore, as a result, that's why** + the consequence · **but, while, whereas** + an opposite idea · **first, then, finally** put steps in order.

Edge case: _while_ and _whereas_ join two **opposite** ideas, never two similar ones — _While buses are cheap, taxis are expensive_ ✓, but not _While buses are cheap, trains are cheap_. At the start of a sentence, _while_ takes a comma after its first idea.

:::

::: verifie
Complete with one linker each: "The bus was full, ___ we walked to school." — "Plastic bags are cheap, ___ they pollute the sea."

---

**so** — then **but**. Walking to school is the **consequence** of the full bus, so a consequence linker opens it. Cheap and polluting pull in **opposite** directions, so the second sentence needs a contrast linker: _but_.

:::

::: retenir
**because** → cause · **so / therefore / as a result / that's why** → consequence · **but / while / whereas** → contrast.

:::
