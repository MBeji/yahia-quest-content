# ⚔️ Present Tenses — Your First Weapon

> 🔁 **Review from 7th–8th grade** — you met the present simple and the present continuous in 7th and 8th grade, and your 9th-grade book does not teach them again: it **uses** them on every page, to describe a family in Module 1, to ask about your habits, to tell what is happening in a dialogue. This chapter is a solid review, so that the rest of the year stands on firm ground.

> 💡 "Choose your tense like a weapon: the **simple** for what is always or usually true, the **continuous** for what is happening right now."

## 🏰 The present simple — habits and facts

Module 1 opens on teenagers who describe their families. Try it with yours: your father works in a bank, your mother teaches at a primary school, your little brother goes to school by bus. None of this is true only today — it is true every day.

Which tense tells what is **always or usually** true — and what happens to the verb after _he_ or _she_?

::: definition
The **present simple** expresses **habits and routines**, and **facts and general truths**.

Form: subject + **base verb** — and with **he / she / it** the verb takes an **-s**.

:::

| Subject             | Verb       | Spelling of the -s                                           |
| ------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| I / you / we / they | work, play | no -s                                                        |
| he / she / it       | works      | most verbs: **+ s** (_plays_)                                |
| he / she / it       | watches    | after **-o, -ch, -sh, -ss, -x**: **+ es** (_goes_, _washes_) |
| he / she / it       | studies    | consonant + **y** → **-ies** (_study_ → _studies_)           |

Read the table from top to bottom: the verb changes only on the **he / she / it** lines, and the end of the verb decides how the -s is written.

::: exemple
Describe your family, step by step:

1. _My father_ is one man → **he**, so the verb takes an -s → _My father **works** in a bank._
2. _My mother_ is **she**, and _teach_ ends in **-ch** → **-es** → _She **teaches** at a primary school._
3. _My little brother_ is **he**, and _go_ ends in **-o** → _He **goes** to school by bus._
4. _My parents and I_ are **we** → no -s → _We **live** in Sousse._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _My brother **go** to school by bus._ ✗ — it tempts you because _go_ is correct with _I_, _you_, _we_ and _they_, so the sentence sounds complete. But _my brother_ is **he**: the verb needs its -s. _My brother **goes** to school by bus._ ✓

::: propriete
Present simple = a **habit** (_She walks to school._) or a **fact** (_Water boils at 100 °C._). Only **he / she / it** adds the -s — and any singular name counts: _my brother_, _Salma_, _water_, _the bus_.

Edge cases: **have** becomes **has** (_She has two sisters._), and a plural subject never takes the -s (_My parents **work**._).

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Water ___ (boil) at 100 °C, and my sister ___ (study) every evening."

---

_Water **boils**_ — a fact, and _water_ is **it**, so the verb takes **-s**. _My sister **studies**_ — a habit, _she_ adds the -s, and _study_ ends in consonant + **y**, so it becomes **-ies**.

:::

::: retenir
Present simple = habits and facts; **he / she / it** add **-s** (goes, watches, studies, has).

:::

## ❓ Questions and negatives — do and does

In the restaurant lesson of Module 5, the book asks you if you sometimes eat out with friends. Now ask the same thing about your sister. The statement is _She eats out._ — and it has an -s.

To turn it into a question or a negative, English needs a helper word. Which one, and where does the -s go?

::: definition
In the present simple, **negatives** and **questions** use the auxiliary **do** — or **does** with he / she / it. After it, the verb comes back to its **base form**.

:::

|              | I / you / we / they              | he / she / it                       |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| statement    | They eat out.                    | She eat**s** out.                   |
| negative     | They **don't** (do not) eat out. | She **doesn't** (does not) eat out. |
| question     | **Do** they eat out?             | **Does** she eat out?               |
| short answer | Yes, they do. / No, they don't.  | Yes, she does. / No, she doesn't.   |

Look at the right-hand column: the -s moves from the verb to the auxiliary. _eats_ becomes _does … eat_ — never both.

::: exemple
Turn _Salma speaks English._ into a negative, a question, and a wh-question:

1. _Salma_ is **she** → the auxiliary is **does**, not _do_.
2. _does_ already carries the -s → the verb goes back to its base: **speak**, not _speaks_.
3. Negative: _Salma **doesn't speak** English._ — Question: _**Does** Salma **speak** English?_
4. With a question word (Module 1), the word comes first: _What language **does** Salma **speak**?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _She doesn't **plays** tennis._ ✗ — it tempts you because _she_ always calls for an -s, so you put it on the verb. But _doesn't_ is _does_ + _not_: the -s is already there. _She doesn't **play** tennis._ ✓

::: methode

1. Choose the auxiliary from the subject: **do** (I, you, we, they) or **does** (he, she, it).
2. Put the main verb in its **base form**.
3. In a question, the auxiliary comes **before** the subject; a question word comes before the auxiliary.

Edge case: the verb **be** never takes _do_. It makes its own negative and question: _She **isn't** tired._ — _**Is** she at home?_ Never mix the two: _She isn't **drink** coffee_ ✗ → _She **doesn't drink** coffee._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Ask about your cousin: _he / live / in Sfax?_ Then give a short negative answer.

---

_**Does** he **live** in Sfax? — No, he **doesn't**._ — _he_ asks for **does**, the auxiliary comes before the subject, and the verb goes back to its base (_live_), because _does_ already carries the -s.

:::

::: retenir
Negatives and questions: **do / does** + base verb — the -s is on **does**, never on the verb as well.

:::

## ⏰ How often? — adverbs of frequency and time markers

In Module 4, you say how often you take the train, the plane or the metro. Karim answers about his school: he takes the bus most mornings, he walks when it is sunny, and he takes a taxi only once or twice a year.

The verb stays the same in all three sentences. So how do you say **how often** — and where does that word go?

::: definition
An **adverb of frequency** answers the question _How often?_ It is a sure sign of a habit, so it goes with the **present simple**.

:::

| Adverb        | How often           | Time expressions (end of the sentence) |
| ------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **always**    | every time          | every day, every week                  |
| **usually**   | most times          | on Sundays, on Friday afternoons       |
| **often**     | many times          | twice a week                           |
| **sometimes** | now and then        | once a month                           |
| **rarely**    | almost never        | once a year                            |
| **never**     | not one single time |                                        |

The adverbs go from the most frequent at the top to the least frequent at the bottom; the expressions in the right column say the same thing with more precision.

::: exemple
Write Karim's habits, step by step:

1. _most mornings_ means **usually** → it goes **before** the main verb: _Karim **usually takes** the bus._
2. Karim is **he** → the verb keeps its -s, the adverb changes nothing: _takes_.
3. A time expression goes at the **end**: _He walks to school **on sunny days**._
4. _once or twice a year_ is almost never → _He **rarely takes** a taxi._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I go **always** to school by bus._ ✗ — it tempts you because in French and in Arabic the adverb comes after the verb. English puts it **before** the main verb: _I **always go** to school by bus._ ✓

::: propriete
The adverb of frequency goes **before the main verb** (_She often reads._) — but **after the verb be** (_She **is never** late._). Time expressions such as _every day_ or _on Sundays_ go at the end.

Edge case: _never_ is already negative. Do not add _don't_: _I **never** eat meat._, not _I don't never eat meat._

:::

::: verifie
Put the words in order: _late / my brother / never / is_

---

_My brother **is never** late._ — the verb here is **be**, so the adverb comes **after** it, not before.

:::

::: retenir
**always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never** + present simple: **before** the main verb, **after** be.

:::

## 🛡️ The present continuous — an action in progress

Module 5 has this scene: one evening the family is in front of the TV, and somebody asks why Jack is not there with them. The question is not about Jack's habits — it is about this very evening.

When the action is happening **now**, while you speak, which tense do you need?

::: definition
The **present continuous** expresses an action **in progress** now, at the moment of speaking, or around now.

Form: **am / is / are** + verb + **-ing**.

:::

|           | I                    | he / she / it         | we / you / they          |
| --------- | -------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------ |
| statement | I **am** watching    | he **is** watching    | they **are** watching    |
| negative  | I**'m not** watching | he **isn't** watching | they **aren't** watching |
| question  | **Am** I watching?   | **Is** he watching?   | **Are** they watching?   |

Two pieces, always: the form of **be** that agrees with the subject, then the **-ing** verb. Spelling of the -ing: _write_ → _writing_ (the final -e goes), _swim_ → _swimming_ (the consonant doubles), _play_ → _playing_.

::: exemple
Ask the family's question about Jack, step by step:

1. The moment is _tonight_, **now** → present continuous.
2. _Jack_ is **he** → the form of be is **is**, and _watch_ becomes **watching**.
3. Negative: _Jack **isn't watching** TV with us._
4. Question with _why_: **is** moves before the subject → _Why **isn't** Jack **watching** TV with us?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _My mother **cooking** dinner._ ✗ — it tempts you because in Arabic and in French one verb is enough to say it, so the -ing word looks like a complete verb. In English the -ing form never stands alone: _My mother **is cooking** dinner._ ✓ The other half-form, _She **is cook**_ ✗, is wrong too: be needs the **-ing**.

::: propriete
Present continuous = **be** (am / is / are, agreeing with the subject) + **verb-ing**. Negative: be + **not**; question: be **before** the subject.

Edge case: a plural subject takes **are**, even a long one (_The students **are** taking a test._). And with a future word (_tomorrow_, _next week_) the same form tells a **planned action** — you will see it in the chapter on the future.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Be quiet! The baby ___ (sleep)."

---

_The baby **is sleeping**._ — _Be quiet!_ shows the action is happening **now**; _the baby_ is **it**, so be is **is**, followed by the **-ing** form.

:::

::: retenir
Present continuous = **am / is / are + verb-ing**, for an action happening now.

:::

## ⚔️ Simple or continuous? — choosing with the markers

In Module 2, a pupil writes a poem about a daydream. Every day he sits at his desk in class — and right now, in his dream, he is flying to the zoo and swimming in a pool.

Same boy, same day, two different tenses. How do you know which one to choose?

::: propriete
The choice depends on **what you describe**, and the **time markers** usually tell you:

:::

| Present simple                                                  | Present continuous                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a habit: _He **sits** at his desk every day._                   | now: _Right now he **is flying** to the zoo._                    |
| a fact: _Water **boils** at 100 °C._                            | in progress: _The water **is boiling** — turn off the gas!_      |
| always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day, on Sundays | now, right now, at the moment, today, these days, Look!, Listen! |

The first two lines put the two tenses face to face — the left column is true again and again, the right column only at this moment; the last line gives the markers that point to each column.

::: exemple
Choose the tense in two sentences, step by step:

1. "Listen! Somebody ___ (knock) at the door." → the marker is **Listen!**: it is happening now → _Somebody **is knocking**._
2. "My father ___ (read) the newspaper every morning." → the marker is **every morning**: a habit → _My father **reads**._
3. Check the form: _somebody_ and _my father_ are both singular (**he / she**), so **is** + -ing, and **-s** on _reads_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Every day, I **am taking** the bus._ ✗ — it tempts you because taking a bus is an action, and actions seem to call for -ing. But _every day_ makes it a **habit**: _Every day, I **take** the bus._ ✓

::: methode

1. Look for a **marker**: a frequency word → present simple; _now_, _Look!_, _at the moment_ → present continuous.
2. No marker? Ask: is it a **habit or a fact**, or is it **happening now**?
3. Build the right form (-s for he / she / it, or be + -ing).

Edge case: _these days_, _this week_, _this year_ describe a **temporary** situation around now, even if it is not happening at this very second: _These days my father **is working** from home._

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Mona usually ___ (walk) to school, but today she ___ (take) the bus."

---

_Mona usually **walks** … today she **is taking** the bus._ — _usually_ marks the habit, so present simple with **-s**; _today_ marks what is different right now, so **is** + **-ing**.

:::

::: retenir
**Habit or fact** → present simple · **now, Look!, these days** → present continuous.

:::

## 🧪 Stative verbs — states, not actions

Your teacher asks a question and you raise your hand: you know the answer, at this very moment. The moment is now — so by the last section, you should say _I am knowing_.

But nobody says it. Why do some verbs refuse the continuous, even **now**?

::: definition
A **stative verb** describes a **state** — a thought, a feeling, a wish, a possession — not an action. It is **not normally used in the continuous**, even with _now_.

The most frequent: **know, understand, believe, like, love, hate, want, need, prefer, seem, be**, and **have** when it means _possess_.

:::

| Verb      | State → present simple                   | Action → present continuous                  |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **have**  | I **have** two brothers. (possess)       | I**'m having** breakfast. (eat)              |
| **taste** | The soup **tastes** salty. (its flavour) | The chef **is tasting** the soup. (tries it) |
| **think** | I **think** you are right. (opinion)     | I**'m thinking about** my exam. (reflect)    |

Three verbs have two meanings: the left column is a state and stays simple, the right column is something you do and can take -ing.

::: exemple
Complete "Right now, she ___ (want) a glass of water." step by step:

1. The marker _right now_ seems to call for the continuous.
2. But _want_ is a **wish**, a state, not an action → no -ing.
3. So the present simple, with the -s for she → _Right now, she **wants** a glass of water._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I **am knowing** the answer._ ✗ — it tempts you because the knowing is happening now, and _now_ usually means -ing. But _know_ is a state: _I **know** the answer._ ✓

::: propriete
A stative verb stays in the **present simple**, whatever the marker: _I **understand** now._ — _She **likes** this song._

Edge case: when a verb has two meanings, ask **state or action?** _think_ = opinion (the _I think that…_ of Module 6) stays simple; _think about_ = reflect can take -ing.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Mmm! This cake ___ (taste) delicious."

---

_This cake **tastes** delicious._ — here _taste_ means **has a flavour**: a state, not an action, so it stays in the present simple, with the -s for _it_.

:::

::: retenir
**Stative verbs** (know, like, want, need, have = possess…) stay in the **present simple**, even now.

:::
