# 🧙 Modal Verbs — The Power-Ups of English

> 💡 "One small word before the verb changes everything: the same _go_ becomes a rule, a ban, a piece of advice, a maybe or a talent. Choose the word, and you choose the meaning."

## 🛡️ Obligation now — must, have to, has to

It is eight o'clock in the evening and Sami's English test is tomorrow morning. The TV is on, and his mother switches it off.

She is not giving him a nice idea he can refuse: revising is necessary tonight, and Sami has no choice. **How does English say that something is necessary?**

::: definition
**Obligation** means that something is necessary: you have no choice, you do it.

English expresses it with **must** or **have to**, followed by the base verb. _I have to revise_ = it is necessary for me to revise. **Must** and **have to** both express obligation.

:::

| Subject              | Obligation in the present |
| -------------------- | ------------------------- |
| I / you / we / they  | **have to** / **must**    |
| he / she / it (Sami) | **has to** / **must**     |

Read the table column by column: **have** becomes **has** with he, she and it — exactly like _have_ in the simple present — while **must** never changes.

::: exemple
Sami writes his plan for the evening, then his mother tells his father about it:

1. Sami speaks about himself, so the subject is **I** → _I must stop watching TV._ (must + base verb)
2. With **I**, have to keeps its form → _I have to revise all my lessons._
3. His mother speaks about Sami, who is **he** → **has** to → _Sami has to sleep early._
4. After **to**, the verb stays bare: _has to sleep_, never _has to sleeps_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _He **must to** study._ ✗ and _He **musts** study._ ✗ — _have to_ carries a **to**, so the hand adds one after _must_ too; and he / she / it asks for an **-s**, so it lands on _must_. Neither is right: **must** takes no _to_ and no _-s_. Correct: _He **must study**._ ✓ — or _He **has to study**._ ✓

::: propriete

- **must** + base verb, the same word for every subject: _I must_, _she must_, _they must_.
- **have to / has to** + base verb: **has to** with he, she, it, and with one person or one thing named (_a good pupil has to…_).
- Edge case: a subject such as _everyone_, _a good teacher_ or _my father_ is **he / she** → **has to**.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "A good pupil ___ (have to) bring his books, and we ___ (must) listen to the teacher."

---

_A good pupil **has to bring** his books, and we **must listen** to the teacher._ — _a good pupil_ is **he**, so have becomes **has**; **must** never changes, whatever the subject, and both are followed by the base verb.

:::

::: retenir
Obligation now: **must** / **have to** (**has to** with he, she, it) + base verb — no _to_ and no _-s_ after must.

:::

## ⏳ Obligation yesterday and tomorrow — had to, will have to

Last year, Rania's father found a new job in Sousse. The whole family left Gafsa: Rania did not want to change school, but she had no choice.

Next year, she will take the national exam, and again there will be no choice: revising every day. The obligation is in the past, then in the future — **but must has only one form. What do you use instead?**

| In the past     | In the present            | In the future    |
| --------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------- |
| **had to**      | have to / has to / must   | **will have to** |
| (every subject) | (has to with he, she, it) | (every subject)  |

Only **have to** travels in time: it becomes **had to** in the past and **will have to** in the future. **Must** stays in the present column.

::: exemple
Tell Rania's story, step by step:

1. _Last year_ points to the past → **had to** → _The family had to move to Sousse._
2. **Had to** is the same for every subject → _Rania had to change school._ (not _has had to_, not _haded to_)
3. _Next year_ points to the future → **will have to** → _She will have to revise every day._
4. After **will**, the verb stays bare, so it is **have**, even with she → _She will have to…_, never _She will has to…_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Yesterday I **must** clean my room._ ✗ — must is the word of obligation you learnt first, so it comes out for every time. But must has no past form (and _musted_ does not exist). Correct: _Yesterday I **had to** clean my room._ ✓ — and for tomorrow: _I **will have to** clean it._ ✓

::: propriete

- Past obligation: **had to** + base verb, for every subject.
- Future obligation: **will have to** + base verb, for every subject.
- Edge case: after **will**, _have_ never becomes _has_: _He **will have to** apologise tomorrow._

:::

::: verifie
Karim was rude to his teacher yesterday. Complete: "Tomorrow he ___ say sorry to her."

---

_Tomorrow he **will have to** say sorry to her._ — _tomorrow_ is the future, so the obligation takes **will have to**; after **will** the verb stays bare, so it is **have**, not _has_, even with he.

:::

::: retenir
Obligation in time: **had to** (past) · **have to / has to / must** (present) · **will have to** (future).

:::

## 🚫 Prohibition — mustn't and Don't

At the school gate, the headmaster has put up a poster with drawings: two pupils fighting, a phone ringing in class, a pupil copying from his neighbour during an exam.

The poster does not say what you have to do. It says the opposite: **what is not allowed.** How do you say that in English?

::: definition
**Prohibition** means that something is **not allowed**.

English has two ways to say it:

- **mustn't** (= must not) + base verb — _You mustn't fight._
- **Don't** + base verb, speaking straight to the person — _Don't fight!_

:::

| Obligation (necessary)      | Prohibition (not allowed)                    |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| You **must** keep quiet.    | You **mustn't** shout. / **Don't** shout!    |
| Pupils **must** be on time. | Pupils **mustn't** cheat. / **Don't** cheat! |

Each line of the table turns an obligation into a prohibition: **must** becomes **mustn't**, or the sentence starts with **Don't**.

::: exemple
Write the poster's rule about the exam, step by step:

1. Copying during the exam is **not allowed** → prohibition → **mustn't** + base verb → _You mustn't cheat in the exam._
2. On a poster you can speak straight to the reader: **Don't** + base verb, with no subject → _Don't cheat in the exam!_
3. For a rule about everybody, **mustn't** works with any subject → _Pupils mustn't cheat in the exam._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _**Don't phoning** in class!_ ✗ — the poster lists the actions as _Phoning in class_, _Fighting_, so the -ing form stays in the sentence. But **Don't** is followed by the **base verb**, exactly like mustn't. Correct: _**Don't phone** in class!_ ✓ — and _You **mustn't phone** in class._ ✓

::: propriete

- **mustn't** + base verb, with any subject: _You / Pupils / He **mustn't** litter._
- **Don't** + base verb, with no subject: it speaks to the reader, and a sign often ends with **!**
- Edge case: mustn't is **must + not** — it keeps must's rules: no _to_, no _-s_ (_He **mustn't** shout_, never _He mustn't to shout_).

:::

::: verifie
Turn "Shouting in the classroom" into two prohibitions: one with mustn't, one with Don't.

---

_You **mustn't shout** in the classroom._ and _**Don't shout** in the classroom!_ — shouting is not allowed, so it is a prohibition; both **mustn't** and **Don't** are followed by the base verb _shout_, never _shouting_.

:::

::: retenir
Prohibition: **mustn't** + base verb (any subject) · **Don't** + base verb (speaking to the reader).

:::

## 💡 Advice — should, shouldn't, ought to

Your pen friend Paul has an important exam next week. He writes to you: he studies all night, he never takes a break, and he is very tired.

You cannot give him orders: it is his life and his choice. But you know what is good for him. **How do you say "this is a good thing to do"?**

::: definition
**Advice** tells someone what is a good thing to do. _You **should** do something_ = it is a good thing to do.

- **should** + base verb, or **ought to** + base verb: _You should rest._ = _You ought to rest._
- **shouldn't** + base verb: it is **not** a good thing to do.

:::

::: exemple
Write three tips in your letter to Paul, step by step:

1. Sleeping well is good for him → **should** + base verb → _You should go to bed early._
2. Same idea with **ought to**: ought always keeps its **to** → _You ought to take a break every two hours._
3. Studying all night is **not** a good thing → **shouldn't** + base verb → _You shouldn't study all night._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _You **should to** take a break._ ✗ and _You **ought take** a break._ ✗ — should and ought to mean the same thing, so the hand gives them the same shape. They are twins with different clothes: **should** never takes _to_, **ought** never goes without it. Correct: _You **should take** a break._ ✓ — _You **ought to take** a break._ ✓

::: propriete

- **should / ought to** + base verb: advice, the same form for every subject (_he should_, never _he shoulds_).
- **shouldn't** + base verb: advice against something.
- Edge case: advice is not obligation. _You **should** eat fruit_ = a good idea, you can choose; _You **must** wear your uniform_ = necessary, no choice.

:::

::: verifie
Your cousin eats sweets every day. Give him one piece of advice with ought to and one with shouldn't.

---

_You **ought to eat** more fruit._ and _You **shouldn't eat** so many sweets._ — both give advice, not orders; **ought** keeps its _to_, **shouldn't** takes the base verb with no _to_.

:::

::: retenir
Advice: **should** / **ought to** + base verb · against it: **shouldn't** + base verb.

:::

## 🔮 Possibility — may, might, could

Karim plays football in the rain without his coat. Will he catch a cold tomorrow? Nobody knows for sure.

It is not certain, and it is not impossible either: it is **possible**. English does not need _perhaps_ + _will_ for that. **Which small words say "it is possible"?**

::: definition
**May**, **might** and **could** + base verb say that something is **possible**, in the present or in the future.

- **may** and **might** mean _perhaps_: _He may catch a cold._ = Perhaps he will catch a cold.
- **could** is similar to may and might: _He could catch a cold._

:::

| Certain                      | Possible (= perhaps)                                |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Karim **will** catch a cold. | Karim **may** / **might** / **could** catch a cold. |
| The bus **is** late.         | The bus **may** / **might** / **could** be late.    |

The left column states a fact; the right column puts _perhaps_ inside the verb. That is all the modal adds: the sentence is no longer sure.

::: exemple
Say what is possible for Karim, step by step:

1. The result is not sure → possibility → **may** + base verb → _He may catch a cold._
2. The same idea with **might** → _He might have a fever tomorrow._ (might = perhaps, and _tomorrow_ shows the future)
3. The same idea with **could** → _He could miss the next match._
4. With he, the verb stays bare: _may catch_, _might have_, _could miss_ — no **-s**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _He **might catches** a cold._ ✗ — the rule "he / she / it → -s" of the simple present is strong, so it jumps onto the verb. But after a modal, the verb is always bare. Correct: _He **might catch** a cold._ ✓ — and the modal itself never changes either: never _He **mights**_.

::: propriete

- **may / might / could** + base verb = possible (perhaps), the same form for every subject.
- Present or future: _She **may** be at home now._ — _It **might** rain tomorrow._
- Edge case: _Could you open the window?_ asks someone to do something — a different use of could, for the chapter on polite requests.

:::

::: verifie
Rewrite with might: "Perhaps the bus will be late because of the storm."

---

_The bus **might be** late because of the storm._ — **might** already means _perhaps_, so it replaces _perhaps_ + _will_; after it the verb stays bare: _be_.

:::

::: retenir
Possibility: **may** / **might** / **could** + base verb = perhaps (present or future).

:::

## 💪 Ability and inability — can, be able to, can't, be unable to

Your class visits an old people's home as volunteers. Mr Hédi is ninety: he still reads the newspaper every morning, but he cannot walk alone any more.

You want to say what he is able to do, and what he is not able to do. **Which words say ability and inability?**

::: definition
**Ability** is what someone is able to do; **inability** is what they are not able to do.

- Ability: **can** + base verb, or **be able to** + base verb.
- Inability: **can't** (= cannot) + base verb, or **be unable to** + base verb.

:::

| Subject         | can / can't        | be able to / be unable to  |
| --------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| I               | I **can** read.    | I **am able to** read.     |
| he / she / it   | He **can't** walk. | He **is unable to** walk.  |
| we / you / they | They **can** help. | They **are able to** help. |

Compare the two columns: **can** is the same for every subject, while **be** changes — **am**, **is**, **are** — exactly as in _I am_, _he is_, _they are_.

::: exemple
Describe Mr Hédi, step by step:

1. He reads the newspaper → ability → **can** + base verb → _He can read the newspaper._ (no -s on can, none on read)
2. The same with **be able to**: he → **is** → _He is able to read the newspaper._
3. He cannot walk alone → inability → _He can't walk alone._ = _He is unable to walk alone._
4. The volunteers help him → they → **are** → _They are able to help him._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Many animals **are unable survive** without our help._ ✗ — can is followed by the bare verb, so the _to_ of _able to_ falls off too. But **able** and **unable** always keep their **to**. Correct: _Many animals **are unable to survive** without our help._ ✓

::: propriete

- **can / can't** + base verb, the same for every subject; **cannot** is the full form of can't, written as one word.
- **be able to / be unable to** + base verb, with **be** agreeing with the subject: I am, he is, they are.
- Edge case: the subject decides _be_, not the verb after it: _My sisters **are** able to cook_, never _My sisters is able to cook_.

:::

::: verifie
Rewrite with be unable to: "My little brother can't swim."

---

_My little brother **is unable to swim**._ — _my little brother_ is **he**, so be becomes **is**; **unable** keeps its **to**, and the verb after it stays bare: _swim_.

:::

::: retenir
Ability: **can** / **be able to** + base verb · inability: **can't** / **be unable to** + base verb.

:::

## 🧭 Choosing the right modal — find the job first

The school club makes a poster called _Save our school_. Five sentences are on it: one rule, one ban, one tip, one warning about what may happen, and one proud line about what the club can do.

Every sentence uses a different small word. **How do you choose the right one without guessing?** You ask first what job the sentence does.

::: exemple
Complete three lines of the poster, step by step:

1. _Pupils ___ respect the school rules._ → a rule, no choice → **obligation** → _Pupils **must** respect the school rules._ (or _have to_)
2. _You look pale. You ___ see the school doctor._ → a good idea → **advice** → _You **should** see the school doctor._
3. _Take your umbrella: it ___ rain this afternoon._ → nobody is sure → **possibility** → _It **may** rain this afternoon._ (or _might_, _could_)

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Take your umbrella: it **must** rain this afternoon._ ✗ — French says _il doit pleuvoir_ for "perhaps it will rain", and the word slides into English. But in this chapter **must** says obligation, and nobody can make the rain obligatory. Correct: _It **may** / **might** rain this afternoon._ ✓

::: methode

1. Find the **job** of the sentence, then take its word:

| Job         | Words                                              | Example                        |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| obligation  | must · have to / has to · had to · will have to    | We **have to** wear a uniform. |
| prohibition | mustn't · Don't                                    | **Don't** litter!              |
| advice      | should · shouldn't · ought to                      | You **ought to** rest.         |
| possibility | may · might · could                                | It **might** snow.             |
| ability     | can · be able to — inability: can't · be unable to | I **can** swim.                |

2. Check the **form**: every one of them is followed by the **base verb**. Three keep a **to**: **have to**, **ought to**, **be able to** — the others never take one.
3. Edge case: the words never take **-s** — except the two that are really verbs: **has to** and **is able to**.

:::

::: verifie
Choose between Don't, should and can: "My grandfather is eighty, but he ___ still ride his bike."

---

_He **can** still ride his bike._ — the sentence says what he is able to do, so its job is **ability** → **can** + base verb. _Should_ would give advice and _Don't_ would forbid it: neither is the job here.

:::

::: retenir
First the job (obligation, prohibition, advice, possibility, ability), then the word — and always the base verb after it.

:::
