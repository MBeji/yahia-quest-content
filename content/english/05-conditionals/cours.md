# ⚡ The First Conditional — The If-Then Pact

> 💡 "Every plan for tomorrow hides a little word: _if_. Learn to use it, and you can say what will happen when the road, the weather or the timetable decides."

## 🚌 Condition and result — the two halves of the sentence

Next summer, Skander travels to London with his class. He already has a plan in his head: maybe he will want to see the whole city in one afternoon, and then the red double-decker bus will be his choice.

His plan has two ideas, and the second one depends on the first. How does English join an idea that may happen to what will follow from it?

::: definition
A **first conditional** sentence (the book calls it **conditional type one**) is made of two clauses:

- the **if clause** states the **condition** — what may happen;
- the **main clause** states the **result** — what will follow from it.

:::

| if clause — the condition     | main clause — the result            |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| If Skander wants a city tour, | he will take the double-decker bus. |
| If the seats are comfortable, | the pupils will enjoy the ride.     |

Read the table from left to right: the left column is always introduced by **if**, and it is the part that must happen first; the right column tells what will happen **because of it**.

::: exemple
Find the condition and the result in: _The pupils will enjoy the ride if the seats are comfortable._

1. Look for the word **if** → it opens _if the seats are comfortable_, so that clause is the **condition**.
2. The other clause, _the pupils will enjoy the ride_, is the **main clause** → it is the **result**.
3. Check the meaning: the comfort comes first, and the enjoyment depends on it → condition and result are in the right place.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: "The condition is the first part of the sentence." ✗ — in _The pupils will enjoy the ride if the seats are comfortable_, the first part is the **result**. It tempts you because most examples start with _if_. The right test is the word, not the place: the condition is **the clause that starts with if**. ✓

::: propriete
The clause introduced by **if** is the condition; the other clause is the main clause, and it gives the result.

Edge case: the if clause can come **first or second** — its job never changes: _If it rains, we will take the metro._ = _We will take the metro if it rains._ In both, _it rains_ is the condition.

:::

::: verifie
_Mum will drive slowly if there is a lot of traffic._ Which clause is the condition, and which is the result?

---

The condition is **if there is a lot of traffic** — it is the clause that starts with **if**, even if it comes second. The result is the main clause, **Mum will drive slowly**: it is what follows from the traffic.

:::

::: retenir
The **if clause** states the condition, the **main clause** states the result — wherever each one stands.

:::

## ⚙️ The form — If + present simple, will + base verb

Skander writes his plan in his notebook: _If I ___ (want) a city tour, I ___ (take) the double-decker bus._

Two verbs to put in the right form. The trip is next summer, so his first idea is to use the future for both. Is that right?

::: definition
**If + present simple → will + base verb** (conditional type one)

The **if clause** takes the **simple present**; the **main clause** takes the **future with will** — often short: _I'll_, _you'll_, _she'll_, _we'll_, _they'll_.

:::

| if clause: simple present     | main clause: will + base verb    |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| If I **want** a city tour,    | I **will take** the bus.         |
| If the train **goes** faster, | we**'ll be** on time.            |
| If they **visit** the museum, | they **will see** the dinosaurs. |

Look at the verbs in bold: the future appears **only once**, in the right-hand column. On the left, the verb is in the simple present even though the action is in the future.

::: exemple
Complete Skander's sentence: _If I ___ (want) a city tour, I ___ (take) the double-decker bus._

1. The first blank is in the **if clause** → simple present → _want_ (no _will_ here, even for next summer).
2. The second blank is in the **main clause** → _will_ + base verb → _will take_.
3. Write the whole sentence → _If I want a city tour, I will take the double-decker bus._ — or, shorter, _I'll take_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _If I **will want** a city tour, I will take the bus._ ✗ — it tempts you because the whole plan is in the future, so your ear asks for _will_ twice. In English, the **if** already carries the future idea: the if clause stays in the **simple present**. _If I **want** a city tour, I will take the bus._ ✓

::: propriete
Only the main clause takes **will**. The if clause follows the rules of the simple present — so with **he, she, it** the verb takes an **-s**.

Edge case: _If the train **goes** faster, I'll be on time._ — _the train_ is _it_, so _go_ becomes _goes_; and after **will** the verb is always bare: _will be_, never _will is_ or _will to be_.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: _If Jenny ___ (get) lost in London, she ___ (check) her street map._

---

_If Jenny **gets** lost in London, she **will check** her street map._ — the if clause takes the simple present, and _Jenny_ is _she_, so **gets** takes an **-s**; the main clause takes **will** + the bare verb, **will check**.

:::

::: retenir
**If + simple present, will + base verb** — never _will_ in the if clause.

:::

## 🚫 Saying no — don't / doesn't in the if clause, won't in the result

In London, Jenny opens her map and sees a hundred little streets. What if she **does not** understand it? And her friend Sami reads a sign: there are repairs on the road home.

Both sentences need a negative — one in the condition, one in the result. Which negative goes where?

::: definition

- In the **if clause** (simple present): **don't / doesn't + base verb**.
- In the **main clause** (future): **won't + base verb** — _won't_ is the short form of **will not**.

:::

| clause      | affirmative                    | negative                              |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| if clause   | If she **understands** the map | If she **doesn't understand** the map |
| main clause | you **will be** home early     | you **won't be** home early           |

Each clause makes its negative with its **own** tense: the simple present uses _do / does_, the future uses _will_.

::: exemple
Write Jenny's sentence: _If she ___ (not understand) the map, she ___ (ask) a policeman._

1. The first blank is in the **if clause**, and _Jenny_ is _she_ → **doesn't** + base verb → _doesn't understand_ (the **-s** is already in _doesn't_).
2. The second blank is the **result**, and it is affirmative → _will ask_.
3. The sentence → _If she doesn't understand the map, she will ask a policeman._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _If she doesn't **understands** the map…_ ✗ — it tempts you because _she_ calls for an **-s**. But _doesn't_ already carries it (_does_ + _not_), so the verb after it stays bare: _If she doesn't **understand** the map…_ ✓

::: propriete
**doesn't / don't** and **won't** are always followed by the **base verb**. Both clauses can be negative at the same time.

Edge case: _If there are repairs on the road, you **won't be** back home before one o'clock._ — _won't_ + _be_, never _won't to be_ or _won't are_. And with both negative: _If you **don't hurry**, you **won't catch** the bus._

:::

::: verifie
Complete: _If Sami ___ (not leave) now, he ___ (not catch) the train._

---

_If Sami **doesn't leave** now, he **won't catch** the train._ — in the if clause, _Sami_ is _he_, so the negative is **doesn't** + the bare verb _leave_; in the main clause, the negative future is **won't** + the bare verb _catch_.

:::

::: retenir
If clause: **don't / doesn't + base verb** · main clause: **won't (= will not) + base verb**.

:::

## ✍️ Which clause first? — the order and the comma

Skander sends two messages to his mother. The first one: _If the bus is late, I'll call you._ The second one: _I'll call you if the bus is late._

The two messages say exactly the same thing — only the order changes. Does the punctuation change with it?

| order                 | example                                | comma?                        |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| if clause **first**   | If the bus is late**,** I'll call you. | **yes** — after the if clause |
| main clause **first** | I'll call you if the bus is late.      | **no**                        |

The comma sits exactly where the condition ends and the result begins — but only when the condition comes first.

::: exemple
Rewrite _We will visit the Tower of London if the weather is fine._ with the if clause first.

1. Take the clause that starts with **if** and put it at the front → _If the weather is fine_.
2. The condition is now first → a **comma** closes it → _If the weather is fine,_
3. Add the result with a small letter → _If the weather is fine, we will visit the Tower of London._ The tenses do not move: present in the if clause, _will_ in the result.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _We will visit the Tower\**,*\* if the weather is fine._ ✗ — it tempts you because you remember "conditional = comma". But the comma belongs to the **if clause that opens** the sentence. When the main clause comes first, there is no comma: _We will visit the Tower if the weather is fine._ ✓ And the comma never goes straight after the word _If_ (_If, the weather…_ ✗).

::: propriete
The two clauses can change places without changing the meaning. **If clause first → a comma after it. Main clause first → no comma.**

Edge case: the comma comes after the **whole** if clause, however long it is — _If the bus from the airport is late again tonight, I'll call you._ Short sentences are sometimes printed without it; in your own writing, and in this chapter's exercises, put it.

:::

::: verifie
Put the if clause first and punctuate: _You will enjoy the museum if you like history._

---

_If you like history, you will enjoy the museum._ — the clause that starts with **if** moves to the front, and because it comes first, a **comma** closes it, before the result. The tenses stay the same.

:::

::: retenir
**If clause first → comma** after it; **main clause first → no comma**. The meaning does not change.

:::

## 🔗 Real possibilities — the chain of consequences

Before her trip, Jenny plays a game with a friend: she tries to guess what will happen to her in London. _If I get lost, I'll check my map. If I don't understand the map…_ Each answer opens the next question.

Why does English use the first conditional for this game — and how do you continue the chain?

::: definition
The first conditional talks about a **real possibility**: something that **may really happen** in the present or the future, and its **likely result**. It is the language of plans, warnings and predictions.

:::

| step | condition (if + present)           | result (will / won't)           |
| ---- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| 1    | If I **get** lost,                 | I**'ll check** my map.          |
| 2    | If I **don't understand** the map, | I**'ll ask** a policeman.       |
| 3    | If I **find** my way,              | I**'ll get** back to the hotel. |

Follow the arrows in your head: the result of one line becomes the idea of the next condition — and each time it moves to the left, it goes back to the **simple present**.

::: exemple
Continue the chain after _I'll ask a policeman._

1. The new condition is the result we just reached → _ask a policeman_ becomes the **condition**.
2. A condition goes into the **if clause**, so the verb loses its _will_ → _If I ask a policeman,_ (present).
3. Add a likely result with **will** → _If I ask a policeman, he **will show** me the way._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _If I **will ask** a policeman, he will show me the way._ ✗ — it tempts you because _I'll ask_ was just written in the future, and you copy it. When a result becomes the next condition, it moves into the if clause and goes back to the **simple present**: _If I **ask** a policeman, he will show me the way._ ✓

::: propriete
Use the first conditional for a situation that is **possible and real**, not certain: the if clause names it, the main clause predicts what will follow. In a chain, **each result can become the next condition** — in the simple present.

Edge case: the chain can turn negative at any link — _If I find my way, I won't need a taxi._

:::

::: verifie
Continue the chain: _If I find my way, I'll get back to the hotel._ → next link, with _(be) more careful next time_.

---

_If I **get** back to the hotel, I **will be** more careful next time._ — the result _I'll get back to the hotel_ becomes the new condition, so it moves into the if clause in the **simple present** (_get_); the new result takes **will** + the bare verb (_will be_).

:::

::: retenir
The first conditional predicts the result of a **real possibility** — and a result can become the next condition, in the present.

:::

## 🚀 Going further (beyond the 9th-grade book)

This section is **not part of the 9th-grade programme**: nothing in the exercises of this chapter tests it. It is here only for the curious, because you will meet these forms at lycée.

- **The zero conditional** — for things that are always true: _If you heat ice, it melts._ (simple present in both clauses).
- **The second conditional** — for an imaginary situation: _If I had a million dinars, I would buy a bus for my school._ (past simple → _would_ + base verb).
- **unless** — it means _if … not_: _Unless you hurry, you'll miss the bus._ = _If you don't hurry, you'll miss the bus._

For the 9th-grade exam, one pattern is enough: **If + present simple, will / won't + base verb**.
