# 🔮 The Future — Unlock Tomorrow

> 💡 "Every hero has a plan for tomorrow. **Be going to** tells what you intend, the **present progressive** with a future word tells what is already arranged, and **will** tells what the future holds."

## 🎯 Be going to — an intention

The end of the year is close, and your class talks about the future. Rania loves Biology: she has made up her mind, one day she will work in a laboratory. Omar is good at languages, and his choice is made too — he wants to translate books.

Neither of them is describing today. Both are saying what they **have decided to do**. Which English structure carries that intention?

::: definition
**Be going to** + **verb** expresses an **intention**: something you have decided to do in the future.

:::

| Subject         | be      | going to + base verb                   |
| --------------- | ------- | -------------------------------------- |
| I               | **am**  | going to **study** Biology.            |
| he / she / it   | **is**  | going to **become** a translator.      |
| we / you / they | **are** | going to **join** the basketball team. |

Read the columns: only the middle one changes with the subject — **am**, **is**, **are**. The verb after _going to_ stays in its base form for everybody.

::: exemple
Write Rania's intention: _Rania / become / a biologist_.

1. She has decided it → it is an **intention** → **be going to**.
2. _Rania_ is _she_ → **is**.
3. After _going to_, the verb keeps its base form → _become_, never _becomes_ or _becoming_.

The sentence: _Rania **is going to become** a biologist._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Omar **going to** be a translator._ ✗ — in fast speech the little verb _is_ disappears, and in Arabic a sentence like this needs no verb "to be" at all. English cannot drop it: _Omar **is going to** be a translator._ ✓

::: propriete
Intention → **am / is / are + going to + base verb**.

- Negative: **not** after am / is / are → _Our teacher **is not going to** come tomorrow._
- Question: am / is / are **before** the subject → _**Is** Dora **going to** finish her studies?_

Edge case: the verb after _going to_ can be _go_ itself → _We **are going to go** to the beach._ Two forms of _go_ in a row are correct.

:::

::: verifie
Complete with **be going to**: "My brothers ___ (join) the school football team."

---

_My brothers **are going to join** the school football team._ — it is their intention, so **be going to**; _my brothers_ are _they_ → **are**, and the verb stays in its base form: _join_.

:::

::: retenir
Intention = **am / is / are + going to + base verb**: _I'm going to study Biology._

:::

## 📅 Present progressive + a future word — a planned action

Peter's Baccalaureate starts next week. His timetable is on the wall, and every afternoon is already booked for revision. Ask him about Monday and he answers: _I'm reviewing Maths on Monday._

_I'm reviewing_ is the present progressive — the tense you use for what is happening **now**. Yet Peter is talking about next Monday. How can this form speak about the future?

::: definition
The **present progressive** + a **future word** expresses a **planned action**: something already arranged for a moment in the future.

Future words: _tomorrow_, _tonight_, _next week_, _next Tuesday_, _the coming month_…

:::

| Now (no future word)               | Planned action (with a future word)                 |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| I **am buying** a book.            | I **am buying** a book **next Tuesday**.            |
| Ann **is playing** basketball.     | Ann **is playing** basketball **tomorrow**.         |
| They **are travelling** to Sousse. | They **are travelling** to Sousse **next weekend**. |

The verb is the same in both columns: **am / is / are + verb + ing**. Only the future word on the right turns the action into a plan.

::: exemple
Write Peter's plan: _my cousins / visit / us / next Saturday_ (the visit is arranged).

1. The visit is already fixed for a day → a **planned action**.
2. _my cousins_ are _they_ → **are**, and _visit_ + **ing** → _visiting_.
3. The future word is what makes it future, so it must be there → _next Saturday_.

The sentence: _My cousins **are visiting** us **next Saturday**._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I **visit** my uncle tomorrow._ ✗ — in your own language a simple present can already talk about tomorrow, so it feels natural. For a planned action English needs the present progressive: _I **am visiting** my uncle tomorrow._ ✓

::: propriete
Planned action → **am / is / are + verb + ing + a future word**.

Edge case: take the future word away and the same form speaks about **now** — _I am buying a book_ means "at this moment". The future word is not a decoration: it carries the future.

:::

::: verifie
Make it a planned action: "We ___ (visit) the Bardo Museum next Friday."

---

_We **are visiting** the Bardo Museum next Friday._ — the visit is arranged for a precise day, so **present progressive**: _we_ → **are** + _visiting_, with the future word **next Friday**.

:::

::: retenir
Planned action = **am / is / are + verb + ing + a future word** (tomorrow, next week, the coming month).

:::

## ⚖️ Intention or planned action?

Two sentences about Jane: _Jane is going to travel to London._ — _Jane is travelling to London tomorrow._ Both talk about her trip, and both are correct English.

Your book asks you to say what each one expresses. How can you tell an intention from a planned action just by reading the verbs?

::: exemple
Say what each sentence expresses, and why:

1. _Sami is going to learn the guitar._ → **going to** + a base verb (_learn_) → an **intention**.
2. _Our teacher is giving us a test tomorrow._ → **is** + _giving_ + the future word _tomorrow_ → a **planned action**.
3. _Is Dora finishing her studies next year?_ → the question keeps the form **is** + _finishing_ + _next year_ → a **planned action**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _We are going to Djerba next week_ → "an intention" ✗ — the words _going to_ catch your eye. But no base verb follows them, only a place: here _going_ is the verb _go_ in the present progressive, with the future word _next week_. It is a **planned action** ✓.

::: methode
To classify a future sentence:

1. Find **going to**. Is it followed by a **base verb**? → **be going to** → an **intention**.
2. Otherwise, find **am / is / are + verb + ing** with a **future word** → a **planned action**.

Edge case: _going to_ + a place (_going to Spain_, _going to the stadium_) is the verb _go_, not _be going to_ + verb.

:::

::: verifie
What does this sentence express? "My parents are buying a new car next month."

---

A **planned action** — _are_ + _buying_ is the present progressive, and _next month_ is the future word; there is no _going to_ + base verb in the sentence.

:::

::: retenir
**going to + base verb** → intention; **am / is / are + verb + ing + future word** → planned action.

:::

## 🔮 Will and won't — the future tense

A scientist on the radio describes the car of 2050. It does not need petrol, because the sun gives it all its energy. It opens with a smart card, not a key. And its price is low.

He is not talking about his own intention: he is saying what **will happen**. Which form does English use for this simple future?

::: definition
The **future tense** is made with **will**:

- Affirmative: subject + **will** + bare infinitive
- Negative: subject + **will not (won't)** + bare infinitive
- Interrogative: **will** + subject + bare infinitive?

The **bare infinitive** is the base verb without _to_: _use_, _be_, _cost_.

:::

| Affirmative                       | Negative                     | Question                         |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| The car **will use** solar power. | It **won't use** petrol.     | **Will** it **use** petrol?      |
| Cars **will be** cheap.           | They **won't be** expensive. | How much **will** they **cost**? |

Look down the columns: **will** never changes with the subject, and the verb after it is always bare — _use_, _be_, _cost_.

::: exemple
Turn the scientist's idea into a question: _cars / be / different_.

1. The question opens with **will** → it carries the future.
2. Then comes the subject → _Will cars…_
3. Then the bare infinitive → _be_, never _are_ and never _to be_.

The question: _**Will** cars **be** different?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _The car **will uses** solar power._ ✗ — with _it_ you are used to adding **-s** (_it uses_). After **will** the verb never changes: _The car **will use** solar power._ ✓

::: propriete
**will / won't + bare infinitive**, the same with every subject; the short form of _will_ is **'ll** (_I'll_, _she'll_, _they'll_).

Edge case: no **to** after _will_ — _I will **do** everything I can_, never _I will to do_. And with _be_: _Will you **be** at home? — I **won't be** late._

:::

::: verifie
Make the sentence negative: "The trip will cost a lot."

---

_The trip **won't cost** a lot._ — **won't** is _will not_, and the verb after it stays bare: _cost_.

:::

::: retenir
Future tense = **will / won't + bare infinitive**; question: **Will** + subject + bare infinitive?

:::

## 💭 Intend to — saying what you plan to do

Your cousin Yasmine has just passed her Baccalaureate. Her friend congratulates her and asks the big question: _What do you intend to do now?_ She already has an answer — studies in Germany.

She could say _I'm going to study in Germany_. But the question already uses another verb for the same intention: _intend_. How is a sentence with it built?

::: definition
**intend to** + bare infinitive expresses an **intention**: subject + **intend(s) to** + base verb.

:::

| Subject             | intend      | to + base verb       |
| ------------------- | ----------- | -------------------- |
| I / you / we / they | **intend**  | to **study** abroad. |
| he / she / it       | **intends** | to **rent** a house. |

_intend_ is an ordinary verb in the present: it takes **-s** with he / she / it, and the verb after **to** stays in its base form.

::: exemple
Yasmine tells you about her friend Kate's plan: _Kate / intend / live / with her friends_.

1. _Kate_ is _she_ → _intend_ takes **-s** → **intends**.
2. After _intend_ comes **to** → _intends to_.
3. After _to_, the base verb → _live_, never _living_ or _lives_.

The sentence: _Kate **intends to live** with her friends._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Kate **intend** to rent a house._ ✗ — _intend_ is followed by another verb, so it is easy to forget that it is itself a verb in the present. With _she_ it takes **-s**: _Kate **intends** to rent a house._ ✓

::: propriete
Subject + **intend(s) to** + base verb. Questions and negatives use **do / does**, like any verb in the present:

- _What **do** you **intend to** do now?_ — _**Does** she **intend to** leave school?_

Edge case: in the negative, the **-s** moves to _does_ → _She **doesn't intend** to tell her parents_, never _doesn't intends_.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "My brother ___ (intend) ___ (buy) a bike next summer."

---

_My brother **intends to buy** a bike next summer._ — _my brother_ is _he_, so **intends** takes **-s**; after **to** comes the base verb **buy**.

:::

::: retenir
**intend(s) to + base verb** = an intention: _I intend to study abroad._

:::

## 🤔 Be thinking of + verb + ing

Yasmine's plan has one problem: she does not have enough money for Germany. She has an idea in her head — a loan from her bank — and she tells her friend: _I'm thinking of getting a loan._

It is another way to talk about an intention. But after _thinking of_, the verb does not stay bare. What happens to it?

::: definition
**be thinking of** + verb + **ing** expresses an **intention**: subject + **am / is / are thinking of** + verb + ing.

:::

| Subject         | be      | thinking of + verb + ing         |
| --------------- | ------- | -------------------------------- |
| I               | **am**  | thinking of **studying** abroad. |
| he / she / it   | **is**  | thinking of **leaving** school.  |
| we / you / they | **are** | thinking of **living** together. |

Two parts change: **am / is / are** follows the subject, and the verb after **of** always ends in **-ing**.

::: exemple
Write Yasmine's idea: _Yasmine / think of / get / a loan_.

1. _Yasmine_ is _she_ → **is thinking of**.
2. **of** is a preposition, and after a preposition an English verb takes **-ing** → _getting_ (the _t_ doubles).
3. The sentence: _Yasmine **is thinking of getting** a loan._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I'm thinking **to leave** school._ ✗ — you have just learned _intend **to**_, and your own language often says "think to do". But _thinking_ is followed by **of** and a verb in **-ing**: _I'm thinking **of leaving** school._ ✓

::: propriete
Two structures, two different verb forms after them:

- **intend to** + base verb → _I intend **to study** abroad._
- **be thinking of** + verb + ing → _I'm thinking **of studying** abroad._

Edge case: in a question, only **am / is / are** moves → _**Are** you **thinking of** studying abroad?_

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "We ___ (think of / spend) the holidays in Tabarka."

---

_We **are thinking of spending** the holidays in Tabarka._ — _we_ takes **are**, and after the preposition **of** the verb takes **-ing**: _spending_.

:::

::: retenir
**be thinking of + verb + ing** = an intention: _I'm thinking of studying abroad._

:::

## 🚀 Going further (beyond the 9th-grade book)

This part is **not** in the 9th-grade programme: read it for curiosity, it is never tested in this chapter.

In a sentence about the future, English does not put _will_ after **when** or **as soon as**: it uses the present. _I will call you **when** I **arrive**._ — _**As soon as** the exam **ends**, we will go to the beach._
