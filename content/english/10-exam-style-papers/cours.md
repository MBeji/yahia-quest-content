# ⚔️ Exam-Style Papers — The Final Arena

> 💡 "Every English paper of the national exam is built on **one text**: you read it, you work on its language, you write about its topic. Know the shape of the paper and half the battle is won."

The paper always has three parts, and all of them use the same short text on one of the six themes of your book — family life, education, health and environment, services, entertainment, civility. **Part 1 — Reading**: questions on what the text says and means. **Part 2 — Language**: grammar gaps inside sentences taken from the text. **Part 3 — Writing**: building a clear paragraph on the same topic. Each section below trains one skill the examiner rewards.

## 🎯 Part 1 · Reading for the main idea

In the Health and Environment module you read a short text about pollution. It said that pollution can be big or small, that people as well as factories cause it, and that we only move our waste to another place. The book then asked: _what is the text about?_

Four answers looked right, because each one used words from the text. How do you choose the one the examiner wants?

::: definition
The **main idea** (or **gist**) is the one message of the **whole** text. Every paragraph supports it.

A **detail** is one fact inside the text: a name, a number, a place, one example.

:::

| Candidate answer                          | Paragraph 1 | Paragraph 2 | Paragraph 3 |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| Factories send smoke into the air.        | a part      | no          | no          |
| Some pollution is invisible.              | no          | yes         | no          |
| What pollution is and how people cause it | yes         | yes         | yes         |

Read the table line by line: a detail lights up one paragraph, the main idea lights up **all** of them.

::: exemple
Find the main idea of the pollution text, step by step:

1. Read the whole text once, quickly, without stopping on new words → you want the general picture, not the details.
2. Say in a few words what each paragraph is about → the main idea must cover every one of them.
3. Test each answer against your paragraph summaries → only _what pollution is and how people cause it_ covers the three paragraphs, so it is the main idea.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: choosing an answer **because its words are in the text**. _Factories send smoke into the air_ looks safe for exactly that reason — but it describes one line of one paragraph. A main idea covers the whole text, even when its words are not copied from it.

::: methode
To find the main idea: read for the gist → sum up each paragraph → keep the answer that covers them all, and reject any answer that covers only one.

Edge case: the main idea is often in the **first or the last sentence**. When it is not written anywhere, you build it yourself from your paragraph summaries.

:::

::: verifie
Text: "Kelly's family moved to a new city. At her new school, the children laughed at her accent. Later, some of them attacked her after school." Main idea: (a) Kelly's accent, (b) Kelly's difficult start at a new school, or (c) the new city?

---

**(b)** — the accent is a detail of the second sentence and the city a detail of the first; only _a difficult start at a new school_ covers all three sentences.

:::

::: retenir
The main idea covers **every** paragraph; an answer that covers only one line is a detail.

:::

## 🔎 Part 1 · Scanning for details — and proving it

In the Education module, Kelly tells a friend about her new school, and the Activity Book gives you statements to correct with the text. In the exam you meet the same task: _True or false? Justify your answer._

Writing only "False" earns almost nothing: the mark goes to the proof. Where do you find it, and what exactly do you copy?

::: definition
**Scanning** means searching the text quickly for **one** piece of information — a name, a number, a key word — without reading everything again.

**Justifying** means copying the **short part of the text** that proves your answer.

:::

Mini-text: _"Sami moved to Sfax last year because his father found a new job. At first, he felt lonely at his new school. Then he joined the drama club, and now he has many friends."_

| Statement                                   | Key word to scan | What the text says                   | Answer |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------ |
| Sami moved because he wanted a bigger house | because          | "because his father found a new job" | False  |
| Sami felt lonely at first                   | lonely           | "At first, he felt lonely"           | True   |
| Sami still has no friends                   | friends          | "now he has many friends"            | False  |

The second column is what your eyes hunt for; the third column is what you copy as your justification.

::: exemple
Statement: _"Sami joined the football club."_ True or false?

1. Choose the key word: _club_ → it is short, and the text must contain it if the answer is there.
2. Scan for _club_ → you find "he joined the drama club".
3. Compare with the statement: _football_ ≠ _drama_ → the statement is **False**.
4. Justify with the text: "he joined the drama club" → this quotation is what earns the mark.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: answering from **what seems normal** instead of from the text. _"Sami was happy at his new school"_ sounds true, because most pupils are — but the text says "At first, he felt lonely". Your opinion is not in the text, so the answer is False.

::: methode
Key word → scan → compare → quote.

Edge case: a statement can be **half true**. _"Sami moved to Sfax because he wanted a bigger house"_: the city is right, the reason is wrong. One wrong part makes the whole statement false.

:::

::: verifie
Statement: _"Sami moved to Sfax two years ago."_ True or false? Justify.

---

**False** — "Sami moved to Sfax last year". The city is right but the time is wrong, and one wrong part makes the statement false. The quotation is your proof.

:::

::: retenir
Scan for the key word, compare, then **quote** the text: the proof earns the mark, not the word "True" or "False".

:::

## 🧭 Part 1 · Reference words — who is "he", what is "which"?

In the Entertainment module you read the story of a little fish who swims away from his father, is carried off by a ship, and ends up in an aquarium far away. The Activity Book then asked who _he_, _the two friends_ and _they_ were.

Three short words, three different characters. How do you know who each word stands for?

::: definition
A **reference word** replaces a noun that came **before** it, so that the writer does not repeat it.

It can be a **pronoun** (_he, she, it, they, him, them_), a **possessive** (_his, her, its, their_) or a **relative pronoun**: **who** for people, **which** for things, **that** for both.

:::

| Reference word      | It points back to…           |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| he / him / his      | one male person              |
| she / her           | one female person            |
| it / its            | one thing or one animal      |
| they / them / their | two or more people or things |
| who / which / that  | the noun **just before** it  |

The right-hand column gives you the test: the word you choose must match in **number** (one or more) and in **kind** (person or thing).

::: exemple
Text: _"Grandfather gave Salma a bike for her birthday. She rides it every day with her friends, who live in the same street."_

1. _She_ → one female person → **Salma** (Grandfather is male).
2. _it_ → one thing that you can ride → **the bike**, not the birthday.
3. _who_ → people, and it stands right after the noun it describes → **her friends**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: taking **the nearest noun** without checking it. Just before _She_, the nearest noun is _her birthday_ — but a birthday cannot ride anything. The right noun matches in number, in kind and in meaning: _She_ = Salma.

::: propriete
To find what a reference word stands for, check three things: **number** (one or more?), **kind** (person or thing?), **meaning** (does the sentence still make sense if you put the noun back?).

Edge case: _they_ can stand for **two nouns together**. _"Marlin and Dory travel far. They cross the ocean."_ → _They_ = Marlin **and** Dory.

:::

::: verifie
_"The volunteers collected the bottles which were on the beach. Then they sent them to a recycling centre."_ What do _which_, _they_ and _them_ refer to?

---

_which_ = **the bottles** (a thing, just before it); _they_ = **the volunteers** (people who can send things); _them_ = **the bottles** (the plural things that were sent). Number, kind and meaning all match.

:::

::: retenir
A reference word matches its noun in **number**, **kind** and **meaning** — never just the nearest noun.

:::

## 📚 Part 1 · Guessing a word from its context

In the Family Life module you read an article about the dangers of pets for small children. The Activity Book asked you to find the words of the text that mean _danger_, _happen_ and _watch_.

In the exam there is no dictionary. When a question asks what a new word means, where do you look?

::: definition
A **word in context** is a new word whose meaning you guess from the **sentence around it**: a synonym close by, an opposite, a cause or a result, an example.

:::

| Clue in the sentence | Sentence                                                            | The new word means |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| a synonym close by   | Pets can be a **hazard**: this danger is real for small children.   | danger             |
| an opposite          | Dog bites are frequent, whereas cat bites are **rare**.             | not frequent       |
| a result             | The dog was **chained** outside all day, so it could not move away. | tied up            |

In each line, the words around the bold one — a synonym, an opposite, a result — give away the meaning written in the right-hand column.

::: exemple
_"Parents should **supervise** their children when they play with a dog: a child must never stay alone with it."_ What does _supervise_ mean: _feed_, _watch_, _punish_ or _leave_?

1. Find the clue: "a child must never stay alone" → an adult must be there, looking.
2. Put each answer in the place of the word → "Parents should **leave** their children" contradicts the clue; _feed_ and _punish_ do not match "never stay alone".
3. Keep the answer that fits the whole sentence → _supervise_ = **watch**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: trusting a word that **looks like French**. _Library_ looks like _librairie_, so many pupils read "a shop that sells books". But the sentence says "you can **borrow** books there for free" — a library is a place where you borrow books. The context wins over the look of the word.

::: methode
Find the clue → replace the word by each answer → keep the one that fits the whole sentence.

Edge case: the same word can change meaning from one text to another. _"The kind of comfort I found here"_ → _kind_ = type; _"The people are so kind"_ → _kind_ = nice. Only the sentence decides.

:::

::: verifie
_"After the storm, the old houses **collapsed**: only piles of stones stayed on the ground."_ What does _collapsed_ mean?

---

**fell down** — the clue is the result that follows: "only piles of stones stayed on the ground". A house that is now a pile of stones has fallen down.

:::

::: retenir
No dictionary in the exam: the **sentence around the word** — synonym, opposite, result — gives its meaning.

:::

## 💭 Part 1 · Inference — reading between the lines

In the Family Life module, Jane writes to a teen magazine: her parents let her go to a party, but her father wants to collect her very early, in front of all her friends. The book asked what matters most to Jane — and the letter never says it.

Yet one answer was right. How can an answer be correct if the text does not write it?

::: definition
An **inference** is a conclusion that the text **suggests** but does not say. It rests on **clues** in the text — never on your own opinion.

:::

Mini-text: _"Rim opened the envelope, read the letter twice, smiled and started to jump and shout. Then she ran to call her parents."_

| Clue in the text            | What it suggests                  |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| she smiled                  | she is pleased                    |
| she jumped and shouted      | she is very excited               |
| she ran to call her parents | she wants to share something good |

No line of the text says "Rim is happy", yet every clue in the left column points to it: that is how an inference is built.

::: exemple
Question: _How does Rim feel?_ — angry, happy, tired or bored?

1. Collect the clues → smiled, jumped, shouted, ran to call her parents.
2. Ask what they have in common → people smile and jump when they are very happy.
3. Check every answer against **all** the clues → _angry_ does not fit "smiled", _tired_ and _bored_ do not fit "jump" → Rim is **happy**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: going **further than the clues**. _"Rim passed her exam"_ is possible — but no clue mentions an exam. The safe inference is the one the clues support: _Rim received good news_.

::: propriete
A good inference is **supported** by at least one clue and **contradicted** by none.

Edge case: an answer copied word for word from the text is a detail, not an inference; and an answer that sounds true in real life but has no clue in the text is **invented**.

:::

::: verifie
_"Ahmed looked at his watch, ran to the bus stop and saw the bus leaving without him."_ What can you infer?

---

**Ahmed was late and missed the bus** — he checks the time and runs (he is in a hurry), and the bus leaves "without him". Every clue supports it, and none contradicts it.

:::

::: retenir
An inference is not written in the text, but **every clue** supports it and **none** contradicts it.

:::

## ⏳ Part 2 · Language — the time marker chooses the tense

In the Tourism module, the book put verbs from the tourists' words in a table — _enjoy_, _came back_, _has made_ — and asked you to name each tense. In the exam, the gap only gives the base verb in brackets: _(visit)_.

Nobody tells you which tense to use. So where is the answer hidden?

::: definition
A **time marker** is a word or group that tells **when** the action happens: _yesterday_, _since 2020_, _next week_… It chooses the tense of the verb in the gap.

:::

| Time marker                                  | Tense            | Form                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ---------------------------- |
| every day, always, usually                   | present simple   | verb / verb + s              |
| yesterday, last…, … ago, in 2019             | simple past      | verb + ed / irregular form   |
| since, ever, just, already, recently, lately | present perfect  | have / has + past participle |
| at 8 o'clock last night (action in progress) | past progressive | was / were + verb + ing      |
| tomorrow, next…, soon                        | future           | will + verb / be going to    |

The left column is what you look for in the sentence; the two columns on the right tell you what to write in the gap.

::: exemple
_"Since 2020, our town ______ (build) three new schools."_

1. Find the time marker → _since 2020_.
2. _Since_ links a past moment to **now** → the **present perfect** (a change between the past and the present).
3. The subject _our town_ is _it_ → **has**.
4. _Build_ is irregular: its past participle is _built_ → _our town **has built** three new schools_.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _"Since 2020, our town **built** three schools."_ ✗ — in French and in Arabic the same idea is said with an ordinary past tense, so the ear accepts it. English needs the present perfect with _since_: _has built_ ✓. And the reverse: _"I **have visited** Dougga last year"_ ✗ — a finished time refuses the present perfect: _I **visited** Dougga last year_ ✓.

::: propriete
Read the whole sentence → find the time marker → choose the tense → build the form (agree with the subject).

Edge case: **if + present → will**. _"If it rains tomorrow, we **will stay** at home."_ The time is future, but after _if_ the verb stays in the present simple (_rains_); _will_ goes only in the result part.

:::

::: verifie
_"My little brother ______ (grow) taller since last summer."_

---

_My little brother **has grown** taller since last summer._ — _since_ links the past to now, so the present perfect: _has_ (he) + the past participle of _grow_, _grown_.

:::

::: retenir
Find the **time marker** first: it chooses the tense, then the subject chooses the form.

:::

## 🗝️ Part 2 · Language — the function chooses the words

In the Services module, a flight attendant asks a passenger for his ticket. She could give an order, but she chooses a polite request. The same idea — _give me your ticket_ — can be an order, a request, a piece of advice or a warning.

In the exam, a sentence may ask: _you want your teacher to repeat the question — what do you say?_ The grammar is easy once you know what the sentence must **do**. So how do you choose?

::: definition
A **function** is what a sentence **does**: advising, asking politely, warning, suggesting, comparing… Each function has its own **patterns** in your book.

:::

| Function       | Patterns of the book                                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| obligation     | must / have to / has to — past: had to — future: will have to       |
| advice         | should / ought to + verb                                            |
| prohibition    | Don't + verb / mustn't                                              |
| possibility    | may / might / could + verb                                          |
| ability        | can / be able to — can't / be unable to                             |
| polite request | Would you mind + verb + ing? / Could you + verb?                    |
| warning        | Mind + noun! / Look out!                                            |
| suggestion     | Let's + verb / Shall we…? / Why don't we…? / What about + verb-ing? |
| comparing      | adjective + er than / more + adjective + than / as + adjective + as |

Read it from the left: first name the function, then take one of its patterns from the right.

::: exemple
_Your teacher speaks fast. You want her to repeat the question, politely._

1. Name the function → you ask someone to do something, politely → **polite request**.
2. Take a pattern of that function → _Would you mind + verb + ing?_
3. Build the form → _repeat_ becomes _repeating_ → _Would you mind **repeating** the question, please?_ (The other pattern works too: _Could you repeat the question, please?_)

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _"Would you mind **to repeat** the question?"_ ✗ — the ear remembers _Would you like **to**…_ and copies it. After _mind_, the verb takes **-ing**: _Would you mind **repeating**…?_ ✓

::: methode
Function → pattern → the form of the verb after it.

Two cases: **advice** → _should_ + the bare verb (_You should rest._); **comparing** → a short adjective takes **-er** (_cheaper than_), a long one takes **more** (_more important than_) — never both (_more cheaper_ ✗).

Edge case: _must_ and _have to_ both express obligation, but in the past only **had to** works: _Yesterday I **had to** walk to school._

:::

::: verifie
It is very hot and your little brother wants to play in the sun all afternoon. Give him a piece of advice.

---

_You **should** stay in the shade._ (or _You ought to drink water._) — the function is **advice**, so _should_ or _ought to_, followed by the bare verb, without _to_ after _should_.

:::

::: retenir
Name the **function** first; it gives you the pattern, and the pattern gives you the verb form.

:::

## 🔗 Part 2 · Language — linkers of cause, result and contrast

In the Health and Environment module, the book took two short sentences — _the air is polluted_ and _factories send out smoke_ — and joined them into one. In the exam, the gap between two ideas often asks for that joining word.

_Because, so, therefore, as a result, that's why, while, whereas_: they look alike. How do you choose the right one?

::: definition
A **linker** connects two parts of a sentence, or two sentences.

- **because** introduces the **cause**;
- **so**, **therefore**, **as a result**, **that's why** introduce the **result**;
- **while** and **whereas** join two **opposite** ideas (**contrast**).

:::

| Linker                                    | What comes after it | Example                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| because                                   | the cause           | We stayed at home **because** it was raining.       |
| so / therefore / as a result / that's why | the result          | It was raining, **so** we stayed at home.           |
| while / whereas                           | an opposite idea    | My brother is noisy, **whereas** my sister is calm. |

The first two lines say the same thing twice: the rain is the cause, staying at home is the result — only the linker and the order change.

::: exemple
_"Many people cycle to work now. ______, the air in our town is cleaner."_

1. Ask what the second part is → cleaner air is the **result** of cycling.
2. A result takes the _so_ family; at the start of a sentence, with a comma → _**As a result,**_ (or _Therefore,_).
3. Reject the others → _because_ would introduce a cause, and _whereas_ needs two opposite ideas.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: putting _because_ in front of the **result**. _"Many people cycle **because** the air is cleaner"_ ✗ turns the story upside down. Ask _why?_ — the answer to _why?_ is the part that follows _because_: the air is cleaner **because** many people cycle ✓.

::: propriete
Cause after **because**; result after **so / therefore / as a result / that's why**; opposite ideas with **while / whereas**.

Edge case: **while** can open the sentence: _"While video games are fun, too many hours of them are harmful."_ Here _while_ means contrast, because _fun_ and _harmful_ are opposites.

:::

::: verifie
_"My father is very patient, ______ my mother gets angry quickly."_ Which linker?

---

**whereas** (or **while**) — _patient_ and _gets angry quickly_ are opposite ideas, so the linker must express **contrast**; _because_ or _so_ would invent a cause or a result that is not there.

:::

::: retenir
**because** + cause · **so / therefore / as a result / that's why** + result · **while / whereas** + opposite idea.

:::

## ✍️ Part 3 · Writing a clear paragraph

In the Health and Environment module, the Activity Book gave you hints about the rainforest — stop logging, protect wild animals — and asked what should and should not be done. Every pupil had the same hints.

Yet some paragraphs got the full mark and others did not. The ideas were the same: so what made the difference?

::: definition
A **paragraph** develops **one** idea. It has a **topic sentence** (general, it announces the idea), **supporting sentences** (reasons, examples, facts, joined with linkers) and a **closing sentence**.

**Unity** means that every sentence speaks about that one idea.

:::

| Role                | Sentence                                                    |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Topic sentence      | We must all protect our forests.                            |
| Supporting sentence | Forests are the home of many wild animals.                  |
| Supporting sentence | They also clean the air we breathe.                         |
| Supporting sentence | People cut too many trees, so some forests might disappear. |
| Closing sentence    | That's why everyone should plant trees and stop logging.    |

The left column is the plan; the right column is the paragraph. Every sentence on the right speaks about forests: that is unity.

::: exemple
Which sentence is the best **topic sentence** for a paragraph about healthy food? _(a) Yesterday I ate an apple. (b) Eating healthy food keeps us fit and strong. (c) That's why you should eat more fruit. (d) Fast food contains a lot of fat._

1. _(a)_ tells one personal event (_yesterday_) → a detail, too narrow to announce a paragraph.
2. _(c)_ opens with _That's why_ → it gives a result, so it closes a paragraph.
3. _(d)_ is one fact → it can support the idea, not announce it.
4. _(b)_ is general and announces the whole idea → **the topic sentence**.

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: adding a sentence because it is true, even though it is **off the topic** — _"My cousin bought a new bike"_ in a paragraph about forests. It fills a line and breaks the unity. Remove every sentence that does not support the topic sentence.

::: methode
Topic sentence → supporting sentences in a logical order, joined with linkers → closing sentence. To continue or re-order a paragraph, each sentence must follow from the one before: no contradiction, no jump to another topic.

Edge case: a sentence opening with _As a result_, _So_ or _That's why_ comes **after** its cause, so it can never open the paragraph.

:::

::: verifie
Put in order: (1) _That's why I take the bus every day._ (2) _It is cheap and comfortable._ (3) _I love going to school by bus._ (4) _It also stops right in front of my school._

---

**3 – 2 – 4 – 1** — (3) is general, so it is the topic sentence; (2) and (4) give the reasons (_also_ adds the second one); (1) opens with _That's why_, so it is the result and closes the paragraph.

:::

::: retenir
One paragraph, one idea: topic sentence → supporting sentences with linkers → closing sentence, and nothing off the topic.

:::
