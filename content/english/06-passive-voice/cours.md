# 🗣️ Communicative Functions — The Words That Open Doors

> 💡 "Grammar builds the sentence. The function decides what it does: ask, warn, thank, suggest, apologise, give your view, react."

## 🙏 Asking politely and offering help

You are on a plane and your bag is too heavy for the shelf above your seat. A flight attendant walks past you in the aisle. A minute later, an old lady behind you cannot close her seat belt.

Two different things happen here: you need someone to do something **for you**, and you want to do something **for someone**. How do you ask without sounding rude — and how do you offer?

::: definition
A **polite request** asks another person to do something for you, politely.

**Offering help** means proposing to do something for another person.

:::

| Function       | Structure                                 | Example                              |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| polite request | **Would you mind** + verb-**ing** …?      | Would you mind closing the window?   |
| polite request | **Could you** + verb …?                   | Could you show me my seat, please?   |
| offering help  | **Can I help you**?                       | Can I help you, madam?               |
| offering help  | **What can I do** for you? / to help you? | What can I do for you?               |
| offering help  | **How can I help you**?                   | How can I help you, sir?             |
| offering help  | **Do you need any help**?                 | Do you need any help with your belt? |

Look at who acts in each row: in the two requests, **you** (the other person) do the action; in the four offers, **I** do it.

::: exemple
Ask the flight attendant to put your bag on the shelf, then help the old lady:

1. Start with **Would you mind** → after _mind_, the verb takes **-ing** → _Would you mind putting my bag on the shelf, please?_
2. Or start with **Could you** → it is followed by the **bare verb**, no _to_ → _Could you put my bag on the shelf, please?_
3. Now **you** are the helper, so the question starts with **I** → _Can I help you, madam?_ or _Do you need any help?_

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Would you mind **to open** the door?_ ✗ — it tempts because many languages put an infinitive after "mind" or "bother". English does not: after **mind**, the verb takes **-ing**. _Would you mind **opening** the door?_ ✓

::: propriete
Ask **who does the action**:

- the other person helps you → a **request**: _Would you mind_ + verb-ing / _Could you_ + bare verb;
- you help the other person → an **offer**: _Can I help you? / What can I do for you? / How can I help you? / Do you need any help?_

Edge case: _Can you help me?_ is **not** an offer. A lost child asks it because **he** needs help; the adult who wants to help answers with _Can I help you?_

:::

::: verifie
Your teacher is carrying a pile of books. Offer help, then ask a classmate politely to open the door.

---

_Can I help you?_ (or _Do you need any help?_) — **I** am the helper, so it is an offer. Then _Would you mind **opening** the door?_ (-ing after _mind_) or _Could you **open** the door?_ (bare verb after _could you_).

:::

::: retenir
Request: **Would you mind + verb-ing? / Could you + verb?** · Offer: **Can I help you? / What can I do for you? / How can I help you? / Do you need any help?**

:::

## ⚠️ Warning people and reading safety signs

Your cousin is about to pay for trainers on a website he has never heard of. At the same moment, your little sister reaches for the iron that you have just used.

You have a second, maybe less, and a long sentence will come too late. How does English warn someone in two or three words — and how does a sign on a wall do it?

::: definition
A **warning** tells someone about a possible **danger or risk**.

- **Mind** + noun phrase! — _Mind the iron!_
- **Look out!** — a cry, usually followed by a sentence that names the danger.
- On a sign, the **imperative**: the bare verb (_Wear gloves!_), or **Don't** + bare verb to forbid (_Don't touch!_).

:::

| Situation                 | Warning                            |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| a hot iron                | **Mind** the iron!                 |
| a low door                | **Mind** your head!                |
| a wet floor               | **Look out!** The floor is wet.    |
| a laboratory              | **Wear** goggles! **Don't** touch! |
| a dangerous building site | **Keep** out!                      |

The left column is the danger; the right column shows the structure that fits it: the danger (or the part of the body at risk) comes straight after **Mind**, a sudden danger gets **Look out!**, and a sign speaks with the bare verb.

::: exemple
Warn your sister and your cousin, step by step:

1. The danger is a thing, _the iron_ → it comes straight after **Mind** → _Mind the iron!_
2. The danger is sudden, you shout first and explain after → _Look out! It's very hot._
3. Your cousin's risk is the website → _Mind the website! It isn't safe._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Mind **to** the iron!_ or _Mind **of** the iron!_ ✗ — it tempts because we say "pay attention **to**" in many languages. **Mind** takes its noun directly, with no preposition: _Mind the iron!_ ✓

::: propriete

- **Mind** + noun phrase: the danger (_Mind the dog!_) or what must be protected (_Mind your fingers!_).
- **Look out!** stands alone; the next sentence says what the danger is.
- A sign uses the bare verb to order (_Wear gloves! Keep out!_) and **Don't** + bare verb to forbid (_Don't spray!_).

Edge case: a sign never adds _to_ or _-ing_ after **Don't**: _Don't smoke!_, not _Don't to smoke!_

:::

::: verifie
Your friend starts to cross the road, but the light is red. Warn him, then write a sign that forbids crossing here.

---

_Look out! The light is red._ (or _Mind the cars!_) — **Look out!** is the cry, the sentence names the danger; after **Mind** the noun comes directly. The sign: _Don't cross!_ — **Don't** + the bare verb forbids.

:::

::: retenir
Warn with **Mind + noun!** or **Look out!** + the danger · signs: bare verb (**Wear gloves!**) or **Don't + verb** (**Don't touch!**).

:::

## 🤝 Thanking

At the airport, a police officer checks your passport. You tell him you have an urgent call to make and nowhere to leave your suitcase. He says: _I'll take it to the check-in desk for you._

"Thank you" is correct, but it feels short for so much kindness. How do you thank someone warmly — and what does the other person say back?

::: definition
**Thanking** means showing someone that you are grateful for what they do or will do for you.

:::

| When                      | You say                                             |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| the help is done          | **Thank you (very much). That's very kind of you.** |
| the help is done          | **It's very nice of you.**                          |
| the help is still to come | **That will be very kind / nice of you.**           |
| the other person answers  | **You're welcome.**                                 |

The first column tells you which line to pick: the help is already done → _That's / It's_; the help is only promised → _That **will be**_.

::: exemple
Thank the police officer, step by step:

1. He has only **promised** to help → the help is still to come → _That will be very kind of you._
2. He comes back: the suitcase is at the desk → the help is done → _Thank you very much. That's very kind of you._
3. He answers your thanks → _You're welcome._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _That's very kind **for** you._ ✗ — it tempts because the help was _for_ you. But the kindness belongs to the **other person**, and English says so with **of**: _That's very kind **of** you._ ✓

::: propriete

- **kind / nice + of + person**: _It's very nice **of** you._
- **That's** for help already given, **That will be** for help that is coming.
- The answer to thanks is **You're welcome.**

Edge case: _Here you are._ is **not** an answer to thanks. You say it when you **hand** something to someone (your passport, your ticket).

:::

::: verifie
Your neighbour says: "I'll water your plants during your holiday." Thank her.

---

_That will be very kind of you._ — the help is **still to come**, so **will be**; and the kindness is hers, so **of** you. When you come back and the plants are fine: _Thank you very much. That's very kind of you._

:::

::: retenir
**Thank you (very much). That's very kind of you.** · help still to come: **That will be very kind of you.** · answer: **You're welcome.**

:::

## 💡 Making suggestions

Next Tuesday is a holiday and your group of friends wants to go out. One wants the zoo, another the beach, a third the fun fair — and each one starts the idea with different words.

All of them are suggesting, but the verb after each opener is not the same. Which form comes after which opener?

::: definition
A **suggestion** proposes an idea of something to do, often together.

:::

| Opener             | What follows              | Example                                                      |
| ------------------ | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Let's**          | bare verb                 | Let's go to the fun fair.                                    |
| **Shall we**       | bare verb …?              | Shall we go to the fun fair?                                 |
| **Why don't we**   | bare verb …?              | Why don't we go to the fun fair?                             |
| **What about**     | verb-**ing** or a noun …? | What about going to the fun fair? / What about the fun fair? |
| **I suggest that** | a clause: subject + verb  | I suggest that we go to the fun fair.                        |

Read the middle column: three openers take the **bare verb**, _What about_ takes **-ing** (or a noun), and _I suggest that_ takes a **whole clause** with its own subject.

::: exemple
Suggest the fun fair to your friends, step by step:

1. **Let's** is followed by the bare verb → _Let's go to the fun fair._ (a full stop: it is not a question)
2. **Why don't we** and **Shall we** also take the bare verb, but they are questions → _Why don't we go…?_
3. **What about** ends with the little word _about_; after it, a verb takes **-ing** → _What about going to the fun fair?_
4. **I suggest that** needs a subject and a verb → _I suggest that **we go** to the fun fair._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _What about **go** to the beach?_ ✗ and _Let's **going**._ ✗ — the openers mean the same thing, so their forms get mixed. Keep them apart: **What about** + **-ing** (_What about going…?_), **Let's** + bare verb (_Let's go._) ✓

::: propriete

- bare verb after **Let's**, **Shall we**, **Why don't we**;
- **-ing** or a noun after **What about**;
- a full clause after **I suggest that**: _I suggest that we **take** the bus._

Edge case: **Why don't we…?** looks like a negative question, but it does not complain or ask for a reason — it is a friendly suggestion.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "What about ___ (visit) the museum?" and "Why don't we ___ (take) the train?"

---

_What about **visiting** the museum?_ — after _about_ the verb takes **-ing**. _Why don't we **take** the train?_ — _Why don't we_ is followed by the **bare verb**.

:::

::: retenir
**Let's / Shall we / Why don't we** + bare verb · **What about** + verb-ing / noun · **I suggest that** + subject + verb.

:::

## 😔 Apologising and expressing regret

A little clown fish ignores his father's warnings and swims to the open sea. Far from home, he knows he was wrong. And today your friend invites you to the cinema, but you have to stay home and study.

In both stories someone must say sorry — once for something already done, once for a refusal. How do you apologise, and how do you say you regret an action?

::: definition
**Apologising / expressing regret** means saying that you are sorry about something.

- **I'm sorry.** / **I'm so (very) sorry that** + clause.
- **I regret** + a noun, or **I regret** + verb-**ing**.

:::

| Structure                         | Example                                   |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| I'm sorry.                        | I'm sorry. I can't join you.              |
| I'm so / very sorry that + clause | I'm so sorry that I forgot your birthday. |
| I regret + noun                   | I regret my mistake.                      |
| I regret + verb-ing               | I regret missing the concert.             |

The right column shows the bridge: after **that** comes a full clause (subject + verb); after **regret** comes either a thing (a noun) or an action with **-ing**.

::: exemple
The little fish finds his father again. Build his apology, step by step:

1. **I'm so sorry that** needs a full clause: subject + verb → _I'm so sorry that I didn't listen to you._
2. He names the **thing** he regrets → a noun after _regret_ → _I regret my mistake._
3. He names the **action** he regrets → verb-**ing** after _regret_ → _I regret swimming to the open sea._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _Yesterday I was ill. I regret **to miss** your party._ ✗ — it tempts because "to" + verb follows so many verbs. For an action you already did, **regret** takes **-ing**: _I regret **missing** your party._ ✓

::: propriete

- **I'm so / very sorry that** + subject + verb.
- **I regret** + noun (_my mistake_) or + verb-ing (_disobeying my father_).

Edge case: **I'm sorry.** can stand alone to refuse an invitation politely: _Would you like to come with me? — I'm sorry. I can't join you._

:::

::: verifie
Yesterday you forgot to bring back your friend's book. Apologise in two ways.

---

_I'm so sorry that I forgot your book._ — after **that**, a full clause (_I forgot_). _I regret forgetting your book._ — for an action already done, **regret** + verb-**ing**.

:::

::: retenir
**I'm (so / very) sorry (that) + clause** · **I regret + noun / verb-ing**.

:::

## 🤔 Sure or not sure, your opinion, your hope

Jack is not at training today. Linda says he is perhaps ill; Karim is completely sure he forgot the time, because he always does. Then the group talks about volunteering, and about next year.

Every sentence opens with a small signal that tells the listener **how sure** the speaker is, or that this is **their own view**, or **what they wish for**. Which signal for which meaning?

::: definition

- **Uncertainty** (not sure): **Perhaps / Maybe** + sentence.
- **Certainty** (sure): **There's no doubt** + sentence.
- **Opinion**: **I think that / I believe that** + clause · **In my opinion,** + sentence.
- **Hope**: **I hope that** + clause.

:::

| You are…              | Opener           | Example                                   |
| --------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| not sure              | Perhaps / Maybe  | Perhaps Jack is ill.                      |
| sure                  | There's no doubt | There's no doubt he forgot the time.      |
| giving your view      | I think that     | I think that helping others is important. |
| giving your view      | In my opinion,   | In my opinion, clubs are useful.          |
| wishing for something | I hope that      | I hope that Jack will come next time.     |

Each opener is followed by a **complete sentence** (subject + verb): the opener only adds how sure, whose view, or what wish.

::: exemple
Jack is absent. Build the four sentences, step by step:

1. Linda is **not sure** → _Perhaps_ + a full sentence → _Perhaps Jack is ill._
2. Karim is **sure** → _There's no doubt_ + a full sentence → _There's no doubt he forgot the time._
3. Your **view** on volunteering → _I think that_ + clause; its subject can be a verb-ing → _I think that **helping others** is important._
4. Your **wish** → _I hope that_ + subject + verb → _I hope that Jack will come next time._

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I hope **you to** pass your exam._ ✗ — it tempts because _I want you to pass_ is correct. **Hope** is followed by a **that-clause**: _I hope that you **pass** your exam._ ✓ (or _will pass_)

::: propriete

- **Perhaps / Maybe** → you are **not sure**; **There's no doubt** → you are **sure**.
- **I think that / I believe that / In my opinion,** → your **view**; _In my opinion_ takes a comma.
- **I hope that** + subject + verb → your **wish**.

Edge case: the subject of the clause can be a verb-ing or a noun: _I believe that **volunteering** is a great experience._

:::

::: verifie
You don't know where Salma is. Say it; then give your view about school clubs.

---

_Maybe Salma is in the library._ — **Maybe** + a full sentence shows you are **not sure**. _In my opinion, school clubs are useful._ (or _I think that school clubs are useful._) — the opener marks **your view**, and a full sentence follows.

:::

::: retenir
Not sure: **Perhaps / Maybe** · sure: **There's no doubt** · view: **I think that / I believe that / In my opinion,** · wish: **I hope that** + clause.

:::

## 😲 Reacting: surprise, satisfaction, interest, indifference

At the stadium you meet a classmate by chance. Later, in a shop, the assistant shows you exactly the jacket you wanted. At night a friend tells you about a film festival — and asks if you prefer the cinema or TV; you really don't prefer either.

Four reactions, four feelings. How do you show each one in English?

::: definition

- **Exclamations**: **What a** (+ adjective) + noun! · **How** + adjective! · **How nice to** + verb! · **It's a surprise! · It's amazing! · What a pity!**
- **Satisfaction**: **This is just what I wanted / needed / was looking for.**
- **Interest**: **I'm interested in** + noun / verb-ing · **It sounds interesting.** · **I'd like to know more about** + noun.
- **Indifference**: **I don't mind** (+ verb-ing) · **I don't care.**

:::

| Feeling        | Example                                         |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| surprise       | What a surprise! How nice to see you here!      |
| admiration     | What a beautiful jacket! How wonderful!         |
| disappointment | What a pity! The shop is closed.                |
| satisfaction   | This is just what I wanted!                     |
| interest       | I'm interested in films. It sounds interesting. |
| indifference   | I don't mind staying at home. I don't care.     |

Each row joins a feeling to its structure: **What a** before a noun, **How** before an adjective, **interested** for the person who feels it, **interesting** for the thing that causes it.

::: exemple
Live the evening, step by step:

1. At the stadium: a noun follows, so **What a** → _What a surprise!_; then **How nice to** + verb → _How nice to see you here!_
2. In the shop: the jacket is exactly right → _This is just what I wanted!_
3. The festival: the thing causes interest → _It sounds interesting._ You want details → _I'd like to know more about it._
4. Cinema or TV? You have no preference → _I don't mind._ (with a verb: _I don't mind staying at home._)

:::

> ⚠️ **Common mistake**: _I'm **interesting** in music._ ✗ — both words are in the lesson, so they get swapped. The **person** who feels the interest is **interested**; the **thing** that gives it is **interesting**: _I'm **interested** in music. It sounds **interesting**._ ✓

::: propriete

- **What a** + (adjective) + noun: _What a lovely present!_ · **How** + adjective: _How nice!_
- **I'm interested in** + noun / verb-ing · **I don't mind** + verb-ing.

Edge case: **What a pity!** is an exclamation, but it shows **disappointment**, not joy: _We can't go on the trip. What a pity!_

:::

::: verifie
Your aunt gives you the exact book you needed. React, then say you want details about its author.

---

_What a lovely present! This is just what I needed!_ — **What a** + adjective + noun is the exclamation, and _just what I needed_ shows **satisfaction**. _I'd like to know more about the author._ — **I'd like to know more about** + noun shows **interest**.

:::

::: retenir
**What a …! / How …! / What a pity!** · satisfaction: **just what I wanted** · interest: **I'm interested in / It sounds interesting** · indifference: **I don't mind / I don't care**.

:::

> 🏆 Gate cleared. You can now ask, offer, warn, thank, suggest, apologise, say how sure you are, give your view, hope, and react — the everyday moves of every dialogue on the exam. Keep these openers close: every reading passage from now on is full of them.
