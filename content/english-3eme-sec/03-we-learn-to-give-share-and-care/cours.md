# ⚔️ We Learn to Give, Share and Care — The Two Pasts and the Words for Those Who Give

> 💡 "A community is not measured by what it owns. It is measured by what it hands over — and by how exactly it can say so."

Giving has its own vocabulary, and English builds most of it out of words you already have: from an act it makes the **name of the person who performs it**, from two words it makes **one adjective**, and from a small verb plus a small particle it makes a meaning neither of them had. This module hands you that machinery — and, at its centre, the one contrast that decides more marks than any other in your year: **the present perfect against the simple past**.

## 🎁 The vocabulary of giving

Eleven words sit at the centre of the topic. Learn each one **with its definition**, because the exam gives you the definition and asks for the word.

| Word                                     | What it names                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **philanthropy**                         | concern for human welfare and advancement, shown by giving money, property or work                  |
| **altruism**                             | unselfish concern for the welfare of others — the opposite of **egoism**                            |
| **activism**                             | vigorous action or involvement as a way of achieving something                                      |
| **volunteerism** (also *voluntarism*)    | giving your time or your talents to charitable or educational work, especially in your own community |
| **charity**                              | generous actions or donations to help the poor, the ill or the **helpless**                          |
| **donation**                             | one act of presenting something as a gift, a grant or a contribution                                |
| **self-sacrifice**                       | giving up your own interests or desires for the good of somebody else                               |
| **generosity**                           | readiness to give — the opposite of **meanness**                                                     |
| **solidarity**                           | fellowship arising from responsibilities and interests people share                                 |
| **endowment**                            | providing a permanent fund whose income pays for something year after year                          |
| **fund-raising**                         | soliciting contributions; raising money                                                             |

Four more turn up in every text on the subject: **benevolence** (the wish to do good), the **common good**, the **welfare** of a population, and **the needy** — an adjective used as a plural noun, like *the homeless*.

> 🗡️ **in-kind contributions** are goods, commodities or services given **instead of money** — blankets, medicine, a lorry, an afternoon of work. In Tunisia they are what most families actually give: food during Ramadhan, clothes and covers in a hard winter, books and school bags in September, blood on 8 April.

## 🧱 The person who does it — agent nouns in -er, -or and -ist

English names the doer by hanging a suffix on the act. Three suffixes do nearly all the work.

| The act                        | The person             | Suffix   |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------- | -------- |
| donate                         | a **donor**            | **-or**  |
| endow an institution           | an **endower**         | **-er**  |
| raise funds                    | a **fund-raiser**      | **-er**  |
| sacrifice one's own interests  | a **self-sacrificer**  | **-er**  |
| philanthropy                   | a **philanthropist**   | **-ist** |
| activism                       | an **activist**        | **-ist** |
| altruism                       | an **altruist**        | **-ist** |

Two regularities make the choice less blind than it looks:

1. a noun ending in **-ism** almost always gives its person in **-ist**: *activism → activist*, *altruism → altruist*, *egoism → egoist*;
2. a **verb** usually gives its person in **-er**, and a small closed group takes **-or** instead: *donate → donor*, *act → actor*, *visit → visitor*.

> ⚠️ Three traps. "donator" ✗ → *donor* (the verb loses its ending before **-or**). "philanthroper" ✗ → *philanthropist*. And **volunteer** is its own person-word — there is no "volunteerer" ✗: the same form is the verb (*to volunteer*) and the person. Facing the giver stands the **beneficiary**: the one who receives.

## 🔗 Compound adjectives — two words, one hyphen, one meaning

Put a hyphen between two words in front of a noun and you have built an adjective.

| Pattern                      | Example                        | It means                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| noun + **-ing**              | a **fund-raising** concert     | a concert that raises funds                       |
| adverb + past participle     | a **well-run** charity         | a charity that somebody runs well                 |
| adverb + **-ing**            | **ever-increasing** costs      | costs that never stop rising                      |
| a whole phrase               | **state-of-the-art** equipment | equipment as modern as anything that exists       |
| adjective + **-ing**         | **hard-working** volunteers    | volunteers who work hard                          |

> ⚠️ Inside a compound adjective the first word **never takes the plural -s**: "a funds-raising dinner" ✗ → _a **fund-raising** dinner_ ✓. And the hyphen belongs to the adjective in front of its noun: once nothing follows, it goes — _the costs are ever increasing._

## 💪 The emphatic form — insisting without raising your voice

When somebody doubts what you are saying, English lets you push the fact back at them by adding **do**, **does** or **did** in front of the base verb.

```
do / does / did  +  base verb
```

- _I **do understand** your doubts, but the figures are on the table._
- _She **did send** the form; I saw the envelope leave._
- _The committee **does publish** its accounts, every single year._

Three conditions, and the mark depends on all three:

1. the sentence is **affirmative** — in a negative or a question, *do* is the ordinary auxiliary and carries no insistence at all;
2. the tense is the **simple present** or the **simple past** — nothing else;
3. the verb that follows goes back to its **base form**: *does need*, never "does needs" ✗; *did give*, never "did gave" ✗.

> ⚠️ When the sentence already has an auxiliary — **be**, a modal, a perfect — you do **not** add *do*. The insistence falls on the auxiliary itself: _She **has** paid._ · _They **are** here._ · _We **can** manage._ "do are" ✗ · "do can" ✗

> 🗡️ A speaker can also insist by **putting the important word first**: _**Now** is the time to work harder._ The word order does the work the raised voice would do.

## 🆘 need — an ordinary full verb, and the one the speech insists on

*need* behaves like any other full verb, and that is the whole rule.

| You want to say         | Structure                    | Example                                          |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| you lack a thing        | **need + noun**              | _The dispensary **needs** a second nurse._       |
| you must do something   | **need + to + base verb**    | _Volunteers **need to** sign the register._      |
| you lack nothing        | **don't / doesn't need**     | _They **don't need** more blankets._             |
| you ask                 | **do / does + need**         | _**Does** she **need** a lift on Saturday?_      |

Which is why the two points meet in one famous line of a fund-raising speech: _Our hospital system **does need** more money._

> ⚠️ No *to* before a noun: "It needs to a second nurse" ✗. And *need* is a verb of state, so it refuses the continuous: "They are needing help" ✗ → _They **need** help_ ✓. The noun is *a need* — _in times of **need**_ — and the adjective is *needy*.

## 🧩 Phrasal verbs — a verb plus a particle, and a brand-new meaning

| Phrasal verb                | Meaning                                      |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **care for** / **look after** | take care of somebody                       |
| **give up**                 | abandon                                      |
| **grow up**                 | attain mental or physical maturity           |
| **catch up with**           | meet people you have not seen for a while    |
| **win** somebody **over**   | gain their favour and their support          |
| **stay up**                 | remain awake and out of bed                  |
| **come round**              | pay somebody an informal visit               |
| **take over**               | assume responsibility for something          |
| **gossip about**            | talk about other people's affairs            |
| **bring out**               | cause something in somebody to appear        |

**Where the object goes** — some of these split around their object, and some never do.

- **Separable**: _She won the whole hall **over**._ = _She won **over** the whole hall._ But a **pronoun must go in the middle**: _She won them **over**_ ✓ · "She won over them" ✗. Same for *give up*: _He gave it **up**_ ✓.
- **Inseparable**: *care for*, *look after*, *come round*, *grow up*, *catch up with* keep verb and particle glued: _She cares **for** her grandmother_ ✓ · "She cares her grandmother for" ✗.

> 🗡️ The meaning is never the sum of the parts. *give up* has nothing to do with giving and *bring out* nothing to do with carrying. Meet each one inside a sentence, and keep the sentence.

## ⏳ should have + past participle — the sensible thing that did not happen

```
should  +  have  +  past participle
```

It looks back and says: **that was the right thing to do, and it did not happen.**

- _They **should have asked** before they emptied the store room._ (they did not ask)
- _Her eighteenth birthday **should have marked** a milestone in her life._ (it did not)

The negative says the opposite — the thing **did** happen, and it was wrong: _They **shouldn't have left** her on her own._

| Sentence                                    | When            | What it says                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| _You **should give** blood._                | now, the future | advice: it is the right thing to do              |
| _You **should have given** blood in April._ | the past        | it was the right thing, and you did not do it    |

> ⚠️ The participle is compulsory: "should have go" ✗ → _should have **gone**_ ✓. And never read this structure as a report: *should have* never says the thing happened.

## 🕰️ The present perfect against the simple past

This is the table of the year. Learn it once, properly, and half the language exercises of the programme fall on their own.

|             | Present perfect                                     | Simple past                                  |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Form**    | **have / has + past participle**                    | the past form, regular (**-ed**) or irregular |
| **Example** | _The club **has repaired** forty bicycles so far._  | _Last March it **repaired** nine._            |

**The four uses of the present perfect** — all four share one idea: the past is **still joined to today**.

1. an action or situation that **started in the past and has continued** up to the present: _He **has driven** the association's van since 2019._
2. one that started in the past and went on **until very recently**: _I **have just come back** from the shelter._
3. an action **repeated in a period that reaches the present**: _They **have written** to the town hall three times this year._
4. an action **just finished**, or one whose **result** counts now: _The doctor **has arrived**._ (she is here)

**The simple past** is the tense most often used to talk about the past. It covers **short, quickly finished actions**, **longer past actions and situations**, and **repeated happenings** — and, decisively, it is the tense that goes with **a time that is named and finished**.

> ⚠️ **The present perfect refuses a finished time-period.** *last week, yesterday, in 2002, two years ago, when I was five* all shut the door on it: "The bank has welcomed two hundred donors last April" ✗ → _**welcomed**_ ✓.

**The words that call each tense**

| Present perfect                                  | Simple past                       |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| up to now · so far · all this year · all my life | yesterday · last week · in 2002   |
| recently · lately · already · just               | two years ago · then · that day   |
| ever · never · yet                               | at eight o'clock · on Friday      |

**since and for** — the pair that decides the hardest questions.

- **since** gives the **point where the period begins**; **for** gives **how long it lasts**: _since 2014_ · _for nine years_.
- Both open a period that reaches today, so the main verb is a **present perfect**: _The centre **has kept** its doors open **since** 2014._ · _… **for** nine years._
- When *since* introduces a **clause**, that clause takes the **simple past** while the main verb stays in the present perfect: _She **has volunteered** at the shelter **since she left** school._

> ⚠️ **The trap of this module, and it is worth naming.** A sentence such as _She **has done** so **since she was five**_ is a **present perfect**, whatever the past form *was* in the second half suggests. Read the **main** verb: *has* + *done*. The past form after *since* only marks where an open period begins. Two errors follow from misreading it: "She does so since she was five" ✗ and "She did so since she was five" ✗ → _She **has done** so since she was five_ ✓.

> 🗡️ The question you are asked names the tense you must answer with. **How long has she…?** opens a period → present perfect. **When did she…?** asks for a finished moment → simple past.

## 🔮 The other simple past — the one that is not about the past

The same past form does a second job that has nothing to do with time. After seven expressions it describes something **unreal, imagined or overdue** — now or in the future.

| Trigger                 | Example                                                        |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **if**                  | _**If** she **were** like me, we could go out together._        |
| **suppose**             | _**Suppose** everybody **gave** one dinar a month._             |
| **as if**               | _He talks **as if** he **knew** every family in the street._    |
| **if only**             | _**If only** the centre **had** a second van._                  |
| **it's (high) time**    | _**It's high time** this street **had** a proper pavement._     |
| **would rather**        | _I'd **rather** you **came** with me on Saturday._              |
| **wish**                | _I **wish** I **had** more time for them._                      |

> ⚠️ This past is not a past. "I wish I have more time" ✗ → _I **wish** I **had**_ ✓. And after *it's high time* the present is impossible: "It's high time we do something" ✗ → _we **did** something_ ✓.

## 🎤 A speech that persuades

A speech has one of three purposes: to **entertain**, to **inform**, or to **persuade**. A fund-raising speech persuades, and it follows a shape.

1. **Greet** the audience and **catch their attention**.
2. **Introduce** the organisation and its **key mission**.
3. **Develop** the key point — figures, examples, what the money actually buys.
4. **State the counterargument** the audience is already forming, then **refute** it.
5. **Call for action** and **close**.

**Four ways of catching an audience** — use one or two, never all four: **ask a question** · **tell an anecdote** (a very short funny story) · **cite a quote** · **give statistics**.

> 🗡️ The counterargument move is what separates a speech from a list. You say out loud what the doubters are thinking, and then you knock it down. Refusing to name the objection does not make it disappear — it leaves it working quietly in the audience's head.

> 🔊 A speaker pauses at the end of a **thought group**, a group of words that expresses one thought, then again at the end of the next one, then at the end of the sentence. The pauses follow the sense, not the commas printed on the page.

## 🧭 Discourse markers — showing the joints of your text

| The move                     | Markers                                                                             |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **open a list**              | firstly · first of all · to begin with · to start with · in the first place · for one thing |
| **continue it**              | secondly · thirdly · for another thing · another thing is                           |
| **add**                      | moreover · in addition · similarly · as well as that · on top of that               |
| **add something stronger**   | besides                                                                              |
| **place in time**            | earlier · later · before that · after that                                          |
| **close**                    | finally                                                                              |

> ⚠️ **besides** is not a neutral *and*: it announces an argument you consider **stronger** than the one before it. And *firstly* promises a *secondly* — if nothing is going to follow, do not open with it.

> 🏆 You can now name the act, name the person who performs it, build an adjective out of two words, insist on a fact without shouting, look back at what should have happened — and, above all, choose between the two pasts without hesitating. That last choice is the one your examiner will ask for most often, in this module and in every one after it.
