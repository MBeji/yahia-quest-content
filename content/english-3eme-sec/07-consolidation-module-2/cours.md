# ⚔️ Consolidation Module 2 — Nine Tasks, Two Modules, and One Quarrel Nobody Settles for You

> 💡 "A revision paper never asks you the question you revised. It asks the same knowledge in a shape you have not met — and then, once, it asks you what you think."

Modules Three and Four are behind you: the holiday you compared, placed and told, and the science page you learnt to read without being caught. This module puts both back on the table as **nine tasks** — a paragraph to rebuild, a brochure to fill, fifteen words to sort and to say aloud, two arguments about genetic engineering to read, a welcome to complete, a news item to put into the right tenses, seven sentence halves to finish, and a text that arrived with no marks at all. Every one of the nine has an answer. But two of them — the two arguments about genetic engineering — are followed by a question that has none: your book prints both sides and asks which one convinces you. It does not tell you. Neither will this chapter: what it gives you is the language you need to hold either side without stumbling.

## 🧵 An argument that holds together

Seven sentences are printed out of order, and the book has already dropped one of them — the opening claim, the one it prints in italics — into the first square. The order of the other six is not a matter of taste: each sentence carries a mark that says where it can and cannot stand.

| The mark on the sentence                                | What it tells you                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| it opens on **This**, **That**, **Such**                | it develops the sentence before it and cannot come first  |
| it opens on **What is more**, **Furthermore**, **Besides** | it adds to a point already made                         |
| it opens on **On the other hand**, **However**          | it turns to the opposite side, so a first side must exist |
| it opens on **While** or **Although** + a good point    | it concedes something before objecting                   |
| it carries a **pronoun with nobody to point at**        | the person or thing was named in an earlier sentence      |
| it states a **claim and points at nothing behind it**   | nothing has to be said before it, so it can stand first   |

> 🗡️ **The safe method is to look for the opening, not for the order.** Read all the sentences and cross out every one that carries a mark. What is left is your first line, and the rest builds itself: ask of each remaining sentence *what has to be said before this one makes sense?* In this activity the book has done that first step for you and printed the opener in italics — so here the method is your **check**, not your discovery: if the sentence you would have chosen is not the one in the square, you have misread a mark.

> ⚠️ **Adding and turning are two different jobs, and the words are not interchangeable.** *What is more* and *Furthermore* pile a second point on the same side. *On the other hand* leaves that side and opens the other one. A paragraph that says "the fees are low. **Furthermore**, the hours are inconvenient" has put a drawback where the reader was promised another advantage, and the reader stumbles. This is the single most common fault in a rebuilt paragraph.

**Conceding, then objecting.** *While it is good to have a games console at home, it may also mean the end of conversation for whole evenings.* The **While** half grants the good point; the main half objects. It is the politest move in argument, and it is worth one sentence of any paragraph you write.

## 🏨 The brochure, and the words it lives on

Module Three gave you the hotel; this module makes you fill a brochure with its own vocabulary.

| Word              | What it does in a brochure                                                       |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **leisure**       | the time you are free to do as you please — a **leisure club**, a **leisure centre** |
| **to relax**      | to let go of the effort of the day                                               |
| **to spend**      | to pass a stretch of time somewhere — *after **spending** a long day on your feet* |
| **a sauna**       | a very hot dry room you sit in to sweat                                          |
| **a magazine**    | what you read in the armchair, with a light snack                                |
| **beauty**        | care given to the face, the skin, the hair — a **beauty salon**                  |
| **to book**       | to keep a place for yourself before you arrive                                   |

Around them stand the rest of the equipment, in the words the brochure itself uses: a **spa bath**, a **steam room**, a **fitness room**, a **hairdresser**, and an **award winning** salon that is always busy. Two more are worth keeping: a chair is **comfy** (the everyday word for *comfortable*), and the club is **well-equipped**.

> 🗡️ **leisure** is a stretch of **time**, not a kind of amusement. A *leisure club* is not a club that amuses you; it is the club you use in the hours that belong to you. That is why a hotel puts the pool, the sauna and the gym in it, and puts the restaurant somewhere else.

> ⚠️ **to book** is the verb English uses for keeping a place in advance, and it can stand with no object at all: *be sure to **book** in advance* ✓. It has nothing to do with the noun *a book*, and it is not *to demand*, which is a much stronger word meaning to insist that something be given to you — "demand a table in advance" ✗ asks the manager for a fight. The everyday alternative is *to **reserve** a table*, and the noun of the pair is *a **booking*** or *a **reservation***.

## 🗺️ Reading the situation of a text

Two of the nine tasks stop in the middle and ask you not about the language but about the **situation**: who wrote this, for whom, and when. That is a real skill, and it is answered from inside the text.

| The question                | What you look for                                                              |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **What type of text is it?** | the genre — a brochure, an advertisement, a letter, a diary, a news report      |
| **Where would you read it?**  | the place the genre lives in — a hotel room, a newspaper, an envelope, a notebook |
| **Who is the speaker?**      | the person the *I* or the *we* stands for                                       |
| **Who is the audience?**     | the person or group the *you* stands for                                        |
| **What is the occasion?**    | the moment that made the text necessary                                         |

Three marks give the situation away almost every time. A text that says **we** and praises its own facilities is selling something. A text that says **you** and gives times, prices and instructions expects you to act. A text in the **past** and in the **first person singular** is telling you what happened to one person.

> 🗡️ **A welcome and a complaint use the same vocabulary and face opposite ways.** *Our leisure club is open from seven* and *your leisure club was closed all week* draw on one word list. What separates them is the tense, the pronoun and the purpose — so read those three before you decide what the page is.

> ⚠️ The task that completes half-written words prints a row of dots and tells you that **each dot stands for one letter**. Count the dots before you write anything: they rule out every word of the right meaning and the wrong length.

## 🔤 Two columns, and where the beat falls

Fifteen words are handed to you in one list and asked to fall into two columns, **Entertainment** and **Science / Technology**.

| Entertainment                                                       | Science / Technology                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| camping · hiking · canoeing · sightseeing · concerts · opera · amusement parks | insulin · cloning · genes · laser · DNA · contact lenses · DVD · hard disc |

Sort by asking **what the word names**, not what it took to make it. *Contact lenses* amuse nobody: they are a piece of applied optics, so they go on the right. *An opera* needs engineers to build its house and electricians to light it, and it is still an evening out, so it goes on the left.

Then the list is asked aloud, and you mark the syllable that carries the beat.

**The rule that saves you on this list: the ending -ing does not move the beat.** It adds a syllable and leaves the stress where the base word already had it. *CAMP → CAMPing* · *HIKE → HIKing* · *CLONE → CLOning* · *SIGHTsee → SIGHTseeing*. And the same rule is what gives you *caNOE → caNOEing*: the beat stays on the second syllable because *canoe* had it there to begin with. Do not read the first four as a rule of their own — the base word decides, and -ing only follows. The ending **-ment** behaves in the same way: *aMUSE → aMUSEment*.

> ⚠️ **That is not true of every ending**, and you already know one that behaves differently: **-tion** and **-sion** pull the beat onto the syllable in front of them, which is why *EDucate* becomes *eduCAtion*. So the question is never "where does the stress go in English?" but "what does **this** ending do?" — and the two answers you need here are that *-ing* and *-ment* leave it alone while *-tion* and *-sion* move it.

> 🗡️ Most of the rest of the list keep the beat on their first syllable — *INsulin*, *OPera*, *CONcerts*, *LAser*, *CONtact lenses*, *HARD disc* — and *genes* has only the one syllable to give it to. The awkward one to say is *amusement parks*, because the beat of the first word is in the middle: *aMUSEment parks*.

> ⚠️ **Two words in this list go the other way, and they are the two sets of initials.** A word spelt out letter by letter takes its beat on the **last** letter: *D-N-**A***, *D-V-**D***. The same is true of initials outside this list — *the U-S-**A***, *an M-**P***. So a pupil who has learnt "English puts the beat at the front" will say those two wrongly every time, and this is the moment to unlearn it.

## ⚖️ Genetic engineering — the two texts, side by side

The heart of the module is two short arguments printed one after the other, and then a single question: **which of the two do you agree with most?** Your book gives no answer, and it is right not to. Know both, and the answer you give will be worth something.

**The case for strict control.** Life has **evolved** slowly, over thousands of years. Changing that process too fast, by touching the genes — **the building blocks of nature** — could do terrible damage. Where will scientists who are allowed to *play God* stop, and can they control what they set in motion? And what use might a **government** make of genetics: an attempt to produce *the perfect baby*, or a population bred to a chosen pattern — the phrase the text uses is *a master-race*.

**The case for going on.** The advances are good news. Doctors will soon be able to use genetic engineering **to help fight disease**, and to tell you that a problem runs in your family and could be passed on to your children. The genes may then be **altered** and the disease cured. Genetics matter in **agriculture** too: plants that resist disease matter **particularly in poor countries**, where a bad harvest is not a loss of money but hunger. Animals can be bred to produce more food and are therefore more **profitable**. And to the objection that scientific progress is *unnatural*, the answer is that scientists can be trusted to use the knowledge **responsibly** — and that people protested about things like **transplant surgery** in the past, and most are in favour of it now.

> 🗡️ **Notice what the second text does with the transplant.** It does not claim that genetics and transplants are the same technique. It claims something narrower and much harder to answer: that an objection resting on *this feels unnatural* has been made before, about something we now accept without a second thought. Answering an objection by showing that it has a poor record is one of the strongest moves in argument, and it costs one sentence.

> ⚠️ **And notice what the first text does with the metaphor.** *The building blocks of nature* is not decoration. A building block is the smallest piece that everything above it rests on, so the metaphor argues that a small change low down can bring a great deal down with it. Read every metaphor in an argumentative text as a claim, because that is what it is.

## 🧱 The language those two texts are written in

**Listing your reasons.** A writer who has three points to make marks them so that the reader can count. The first of the two texts uses **To begin with**, … **furthermore**, … **Lastly**, … ; the second uses **In the first place**, … **Secondly**, … **Finally**, … . Three jobs, three places: one phrase opens the list, one adds to it, one closes it — and the two sets are interchangeable as long as you do not mix a middle marker into the opening slot. *For example* and *For instance* do something else entirely: they illustrate a point you have already made instead of adding a new one, so a reader counting your arguments will not count them.

> 🗡️ **A marker of addition can stand in the middle of its sentence.** *I worry, **furthermore**, where scientists will stop* — the phrase has not stopped doing its job by moving; it is still announcing the second reason, and the two commas are what let it sit there. So when you are asked to find the phrases that list the arguments, do not look only at the first word of each sentence.

**Hedging.** An argument about the future is rarely stated flat. **might** and **could** say that a thing is possible; **will** says the writer expects it; **must** states a conclusion he is sure of. *Touching the genes **might** cause terrible damage* is a warning. *Touching the genes **will** cause terrible damage* is a prophecy, and a reader who does not share it stops there.

**Who is holding it.** The first text does not say the technique is bad; it says it is dangerous **in the wrong hands** — that is, when the wrong people are the ones using it. The phrase is fixed, and it is *hands*: not *heads*, which would be about ideas, and not *eyes*, which would be about being seen. Two more of the module belong beside it: to let a thing **go on** is to let it continue unchecked, and something that has already escaped its owner **has got out of hand**.

**Sounding a warning without a prophecy.** _It **could** happen_ — the first text closes on those three words, and they are what makes the paragraph arguable instead of shrill. The writer calls his own picture *a nightmare scenario, of course*, and then refuses to drop it. Conceding how unlikely a danger is, and keeping it on the table anyway, is a move you can borrow whole.

**Where an -ly adverb stands.** The second text is built out of a word family — *gene → genetic*, *medicine → medical*, *particular → particularly*, *responsible → responsibly*, *hope → hopefully* — and the -ly forms do not all stand in the same place.

| The adverb modifies    | Where it goes                    | Example                                            |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **the whole sentence** | at the head, with a comma        | _**Hopefully**, a cure will be found in our lifetime._ |
| **a verb**             | after the verb, or after its object | _The technique can be used **responsibly**._        |
| **a word or a phrase** | immediately in front of it        | _It matters **particularly in poor countries**._      |

> ⚠️ An -ly adverb placed at the head does **not** describe the subject. _**Hopefully**, the doctors will find a cure_ says that the writer hopes; it does not say the doctors are hopeful. If you mean the doctors, you need an adjective: _the doctors are **hopeful**_.

## 📰 Telling what happened, and finishing a half

A news item is the module's tense exercise: a chain of finished events, one of them earlier than the rest, and a doer who often does not matter.

- **Simple past** for the chain: _the boy **climbed** on to the wall, **slipped** and **fell**._
- **Past perfect** for the one event that was already over: _a few days earlier the family **had watched** the film together._
- **Passive** whenever what happened matters more than who did it: _the boy **was found** at seven o'clock._

**And the small word that decides whether you name the doer.** The passive drops the doer; **by** puts him back, and the grammar summary at the end of your book is precise about when: *by* is used **only if a name or a noun giving necessary information is included**. _He **was taken** to hospital_ needs no *by* — an ambulance took him, and nobody is surprised. _He **was found by his elder brother**_ needs one, because who found him is part of the story.

**Finishing a half.** Seven sentence openings are printed and eight completions are offered, so one completion is left over. Read the **grammar** of the opening before you read its meaning: it usually tells you the shape of what must follow.

| The opening ends on…                | What must follow                                        |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| _after having_ / _before having_    | a **past participle**, or an object + a past participle    |
| _he was born_                       | a **date**, a **place**, or both                            |
| _she tied the medal_                | a phrase of **place** — *around his neck*                   |
| _he called him_                     | a **name** or a description of the person                   |

> 🗡️ **after having + past participle** has two readings, and the difference is who acted. _After having **won** the title, he retired_ — he did the winning. _After having **his bicycle stolen**, he learnt to fight_ — somebody else did the stealing. The object slipped in front of the participle is the signal that the doer changed: **have something done** takes the action off the subject and puts it on somebody else.
>
> ⚠️ That structure then splits again, and the **meaning of the verb** is what tells you which way. _She **had the roof repaired**_ — she arranged it and paid for it. _He **had his bicycle stolen**_ — nobody arranges to be robbed, so this one simply happened to him. Same three words in the same order, one reading you choose and one you suffer; ask yourself whether anybody would ever want the thing done, and the sentence answers itself.

## ✒️ The marks that were never printed

The last task hands you a text with no punctuation and no capitals at all.

- A sentence **opens on a capital** and **closes on a full stop**, a question mark or an exclamation mark. A comma cannot close one: _we were lucky, we found a table outside_ ✗ joins two whole sentences with a comma, and English asks for something stronger — a full stop, a linking word, or the semicolon below.
- A **comma** separates the items of a list and follows a long opening phrase.
- A **semicolon** is the one mark that *can* hold two whole sentences together with no linking word: _the rain had stopped; the pitch was still unplayable._ It is the third way out of the comma above — a full stop, a linking word, or a semicolon. The full stop makes two sentences of the two halves; the linking word and the semicolon both keep them in one, and of those two the semicolon is the one that does it without adding a word.
- A **colon** goes after a general term that is about to be opened out into its members: _the square was full of street performers: fire-eaters, acrobats and singers._ The colon says *here they are*; a comma in the same place would turn *street performers* into one more item of the list, beside the fire-eaters.
- **Proper nouns** keep their capital wherever they stand, and so does **I**.
- The **title of a film, a book or a song** keeps its capitals inside the sentence: _they watched **Braveheart** on the Sunday evening._
- The **name of an award or of an institution** is a proper noun too: _the **Presidential Medal of Freedom**_, _the **National Pedagogic Centre**_ — but a plain *medal*, a plain *centre*, take none.

> ⚠️ **Grandeur does not earn a capital; being a name does.** *A world champion*, *a president*, *a doctor* stay in small letters however impressive the person, because they describe rather than name. Two things bring the capital back: the word welded to a name (*President Bourguiba*, *Doctor Zayani*), and the word taken up into the official title of something (*the Presidential Medal of Freedom* — where *medal* is part of the award's own name, while *he was given a medal* is not).
