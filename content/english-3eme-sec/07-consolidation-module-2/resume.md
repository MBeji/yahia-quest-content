# 📜 Summary: Consolidation Module 2

- **An argument that holds together** — seven sentences are printed out of order and the book drops one of them, the opening claim, into the first square for you. Each of the others carries the mark that places it: **This / That / Such** develops the sentence before it; **What is more / Furthermore / Besides** adds to a point already made; **On the other hand / However** turns to the opposite side, so a first side must already exist; **While / Although** + a good point concedes before objecting; a **pronoun with nobody to point at** needs its person named earlier. A sentence that states a claim and points at nothing behind it needs nothing said before it, so it can stand first. Method: cross out every sentence that carries a mark, and ask of each one that is left *what has to be said before this makes sense?* Trap: **adding and turning are different jobs** — "the fees are low. **Furthermore**, the hours are inconvenient" puts a drawback where the reader was promised a second advantage.
- **The brochure, and the words it lives on** — **leisure** (the time you are free to use as you please: a *leisure club*), to **relax**, to **spend** (a stretch of time), a **sauna**, a **magazine**, **beauty** (a *beauty salon*), to **book**. Around them the brochure's own words: a **spa bath**, a **steam room**, a **fitness room**, a **hairdresser**, an **award winning** salon, a **comfy** chair, a **well-equipped** club. Traps: **leisure** is a stretch of **time**, not a kind of amusement — a leisure club is the club you use in the hours that belong to you; and **to book** means keeping a place in advance (it can stand with no object: *be sure to book in advance*), not *to demand*, which insists that something be given to you.
- **Reading the situation of a text** — two tasks stop and ask about the **situation** rather than the language: *What type of text is it?* (the genre), *Where would you read it?* (the place the genre lives in), and the three columns *speaker / audience / occasion* (who the **I** or **we** stands for, who the **you** stands for, and the moment that made the text necessary). Three marks give it away almost every time: **we** + praise of its own facilities = something is being sold; **you** + times, prices and instructions = you are expected to act; **past** + first person singular = one person telling what happened to them. A welcome and a complaint use one word list and face opposite ways — the **tense**, the **pronoun** and the **purpose** separate them. And the task that completes half-written words says that **each dot stands for one letter**: count the dots, and every word of the right meaning and the wrong length is ruled out.
- **Two columns, and where the beat falls** — the fifteen words sort into **Entertainment** (camping · hiking · canoeing · sightseeing · concerts · opera · amusement parks) and **Science / Technology** (insulin · cloning · genes · laser · DNA · contact lenses · DVD · hard disc). Sort by asking **what the word names**, not what it took to make it. Then say them aloud: **-ing does not move the beat** — it adds a syllable and leaves the stress where the base word had it (*CAMP → CAMPing*, *HIKE → HIKing*, *SIGHTsee → SIGHTseeing*, and *caNOE → caNOEing* by the very same rule), and **-ment** behaves the same way (*aMUSE → aMUSEment*). **-tion** and **-sion** do the opposite: they pull the beat onto the syllable in front of them (*EDucate → eduCAtion*). Most of the rest keep the beat first (*INsulin*, *OPera*, *CONcerts*, *LAser*, *CONtact lenses*, *HARD disc*), but the two sets of initials go the other way: a word spelt out letter by letter takes its beat on the **last** letter — *D-N-**A***, *D-V-**D***.
- **Genetic engineering, the two texts** — **for strict control**: life has **evolved** slowly over thousands of years, and tampering with the genes, **the building blocks of nature**, could do terrible damage; where will scientists allowed to *play God* stop, and can they control what they unleash; and what use might a **government** make of genetics — *the perfect baby*, a *master-race*. **For going on**: doctors will soon use genetic engineering **to help fight disease** and to tell you that a problem runs in your family; the genes may then be **altered** and the disease cured; plants that resist disease matter **particularly in poor countries**; animals bred to produce more food are more **profitable**; and to the objection that progress is *unnatural*, the answer is that the knowledge can be used **responsibly** — people protested about **transplant surgery** too, and most are in favour of it now. Two moves worth stealing: answering an objection by showing it has been made before and did not last, and reading a **metaphor as a claim** (a building block is what everything above it rests on).
- **The language those two texts are written in** — **listing your reasons** in three slots, one to open, one to add, one to close: *To begin with… furthermore… Lastly…* or *In the first place… Secondly… Finally…*; an addition marker can sit **in the middle of its sentence** between two commas (*I worry, **furthermore**, where scientists will stop*), and *For example / For instance* illustrate instead of adding. **Hedging**: **might** and **could** say a thing is possible, **will** says the writer expects it, **must** states a conclusion he is sure of. **In the wrong hands** — the fixed phrase is *hands*, not *heads* (ideas) or *eyes* (being seen); to let a thing **go on** is to let it continue unchecked; what has escaped its owner **has got out of hand**. **Where an -ly adverb stands**: on the **whole sentence** at the head with a comma (*__Hopefully__, a cure will be found*), on a **verb** after it or after its object (*used **responsibly***), on a **word or phrase** immediately in front of it (*__particularly__ in poor countries*) — and an -ly adverb at the head is the writer's own comment, never a description of the subject (*the doctors are **hopeful*** needs an adjective).
- **Telling what happened, and finishing a half** — a news item runs on three tenses: **simple past** for the chain of events, **past perfect** for the one that was already over, **passive** whenever what happened matters more than who did it. **by** puts the doer back, and the grammar summary is precise: *by* is used **only if a name or a noun giving necessary information is included** — *he was taken to hospital* (no *by* needed) against *he was found **by his elder brother***. Then seven openings are printed against eight completions, so one is left over: read the **grammar** of the opening first — *after having* wants a **past participle** (or an object + a past participle), *he was born* wants a date or a place, *she tied the medal* wants a phrase of place, *he called him* wants a name. **after having + past participle** has two readings: *after having **won** the title* (he acted) against *after having **his bicycle stolen*** (somebody else acted). And **have something done** splits again by the meaning of the verb: *she **had the roof repaired*** (she arranged it) against *he **had his bicycle stolen*** (nobody arranges that).
- **The marks that were never printed** — a sentence **opens on a capital** and **closes on a full stop**, a question mark or an exclamation mark; a **comma cannot close one** (*we were lucky, we found a table* ✗ joins two whole sentences, and English asks for something stronger — a full stop, a linking word, or a **semicolon**, the one mark that holds two whole sentences together with no linking word). A **comma** separates the items of a list and follows a long opening phrase. A **colon** goes after a general term about to be opened out into its members (*street performers: fire-eaters, acrobats and singers*) — a comma there would turn *street performers* into one more item of the list. **Proper nouns** and **I** keep their capital wherever they stand, and so do the **title of a film, a book or a song** and the **name of an award or of an institution**. Trap: **grandeur earns no capital** — *a world champion*, *a president*, *a doctor* describe rather than name, and the capital comes back only when the word is welded to a name (*President Bourguiba*) or taken up into an official title (*the Presidential Medal of Freedom*, against *he was given a medal*).
