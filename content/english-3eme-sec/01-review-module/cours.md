# ⚔️ Review Module — Reloading Everything Second Year Left You

> 💡 "Before you climb higher, make sure the ladder under you still holds."

This module opens the book and teaches nothing new. It **recycles**: every rule you carry from second year is put back on the table, checked and repaired before the new programme begins. Nine of them decide most of the marks you will win or lose this year — reporting somebody's words, `let` and `make`, `used to`, comparing, the two pasts, the passive, linkers, the shape of a text, and the way English builds one word out of another. Clear this gate and the modules that follow cost you half the effort.

## 🗣️ Reporting somebody's words

When you repeat what a person said, you drop the inverted commas and open a **that-clause**. Three things then move.

| What moves       | Rule                                                        | Example                                                            |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The **person**   | every _I / my / we / our_ of the speaker changes            | _"I lost **my** badge."_ → _He said that he had lost **his** badge._ |
| The **tense**    | it steps one tense back                                     | see the table below                                                |
| The **pointers** | words aimed at the speaker's here and now move away         | _here_ → _there_ · _this_ → _that_ · _now_ → _then_ · _today_ → _that day_ |

| Direct speech                     | Reported after _said_       |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| present simple _(work)_           | simple past _(worked)_      |
| present continuous _(am working)_ | past continuous _(was working)_ |
| simple past _(worked)_            | past perfect _(had worked)_ |
| present perfect _(have worked)_   | past perfect _(had worked)_ |
| **will** _(will work)_            | **would** _(would work)_    |

> 🗡️ **say** and **tell** do not take the same road. You **tell somebody** something — the person comes straight after the verb, with no _to_. You **say** something, and if you name the listener you need _to_: _She **told me** that the office was closed._ ✓ · _She **said to me** that the office was closed._ ✓ — "She said me…" ✗ · "She told to me…" ✗

## ⛓️ let and make — the two verbs that refuse "to"

Both take **an object and then the base verb**. No _to_, ever.

- **let** = allow: _My parents **let me choose** my own subjects._
- **make** = oblige: _The storm **made us cancel** the trip._

> ⚠️ **make** gets its _to_ back the moment it turns passive: _Something **made** the company **close** the factory._ → _The company **was made to close** the factory._ ✓ — "was made close" ✗. **let** has no passive of this kind at all; English uses _be allowed to_: _We **were allowed to** leave early._

## ⏳ used to — the tense of a world that has changed

**used to + base verb** names a habit or a state that belonged to the past and **has stopped**: _People **used to write** letters._ (they no longer do) · _This street **used to be** quiet._

| Form        | Rule                           | Example                              |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Affirmative | **used to** + base verb        | _We **used to walk** to school._     |
| Negative    | **didn't use to** + base verb  | _We **didn't use to** take the bus._ |
| Question    | **Did** + subject + **use to** | _**Did** you **use to** live here?_  |

> ⚠️ Do not confuse it with **be used to**, a **present** state that takes a **noun or an -ing form**: _She **is used to working** nights._ = she is accustomed to it, and she still does it. Two traps live in one letter: "use to be" drops the **-d** of the past form, and "used to being" borrows the **-ing** that belongs to the other structure.

## ⚖️ Four ways of comparing, and one double

| What you want to say                | Structure                              | Example                                       |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| A is above B                        | **-er than** / **more … than**         | _The new lab is **bigger than** the old one._ |
| A is at the top of a group          | **the -est** / **the most …**          | _It is **the most useful** room in school._   |
| A and B are level                   | **as … as**                            | _The library is **as old as** the school._    |
| A does not reach the level you need | **not … enough** (after the adjective) | _The room is **not big enough**._             |

Short adjectives take **-er / -est** (_old, older, the oldest_); long ones take **more / the most** (_useful, more useful, the most useful_). A few are irregular: _good → better → the best_, _bad → worse → the worst_, _far → further → the furthest_. The adverb **well** follows _good_: _better, the best_.

> 🗡️ Two changes that move together take the **double comparative**: **the + comparative …, the + comparative …** — _**The longer** you wait, **the harder** it gets._ Both halves need their **the**, neither half ever takes a superlative, and _more_ never doubles a comparative ("the more better" ✗).

## 🔮 The present perfect against the simple past

- A moment is **named and finished** → **simple past**: _The library **opened** in 1998._
- **No moment is named** and the result counts today → **present perfect**: _It **has welcomed** thousands of readers._
- A period **starts in the past and reaches today** → **present perfect**, with **since** (a starting point) or **for** (a length of time): _It **has been** open **since** 1998._ · _… **for** twenty-five years._
- The same period, insisting on the **activity that has not stopped** → **present perfect continuous**, _have/has been + -ing_: _**Since** the war ended, the museum **has been rebuilding** its collection._

> ⚠️ The trap of this module: a sentence that opens with **Since …** and then falls back into a present or a simple past. "Since 1998 the library **is** open" ✗ · "Since 1998 the library **was** open" ✗ → _Since 1998 the library **has been** open_ ✓. And the reverse trap: **ago**, **last year**, **in 1998** name a finished moment, so they refuse the present perfect altogether.

## 🛡️ The passive — when the doer does not matter

The passive is **be + past participle**. The tense is carried by **be**, never by the participle.

| Active                               | Passive                             |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| _They **build** bridges here._       | _Bridges **are built** here._       |
| _They **built** the bridge in 1970._ | _The bridge **was built** in 1970._ |
| _They **have repaired** the road._   | _The road **has been repaired**._   |
| _You **can use** this machine._      | _This machine **can be used**._     |

After a modal the shape is fixed: **modal + be + past participle** — _must **be signed**_, _cannot **be used**_.

> 🗡️ A verb with **two objects** (_give, offer, send, pay, tell_) has two passives, and the common one starts from the **person**: _The jury gave the finalists a second chance._ → _The finalists **were given** a second chance._ ✓ Starting from the thing needs a **to**: _A second chance was given **to** the finalists._

> ⚠️ Never drop **be**, and never keep an active -ing form: "This machine can used" ✗ · "This machine can be using" ✗.

## 🧩 Holding a text together

A text is not a pile of sentences. Three things bind it.

**1. Linkers.** Each one announces a relation, and the relation chooses the word.

| Relation     | Linkers                     | Example                                                 |
| ------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Cause**    | because, as, since          | _We left early **because** the road was closed._        |
| **Result**   | so, therefore, that is why  | _The road was closed, **so** we left early._            |
| **Contrast** | but, however, yet, although | _The road was closed. **However**, we arrived on time._ |
| **Addition** | and, besides, moreover      | _It was late. **Besides**, we were tired._              |

> ⚠️ **because** and **so** point in opposite directions: _because_ is followed by the **cause**, _so_ by the **result**. Swap them and the sentence states the opposite of what you mean. **In fact** confirms what has just been said; it never contradicts it.

**2. Order.** A paragraph follows a line, usually time, and the markers give it away: _in 1902 … that winter … within two years … then … today_. A sentence opening with **then**, **so** or **however** can never come first: it leans on something that must already be there.

**3. Reference words.** _he, it, this, these, there, the one_ all point back at something said earlier. When a part has been deleted from a passage, hunt for the word that has lost what it points at — the gap is exactly there.

- **The head** carries the **two addresses**, the writer's and the receiver's, and the **date**.
- **The salutation** opens the letter itself — _Dear Mr Sassi,_ / _Dear Madam,_ — always with a comma.
- **The body** says why you write, gives the facts, and ends with what you ask for.
- **The closing formula** — _Yours sincerely,_ / _Sincerely,_ — sits under the last line of the body.
- **The signature block** comes last: the signature, the name, and the job title under it.

## 🧱 Word building

English rebuilds one word into another with **suffixes**. Read the **slot** first: what sits before and after the gap tells you which class of word it needs.

| You need         | Where it sits                        | Common endings                      | Example                    |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| A **noun**       | after _the, a, every_, or before _of_ | -tion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ty      | _decide → **decision**_    |
| An **adjective** | before a noun, or after _be / seem_  | -al, -ful, -less, -ous, -ive, -y    | _care → **careful**_       |
| An **adverb**    | beside a verb                        | -ly                                 | _careful → **carefully**_  |
| A **verb**       | after a subject                      | -ise/-ize, -en, -ify                | _strong → **strengthen**_  |

A few nouns are built by changing the **end** of the word instead of adding a syllable: _grow → **growth**_, _long → **length**_, _descend → **descent**_. And one verb can feed a whole family: _employ_ gives **employer** (who gives the work), **employee** (who does it), **employment** and **unemployed**.

> 🗡️ The word you have to produce is nearly always one you already know. The work is to decide **which class** the slot needs, then to recall that word's form — never to invent an ending.

> 🗡️ English also builds long labels by **stacking words in front of the noun**: a machine that tests lungs is a **lung-testing machine**, and a station in space is a **space station**. The describing words come **first**, the noun **last** — the opposite of the order your first language uses.
