# ⚔️ A Change Is as Good as a Rest — Comparing, Placing, and Telling the Holiday That Went Wrong

> 💡 "Anybody can go away. What you bring back is a story — and a story needs the right words for what was better, where you went, and what had already happened before you got there."

A holiday is three language jobs in one. Before you leave you **compare** places and choose. While you are there you **place** yourself: along a shore, through a wood, off a path. Afterwards you **tell** it, and telling means putting two past moments in the right order — and, when the week went wrong, writing to the company in language that gets an answer. This module gives you the words and the structures that carry them.

## 🏝️ The words of the topic

**Leisure activities** — the twelve the module names.

| Activity              | What you actually do                                              |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **canoeing**          | move a small light boat with a paddle                             |
| **harbour cruising**  | go slowly round a harbour in a boat, looking at it                |
| **hiking**            | take a long walk in the countryside, for the walk itself          |
| **sightseeing**       | visit the monuments and famous places of a town, usually guided   |
| **sunbathing**        | lie in the sun                                                    |
| **surfing**           | ride a wave standing on a board                                   |
| **water skiing**      | be pulled over the water behind a boat                            |
| **roller skating**    | move on wheels fixed under your shoes                             |
| **sky diving**        | jump from a plane with a parachute                                |
| **safari**            | watch wild animals, usually from a vehicle                        |
| **dog sledge riding** | be pulled over snow by dogs                                       |
| **cycling**           | travel by bicycle                                                 |

**Where you sleep.** A **cottage** is a small house in the country. A **terraced house** is one house in a row, joined to its neighbours on both sides. An **apartment** may be **spacious**, with an **en suite** shower — a shower opening straight off the bedroom. A **tent** is what you carry and put up yourself, and sleeping in one is what **camping** means. A **resort** is not a building at all: it is a whole place people go to for their holidays, and **accommodation** is the general word for wherever you end up sleeping.

**What a hotel offers.** The module prints the list in two columns, and the columns matter: some services belong to the **hotel** — a swimming pool, a games room, a mini-gym, a sauna, **ironing facilities**, **baby-sitting facilities**, a car park, a **24-hour laundry service**, free newspapers — and some to the **room** — a colour TV, a direct dial telephone, a hairdryer, a mini-bar, a private bathroom, 24-hour room service, air-conditioning, a writing desk.

> 🗡️ **Facilities** is the polite hotel word for *the means of doing something*. *Ironing facilities* means there is an iron; *baby-sitting facilities* means somebody will look after your children. Read a brochure looking for the **facility**, not for the object.

**The coast.** A **shoreline** is the line where the land meets the sea; a **bay** is a curve of sea in the land; a **creek** is a narrow inlet; a **cliff** is a high wall of rock; a **reef** lies just under the surface; a **lagoon** is shallow water shut off from the open sea; a **cave** is a hole in the rock; a **harbour** is where boats are safe; a **wreck** is a sunken ship; a **path** is a narrow way for walkers; water is **crystal clear** when you can see the bottom through it.

**How it left you feeling** — before: **excited**, **enthusiastic**, **hopeful**. After, when it went wrong: **disappointed**, **regretful**, **embarrassed**, **nervous**, **fed up** (with). And when a week went badly, it was **spoilt**; you ask the company for **compensation**; a coach that stops working has **broken down**; a **fortnight** is two weeks; to **get rid of** something is to free yourself of it.

## 📈 Comparing — the comparative and the superlative

| | Short adjective | Long adjective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Comparative** | quiet → **quieter than** | expensive → **more expensive than** |
| **Superlative** | quiet → **the quietest** | expensive → **the most expensive** |

One-syllable adjectives take **-er / -est**. Adjectives of three syllables or more take **more / most** — and **less / the least** for the other direction. Two-syllable ones vary: *heavy → heavier*, but *careful → more careful*.

**The five irregulars, learnt once and for ever:**

| | Comparative | Superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **good** | better | the best |
| **bad** | worse | the worst |
| **far** | farther / **further** | the farthest / **the furthest** |
| **little** | less | the least |
| **much / many** | more | the most |

**The word in front of the comparative.** English strengthens a comparative with **much**, **far**, **a lot** or **many more**, and weakens it with **a bit** or **a little**: _The coast road is **much** slower._ · _It is **a bit** further than the map says._

> ⚠️ **English does not put *very* in front of a comparative.** "very slower" ✗, "very more expensive" ✗ → _**much** slower_ ✓, _**far** more expensive_ ✓. And English does not double a comparative either: "more quieter" ✗, "more better" ✗ — *quieter* and *better* already carry the *more*. In the same way, an adjective that has its own irregular form refuses a regular ending on top of it.

**than or the?** A comparative takes **than** and looks at one other thing: _quieter **than** the port_. A superlative takes **the** and looks at a whole group: _**the** quietest of the four_. Mixing them is the classic slip: "the quietest than the port" ✗.

**What follows the superlative.** With a **place**, English usually says **in**: _the busiest café **in** the medina_. With a **group you can count** or a **period**, it says **of**: _the busiest **of** the four cafés_ · _the best day **of** the week_.

> 🗡️ To say that two things are level, do not compare at all: **as + adjective + as**. _The mountain route is **as long as** the coast road._

**Reading before you choose.** An advertisement and a timetable are not read like a story: you **scan** them for the two or three facts you need — the times, the places, the order — and you ignore the rest. Everything an advertisement claims can be checked against its own lines, and everything it does **not** say is not yours to assume.

| Preposition | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **along** | follows the line of something | _walk **along** the quay_ |
| **across** | goes from one side of a surface to the other | _row **across** the bay_ |
| **through** | goes in one side of something and out the other | _drive **through** the tunnel_ |
| **around** | goes round something, or here and there in it | _sail **around** the island_ |
| **into / out of** | enters / leaves an enclosed space | _step **into** the cave_ |
| **off** | leaves the surface or the way you were on | _never step **off** the path_ |
| **towards** | moves in the direction of, without saying you arrive | _walk **towards** the lighthouse_ |
| **back to** | returns | _**back to** the hotel by midnight_ |

**And the three that place you rather than move you:** **at** a point (_at the harbour_, _at 18.00_), **on** a surface (_on the beach_, _on Friday_), **in** an enclosed space or area (_in the countryside_, _in July_).

> ⚠️ *along* and *across* are not interchangeable. Walking **along** the quay keeps the water on one side of you the whole time; walking **across** the quay takes you over it and away in a few steps. Choose by asking whether the traveller **stays with** the thing or **leaves** it behind.

## ⏳ The past perfect against the simple past

```
had  +  past participle       (the same had for I, you, he, she, we, they)
```

The simple past tells you **what happened**. The past perfect tells you **what had already happened before that** — it is the tense of the earlier of two past moments.

- _We reached the museum at four. It **had closed** at half past three._ (the closing came first)
- _The room was spotless: the owner **had cleaned** it that morning._ (the cleaning came first)
- _She **was** furious. She **had stood** at the door for an hour._ (the standing came first)

**Reading the order out of the sentence.** The past perfect is what lets a story be told out of order without confusing anybody. Whatever the sentence puts first, **the past perfect is the earlier event**: _When the driver opened the boot, somebody **had taken** her camera._ — the theft came first, although the sentence names it second.

**before and after already fix the order.** When one of those two words is there, the order is written on the page, so the past perfect becomes optional. Turning one word into the other means turning the two halves round as well, and only then is the order kept: _The lift **had stopped** working **before** we booked_ = _**After** the lift stopped working, we booked._

> ⚠️ **The case the book itself sets aside.** When two past actions are **close in time, or closely connected**, the past perfect is **not usually used**. The Grammar summary gives the model sentence: _When he arrived at the hotel, he **asked** for a room._ — not "had asked". Marking an order that nobody could get wrong makes the sentence heavy, not precise.

> 🗡️ For a length of time filled before that past moment, English can stretch the tense: **had been + -ing**. _By midday they **had been waiting** three hours for the plumber._

**Giving the account a title.** A holiday told afterwards is usually asked for a title, and a title has to hold the **whole** account, not its most striking detail. Test any title you invent against the first line and the last: if it fits one end and contradicts the other, it is a caption, not a title.

## ❓ Asking — the WH words of an interview

Section Four asks you to interview a classmate about a holiday. The whole job is choosing the question word, because each one asks for a different kind of information.

| You want | Question word | Answer it gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the place | **where** | _In Tabarka._ |
| the moment | **when** | _On the second Friday._ |
| the person | **who** | _My uncle._ |
| the reason | **why** | _Because it was cheap._ |
| the length of time | **how long** | _Ten days._ |
| the distance | **how far** | _Sixty kilometres._ |
| the number of times | **how often** | _Twice a year._ |
| a price or a quantity you cannot count | **how much** | _Ninety dinars._ |
| a number you can count | **how many** | _Four nights._ |

The shape is **WH word + auxiliary + subject + base verb**: _**When did you** book?_ The one exception is the question whose WH word is itself the subject, and there no *do* is added: _**Who paid** for the trip?_ When you then report your classmate's answer to the class, the rules you reviewed in the Review Module apply unchanged.

> 🗡️ An **interview** is not a conversation. One speaker brings prepared questions on one topic and the other answers them; the interviewer does not give her own opinion, and she leaves the other person time to think.

## 🔗 Linkers — the joints of a letter of complaint

| The job | Linkers |
| --- | --- |
| **announce that what follows is bad news** | unfortunately · I am sorry to say |
| **open the list of problems** | to start with · in the first place · firstly |
| **add another problem** | added to this · besides · what is more |
| **bring in the last one** | last but not least · finally |
| **give the cause** | as · since · because · because of · owing to |
| **give the result** | thus · therefore · so |
| **give the purpose** | so that · in order to |
| **set one fact against another** | although · even though · in spite of · despite · however |

**The one rule that decides half of these.** A **conjunction** is followed by a **whole clause**, with its own subject and verb. A **preposition** is followed by a **noun** or an **-ing** form.

| Conjunction + clause | Preposition + noun |
| --- | --- |
| **because** the wind was strong | **because of** the wind |
| **as** the road was flooded | **owing to** the state of the road |
| **although** it rained | **in spite of** the rain · **despite** the rain |

> ⚠️ Three slips this rule catches at once: "because of the wind was strong" ✗ (a clause after a preposition), "owing the state of the road" ✗ (the *to* belongs to it), "despite of the rain" ✗ (that *of* belongs to *in spite of*). And *however* in the sense of *but* is an **adverb**, not a conjunction: it needs its own sentence, after a full stop or a semicolon.

> 🗡️ **At last**, **eventually** and **last but not least** are not the same. *At last* is relief after a long wait — _the taxi came **at last**_. *Eventually* reports how a process ended — _**eventually** they refunded half_. *Last but not least* introduces the **final item of a list**, and says it matters as much as the others.

## ✉️ Writing the letter of complaint

**The blocks on the page**, from the top: your own address · the date · the address of the company · the salutation (*Dear Sir or Madam,*) · the body · the complimentary close (*Yours faithfully,*) · your name.

**The plan of the body — three parts, in this order:**

1. **the reason for writing**, with the exact details of the holiday: which one, where, when;
2. **the details of the problems**, one after another, joined by the linkers above;
3. **what you expect from the company**: a refund, another holiday, compensation.

**The register.** You are writing to a company you do not know, so the request in Part 3 is **firm and polite at once**. Three things spoil it, and each of them in a different way: an **order** ("Send the money back today.") reads as rudeness; a **threat** ("Refund me or I shall write to the newspapers.") puts the reader on the defensive; and a request buried under **hedges** ("I was more or less wondering about something of that kind.") never says what you want, so the third part does no work at all. The shape that carries it is _I **should be grateful if you would** …_ — a polite opening around a verb that names the thing exactly.

> ⚠️ The letter usually closes on **look forward to** — and there *to* is a **preposition**, not the *to* of an infinitive, so the verb after it takes **-ing**: _We **look forward to seeing** you in September._ ✓ · "look forward to see" ✗. The preposition itself is fixed too: "look forward for" ✗ and bare "look forward" ✗ are both outside the expression.

## 📊 Reporting a survey

The mini-project of this module is a survey of what teenagers in Tunisia do with their free time: you count, you draw the chart, you write the report. A report **says how many people you asked**, **what the survey was about**, and **what came out of it** — and it turns the figures into words.

| The figure | The phrase |
| --- | --- |
| nobody | **none of us** |
| about one in two | **half of us** |
| more than half | **most of us** |
| almost everybody | **nearly all of us** |
| a small number | **some of us** |

## 🔊 The vowels — the spelling does not decide the sound

Start from the two pairs that prove the point. **food** and **flood** are written alike and share nothing else. **board** and **boat** differ by a single letter and land in two different boxes.

| Box | Key word | The words of this module that go in it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the long one | **moon** | food · through · routine · blue · lagoon |
| the wide one | **now** | found · south · around · mouth · countryside |
| the short one | **book** | wood |
| the open one | **more** | board |
| the sliding one | **throw** | boat |
| the plain one | **bus** | flood |

Read the table by the key word, never by the letters: *through* has no *oo* at all and still belongs with *moon*; *wood* and *flood* both end in *-ood* and sit two boxes apart; *mouth* and *through* both carry *ou* and share nothing. The spelling never decides the sound — the ear does, and the key word is how you remember which ear.

> 🏆 You can now compare two places without doubling a comparative, take somebody along a shore and through a wood without losing them, put two past moments in the right order — and, when the fortnight is spoilt, write the letter that gets the money back. That last skill is the one your examiner will ask you to prove on paper.
