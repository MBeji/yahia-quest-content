# ⚔️ Science and Technology: a Blessing or a Curse? — The Words for What We Build

> 💡 "The same discovery saves a life in one hospital and frightens a whole country in the next. Nothing decides which of the two you are talking about except the words you choose."

Your module asks a question and refuses to answer it for you: is what science and technology hand us a **blessing** — something with a positive impact — or a **curse** — something with a negative one? Every text in it argues one way or the other, and two speakers argue against each other for a whole section. Your job is not to pick a camp. It is to arrive with the language that lets you hold one: the exact verb, the reversed word, the two-word adjective, the modal that forbids and the modal that merely permits.

## 🔬 Three verbs your book refuses to confuse

The very first activity of the module is a dictionary page, and it separates three verbs students mix up every year.

| Verb          | What it does                                                                     | Your book's example       |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| **discover**  | find something that **already existed** but had been unknown                     | to discover electricity   |
| **invent**    | make or create something **new**, especially a device that never existed         | to invent the telegraph   |
| **originate** | **begin** something new, above all a new idea, a new method, a new movement      | to originate a movement   |

> 🗡️ *invent* has a second life the module keeps: to **invent** an excuse or a story is to **make it up**, to **fabricate** something that is not true. The quality behind it is **ingenuity** — the skill of devising things cleverly.

And the two big words themselves, in the book's own terms:

- **science** — a branch of knowledge dealing with facts and truths systematically arranged, gained through **observation and experimentation**. Your book separates **pure science** (knowledge for its own sake) from **applied science** (knowledge put to work).
- **technology** — the branch of knowledge that deals with the **creation and use of technical means** and their interaction with life, society and the environment, drawing upon industrial arts, engineering, applied science and pure science.

A short bench of words travels with them all module long: a **device** (a machine built for one job), a **lens**, a **laser**, a **robot**, **surgery**, **dialysis**, a **kidney**, to **diagnose**, **disposable**, **cellular**, to **orbit**, to **set up** a company, and **nano technology** — the ability to measure, see, predict and make things on the scale of atoms and molecules.

## 🚫 Reversing a word — the negative prefixes

This is the grammar point the module puts first, and it is worth every minute. English reverses a word by hanging a small block in front of it. The block you write is not chosen by ear.

**One prefix, four spellings.** *il-*, *im-*, *ir-* and *in-* are the **same** Latin prefix. When a word takes it, the **first letter of that word** decides which of the four you write.

| You write | Before                | Examples from the module                          |
| --------- | --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **il-**   | **l**                 | legal → **il**legal · logical → **il**logical       |
| **im-**   | **m**, **p**, **b**   | moral → **im**moral · possible → **im**possible     |
| **ir-**   | **r**                 | regular → **ir**regular · responsible → **ir**responsible · reversible → **ir**reversible |
| **in-**   | the other letters     | curable → **in**curable · human → **in**human · estimable → **in**estimable |

> ⚠️ **The spelling rule tells you how to write the prefix; it does not tell you which words take it.** *successful* does not become "insuccessful" ✗ and *known* does not become "inknown" ✗ — both take **un-**. Which prefix a word takes is learnt with the word, one at a time.

**Two prefixes that follow no letter rule.**

- **un-** — able → **un**able · acceptable → **un**acceptable · successful → **un**successful · known → **un**known · wanted → **un**wanted · ethical → **un**ethical
- **dis-** — appear → **dis**appear · abled → **dis**abled · order → **dis**order

**Two prefixes that are not pure negations.** They change the action instead of cancelling it.

- **mis-** says the action was carried out **wrongly or badly**: *misunderstand* is not "not understand", it is to understand the wrong way; *misread*, *misuse*, *misjudge* work the same.
- **de-** **reverses** an action that had been done: *code* → **de**code, *activate* → **de**activate. _(Your grammar summary prints *freeze* → *defreeze*; keep it for the exam, but note that the English for undoing a freeze is **defrost**, **thaw** or **unfreeze** — *defreeze* is in no dictionary.)_

> 🗡️ **Some words only look reversed.** *insert* is to put something **in**, not the opposite of anything; *dismiss* is to reject, not the opposite of "miss"; *inflame* is to make red and swollen, not the opposite of "flame". The test is quick: pull the opening letters off and ask whether what is left means the **opposite** of the whole word. If it does not, there was no prefix there.

> 🗡️ Your book's own observation box puts four words side by side: *abnormal*, *unwanted*, *unethical*, *inestimable*. Three of them carry the prefixes above. The fourth, **abnormal**, carries **ab-**, which is not in the grammar summary's list of eight and means "away from" — away from what is normal. Notice it, keep the word, and do not try to build new words with it.

> ⚠️ Two traps that cost marks every year. First, a prefix **never doubles a letter that was not there**: *responsible* already begins with r, so *ir* + *responsible* gives **irresponsible**, with two r's — count them. Second, a word can exist with two different prefixes and two different meanings: *able* gives both **unable** (not able to do one thing) and **disabled** (living with a disability). The sentence around the word tells you which is wanted.

## 🧬 The vocabulary of the laboratory

The listening section is built on genetics, and the book defines its terms before it plays anything. Learn each with its definition: the exam gives the definition and asks for the word.

| Word                    | What it names                                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **ethics**              | a set of principles of proper conduct; a system of moral values                                        |
| **an issue**            | a question that arises for discussion                                                                  |
| **controversial**       | likely to cause prolonged argument, especially about social, moral or political matters                |
| **DNA**                 | a molecule found within a structure known as a chromosome                                              |
| **a chromosome**        | a structure within the nucleus that contains DNA                                                       |
| **cloning**             | creating a new organism                                                                                |
| **gene therapy**        | insertion of normal DNA directly into cells to correct a genetic defect                                |

| **a designer gene**     | a gene altered or created by genetic engineering, especially for use in gene therapy                   |

| **blood clotting**      | a process in which liquid blood changes into a semisolid                                               |

**Genetic engineering** itself is the application of what genetics has learnt to problems such as food production, waste disposal, medicine and disease. Its procedures **may** involve **artificial insemination**, **cloning**, **in vitro fertilization** or the direct manipulation of the genetic material by the **recombinant DNA** technique. Around them stand **a surrogate** mother (one who takes the place of another), a **transgenic** animal, a **hereditary** disorder, a **transplant**, a **tumour** that may **shrink** or **spread**, a **side effect**, and a **species** that is **extinct** or merely **endangered**.

| ------------ | --------------- | ------------ | -------------- |

> ⚠️ *conceal* and *hide* both mean to keep out of sight, so they are **synonyms**, not opposites. The opposite of *conceal* is *show*.

**The nouns that refuse the easy ending.** The module ends its listening work with a table of word families, and the interesting half is the one where the noun is not the verb plus a syllable.

| Verb          | Noun               | Verb        | Noun            |
| ------------- | ------------------ | ----------- | --------------- |
| treat         | **treatment**      | suspect     | **suspicion**   |
| diagnose      | **diagnosis**      | prove       | **proof**       |
| choose        | **choice**         | release     | **release**     |

| perform       | **performance**    | avoid       | **avoidance**   |
| insert        | **insertion**      | confess     | **confession**  |

Two more come from adjectives instead of verbs: *innocent* → **innocence**, *curious* → **curiosity**.

> 🗡️ *diagnosis*, *choice*, *proof* and *suspicion* are the four to write out by hand. "diagnosement" ✗, "choosal" ✗, "provement" ✗, "suspectation" ✗ — English already has a word for each of them, and inventing an ending is how the mark is lost. Note also that *cure* and *release* are the **same word** as their verb, and that *diagnostic* is normally an **adjective** in English — _a diagnostic test_ — and is never the noun built on *diagnose*, which is *diagnosis*.

## 🔗 Compound adjectives — the shorthand of a science page

Two words, one hyphen, in front of a noun: that is how a scientific text says in three words what a sentence would take ten to say. The book's grammar summary sets out **eight** patterns, and no others.

| Pattern                       | The book's example              |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| adjective + noun              | a long-range weather forecast   |
| number + year + old + noun    | an 18-year-old young woman      |
| adverb + **-ing** form        | rapidly-growing populations     |
| noun + **-ing** form          | a foot-stamping dance           |
| adverb + past participle      | a well-run philanthropy         |
| number + noun                 | two five-pound notes            |
| noun + adjective              | odour-free tablets              |
| noun + past participle        | a snow-covered mountain         |

The module's own articles use six of them:

- a **long-term** threat — adjective + noun
- a **12-year-old** girl — number + year + old + noun
- a **rapidly-increasing** cancer — adverb + *-ing* form
- a **life-threatening** reaction — noun + *-ing* form
- a **genetically-modified** pea — adverb + past participle
- **24-hour** shopping, a **ten-year** research project — number + noun

**Reading them backwards is the exercise.** Every compound adjective unfolds into a clause, and the module asks you to fold a clause back into one:

| The long way round                                | The compound                     |
| ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| cells that produce insulin                        | **insulin-producing** cells      |
| chemicals that cause cancer                       | **cancer-causing** chemicals     |
| a period of six months                            | a **six-month** period           |
| 104 chemicals made by men                         | 104 **man-made** chemicals       |
| 3000 twins who are two years old                  | 3000 **two-year-old** twins      |

> ⚠️ **In these patterns the noun inside the block stays singular.** *a six-month period*, not "a six-months period" ✗ · *3000 two-year-old twins*, not "two-years-old" ✗ · *24-hour shopping*, not "24-hours" ✗. The plural is already carried by the noun at the end of the phrase.

> 🗡️ **-ing form or past participle? Ask who does the action.** In *insulin-producing cells*, the cells **produce** the insulin, so the block takes the **-ing** form. In *man-made chemicals*, the chemicals are **made by** men, so the block takes the **past participle**. Same test every time: if the thing you are describing acts, use *-ing*; if it is acted on, use the participle. "a virus-carried insect" ✗ says the virus is carrying the insect.

## 🎚️ Modals — permitting, forbidding, advising, doubting

Your module works four of them hard, and the grammar summary adds the rest of the family.

| Modal        | What it does                                          | Example from the module's world                            |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **can**      | ability, or something that is possible                 | _Chatting on the web **can** be a comfort to lonely people._ |
| **can't**    | inability, or something that is **not possible**       | _I **can't** think of a morally acceptable reason for cloning._ |
| **may**      | permission, and also a real possibility                | _Inventions **may** be a blessing or a curse._               |
| **might** / **could** | a possibility in the future                   | _Cloning **might** be one way to protect endangered species._ |
| **must**     | obligation, or emphatic advice                         | _Which gadgets **must** you have?_                           |
| **mustn't**  | a **prohibition** — it is not allowed                  | _Technology is good but it **mustn't** fall in the wrong hands._ |
| **should**   | advice, a recommendation                               | _Researchers **should** publish their failures too._         |
| **needn't**  | **absence of necessity** — there is no need            | _You **needn't** buy a second charger._                      |
| **had better** | urgent advice, with a warning behind it              | _You **had better** back up that file._                      |

Three rules hold the whole table up.

1. A modal is followed by the **infinitive without *to***: *can wake*, never "can to wake" ✗. The grammar summary says it of *can*, *could*, *may* and *might* in so many words.
2. A modal **never takes -s**: *she can*, *it may*, never "she cans" ✗.
3. The verb after it stays in the **base form**: *may leave*, never "may leaves" ✗ or "may left" ✗.

> 🗡️ One member of the family keeps its *to*, and it is worth knowing before somebody catches you out with it: **ought to**, which advises exactly as *should* does — _You **ought to** read the whole report._ Every modal in the table above drops the *to*; that one carries it in its name.

> ⚠️ **mustn't and needn't are not two ways of saying the same thing, and the gap between them is huge.** *You **mustn't** switch it off* forbids you. *You **needn't** switch it off* tells you nobody is asking you to — you may switch it off if you like. Confusing the two turns a ban into a free choice.

> 🗡️ **can't and mustn't** are just as far apart. *can't* says the thing **is not possible**; *mustn't* says it **is not allowed**. A scanner **can't** work during a power cut. A laboratory **mustn't** release a drug before the tests are over — it could, physically; it is forbidden to.

## 🔊 The main stress in a two-part expression

Your module's pronunciation work is on **stress patterns**, and it sorts the section's expressions into two groups.

- Expressions written as **two separate words** — *gene therapy*, *funeral homes*, *burial services*, *designer babies*, *blood clotting*, *lab dish*, *breast cancer*. The book's answer for this group is that the main stress falls on the **second** word.
- Compound nouns written as **one block or joined by a hyphen** — *make-up*, *book-case*, *test-tube*. Here the main stress falls on the **first part**, and this half is not in dispute.

> ⚠️ Learn the book's answer for the exam, and know why it is worth no more than that. Outside this
> page, **the two groups behave the same way**: *GENE therapy*, *BLOOD clotting*, *BREAST cancer*,
> *LAB dish*, *FUneral homes*, *BUrial services* and *deSIGNer babies* all carry their main stress on
> the **first** word, exactly like *MAKE-up* and *TEST-tube*. The contrast the exercise is built on
> does not hold in standard English — which is why nothing in this chapter asks you to choose a
> stress for a two-word expression.

> 🗡️ The test is worth doing out loud with a classmate, which is exactly what the book asks: say the word both ways and let your partner tell you which one sounded like English.

## 📰 Reading a science page

Six newspaper extracts carry the reading section: a study clearing mobile phones, a camera that catches skin cancer, a genetically-modified pea abandoned, bird flu in Turkey, the rise of internet shopping, a patch for diabetics. They bring a set of verbs that a science page cannot do without.

| Word              | What it means there              | Word            | What it means there                        |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **trigger**       | activate, set off                | **prick**       | make a tiny hole with a sharp point        |
| **dismiss**       | reject                           | **decapitate**  | cut off the head                           |
| **rule out**      | exclude                          | **poultry**     | chickens and other farm birds              |
| **leap** (leapt)  | increase suddenly                | **a patch**     | a small piece worn on the skin             |

Around them: a **handset**, a **threat**, **consumption**, a **nuisance**, something **available**, a **promising** result, a **malignant** tumour, and to **detect**, to **infect**, to **inflame**.

> ⚠️ **One word, two meanings — *strain*.** In *a strain of beans* it is a **variety** of a plant. In *the deadly bird flu strain* it is a **type of virus**. The dictionary offers you several entries; the sentence around the word chooses one, and that choice is the whole exercise.

**Three verdicts, not two.** When your book asks you to judge a statement against a text, it offers **true**, **false** and **not mentioned** — and the third is the one that catches people out. *False* means the text says the opposite. *Not mentioned* means the text says nothing about it, neither for nor against. A statement you happen to know is true in life is still **not mentioned** if the text is silent on it.

## 💬 Saying what you approve of, and what you doubt

Section Four puts you in a role play: a parent who **disapproves** of computer games against a son or daughter who **approves** of them. The module gives you the phrases.

| The move                     | The phrases                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| open an opinion              | Do you think that … · In my opinion · To my mind · As far as I am concerned |
| warn that criticism is coming | Frankly, … · Honestly, …                                            |
| state a preference           | **I'd rather + base verb + than + base verb**                       |
| half-concede, then object     | It sounds interesting, but …                                        |
| doubt                        | I have my doubts about … · You haven't convinced me yet             |

> ⚠️ **I'd rather** has two shapes and they are not interchangeable. Before **your own** action it takes two bare verbs: _I'd **rather use** a computer **than write** everything by hand._ ("I'd rather to use" ✗ · "I'd rather using" ✗ · "than to write" ✗). Before **somebody else's** action it takes a subject and a past form: _I'd **rather you came** early._

The section also asks what a computer is for, and the answer it wants is a list of nine: **send e-mail**, **write a letter**, **do calculations**, **surf the internet**, **print**, **listen to music**, **watch DVDs**, **record CDs**, **play games**. The gadgets around it are a **game console**, an **MP3 player**, a **digital** camera, a **DVD**, a **CD** — and any of them is a **gadget**.

## ✍️ Writing to persuade

The writing section has one function: **persuading people to do things**. Before you write a line, your book makes you read two texts and fill in three boxes about each of them.

1. **Type of text** — the genre. An advertisement is not an article, and an article is not a letter.
2. **Expected readers** — the audience. Who is meant to read this, and what do they already care about?
3. **Purpose** — what the writer wants to happen once the page is closed.

A text that persuades **speaks to the reader and asks for something**: it does not merely explain, and it does not merely describe. Your book's own checklist is a good judge of a draft — does the text have a specific purpose, does it address specific readers, are the sentences related to one another, is the information arranged in the right order, are the linkers used properly, is anything unnecessary, and does the beginning catch the reader's attention?

> 🗡️ Then comes the **mini-project**, the module's own piece of work: nano technology, robotics, bioethics, genetic engineering, artificial intelligence or medical innovations. Choose one, search, and be ready to present it. It is the only place in the module where you decide the topic yourself.

## ⚖️ The debate the module is built on

Two speakers are recorded, one after the other, and neither of them is the book's opinion. You are expected to know both lists.

**For — the first speaker, a professor of reproductive medicine.** Gene therapy can **repair** damaged genes and **replace** missing ones. Used against cancer it **targets only the diseased cells**, avoiding the damage that radiation and chemical treatments do to healthy tissue: a woman whose breast cancer had already spread to major organs was injected with a genetically-engineered drug, her tumours shrank by 25 per cent in a year and had almost disappeared after three. **Germline** gene therapy could cure inherited disorders, including behavioural ones such as schizophrenia, or **insert genes that enhance intelligence or athletic performance**, and its benefit would be **passed on to later generations**. **Designer babies** could answer the wishes of parents who need a cure for a sick child. **Recombinant DNA** can show whether a suspect is guilty or innocent — four men convicted of a murder they had not committed were released when the evidence proved it — and it can establish the identity of the dead and determine ancestry.

**Against — the second speaker, a member of a medical ethics alliance.** Gene therapy has produced **fevers, abnormal blood clotting, partial paralysis and death** during testing: a young man died after a cold virus was inserted into his liver to cure a genetic disease, and his heart, liver and kidneys were harmed instead. There is an **ethical question of access** — will the technology reach people in poor, developing areas, or only rich, developed countries? **DNA testing** has consequences nobody asked for: it can tell people who wish to hide their identity **things they never wanted to know**. It has also become a **business**: funeral homes and burial services offer to keep samples for a fee, which raises questions of privacy and of ethics — what are the rights of the dead person, who may see the samples, where are they stored? **Designer babies** she calls crazy and unethical: children are of **inestimable value in themselves**, and selecting them for somebody else's benefit is contrary to their dignity. Genetic **disorders** are as much a social construct as a difference in genes — we are all disabled in some respect. And intelligence and athletic performance depend on **upbringing, nutrition, education, experience and healthy living** as much as on the networking of hundreds of genes. Ageing, illness and death are parts of a human biography; trying to eliminate them denies human evolution.

> 🏆 You can now name what was discovered and what was invented, reverse any word with the right prefix, fold a whole clause into a two-word adjective, forbid something without confusing it with what is merely impossible, and read a science page without being caught by *strain* or by *not mentioned*. Blessing or curse is still your answer to give — but from now on you give it in the module's own language, and that is what an examiner is listening for.
