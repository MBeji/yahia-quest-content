# ⚔️ In Time of Test, Family Is Best — Roles, Rules and the Space Between Two Generations

> 💡 "A family is not a list of names. It is a list of things people do for one another — and of the sentences they use to say so."

Every household runs on two invisible systems. The first is a **share-out of roles**: who provides, who comforts, who decides, who takes the rubbish down. The second is a **grammar of influence**: who *wants* somebody to do something, who *makes* them, who *lets* them, who simply *has* it done. This module gives you both — the words for the roles, the structures for the influence, and the phrases you need when the two generations under one roof do not see eye to eye.

## 🏰 Four roles hold a family together

A family functions when four jobs are covered. Two of them are **instrumental** — practical, organisational, material. Two of them are **affective** — they work on feelings.

| Role                                         | Type             | What it covers                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Provision of resources**                   | **instrumental** | money, food, clothing, shelter                                          |
| **Nurturance and support**                   | **affective**    | comfort, warmth, reassurance                                            |
| **Life skills development**                  | both, task by task | helping somebody grow: school, emotions, choosing a career path  |
| **Maintenance and management of the system** | **instrumental** | leadership, decision making, handling the finances, discipline, standards |

> 🗡️ The test is simple. Ask: **does this act move a thing, or does it move a feeling?** Ironing a shirt, paying a bill, fixing a bedtime → **instrumental**. Sitting with someone who has had a terrible week → **affective**. The same person does both all day long; it is the **act** you classify, not the person.

**The vocabulary of the roles**: to *provide* · to *nurture* · *comfort* · *warmth* · *reassurance* · *shelter* · a *resource* · *leadership* · *maintenance* · to *establish* clear roles · to *handle* the finances · a *standard* · to be *overburdened* (given far too much to carry) · to *share roles* · *chores* (the small daily jobs: hoovering, watering the plants, taking out the trash, setting the table, ironing, feeding the cat).

## ⚡ If + present + present — the automatic result

Most conditional sentences you already know talk about **one occasion**: _If it rains tomorrow, we **will** stay at home._ This module gives you a different one. When the result is **automatic or habitual** — it happens *every single time* — both halves stay in the **present**.

```
If  +  present   ,   present
    ( condition )   ( result )
```

- _If my father works nights, my mother **takes** the children to school._
- _If you keep your problems to yourself, nothing **gets** better._
- _Water **boils** if you heat it to a hundred degrees._

The two halves can change places. When the **if-clause comes first**, a comma separates them; when it comes second, there is **no comma**:

> _**If** roles are not fairly shared, some members **become** overburdened._
> _Some members **become** overburdened **if** roles are not fairly shared._

| The sentence says…                                     | Structure                       | Example                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| every time this happens, that follows — a rule          | **If + present, present**       | _If she raises her voice, the little one **cries**._        |
| on one future occasion, this will follow — a prediction | **If + present, will + verb**   | _If she raises her voice tonight, the little one **will cry**._ |

> ⚠️ **Never put `will` inside the if-clause.** _"If you will trust your children…"_ ✗ → _**If you trust** your children…_ ✓. The if-clause takes the present, whichever of the two structures you choose.

## 🗡️ want + object + to-infinitive — what one person wishes another would do

To say what somebody wishes **another person** would do, English uses one single pattern:

```
want  +  object (a person)  +  to  +  base verb
```

- _My grandmother **wants my brother to phone** her every Sunday._
- _They **don't want us to leave** before dinner._
- _**Does** your father **want you to study** abroad?_

Two details decide the mark:

1. the word after *want* is the person concerned: a noun (_wants **my brother** to phone_) or, **if it is a pronoun**, an object pronoun — *me, you, him, her, us, them* — never a subject pronoun;
2. the **to** is compulsory.

> ⚠️ English has **no that-clause after want**. _"My father wants that I choose engineering"_ ✗ → _My father **wants me to choose** engineering_ ✓. This is the single most frequent mistake of French- and Arabic-speaking students, because both languages say it with a subordinate clause.

The same pattern works with **would like** (a politer wish) and with **expect**: _I **would like you to meet** my uncle._ · _They **expect their daughter to succeed**._

## 🔑 make / let / have + object + base form — force, allow, arrange

Three verbs describe influence over somebody else. All three take the **object** and then the **base form of the verb — with no `to`**.

```
make / let / have  +  object  +  base verb
```

| Verb     | Meaning                                            | Example                                                    |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **make** | **force**, oblige — the person has no choice        | _My aunt **makes her sons tidy** the kitchen after meals._  |
| **let**  | **allow**, give permission                          | _Our parents **let us choose** our own subjects._           |
| **have** | **arrange** for it to be done — a job, a service    | _The head teacher **has the pupils on duty ring** the bell._ |

- **Negative and question** are built on the ordinary auxiliaries: _She **doesn't let** me stay out late._ · _**Did** they **make** you apologise?_
- The object is a person or a group: _makes **her sons** tidy_, _lets **us** choose_, _has **the pupils on duty** ring_.

> 🗡️ **The `to` is the border.** *want* keeps its **to** (_wants me **to** go_); *make*, *let* and *have* drop it (_makes me go_, _lets me go_, _has me go_). Learn the four together and the border stops moving.

> ⚠️ Watch **have**. In _She **has** the pupils ring the bell_, "has" is not the auxiliary of a perfect tense: it is the causative, and it means she arranged it — somebody else does the ringing. Compare _She **has rung** the bell_ (she rang it herself).

## 🧩 Building words for feelings and roles

You can double your vocabulary of family life without learning a single new root, by adding a **suffix** (which usually changes the word class) or a **prefix** (which usually reverses the meaning).

| Suffix    | Turns a word into… | Examples                                             |
| --------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **-ful**  | adjective, "full of" | care → **careful** · use → **useful** · help → **helpful** |
| **-less** | adjective, "without" | care → **careless** · use → **useless** · help → **helpless** |
| **-ly**   | adverb              | open → **openly** · polite → **politely** · quiet → **quietly** |
| **-ment** | noun                | agree → **agreement** · assign → **assignment** · disappoint → **disappointment** |
| **-ion**  | noun                | depress → **depression** · frustrate → **frustration** · express → **expression** |
| **-ness** | noun (from an adjective) | kind → **kindness** · lazy → **laziness** · sad → **sadness** |
| **-ance** | noun                | maintain → **maintenance** · appear → **appearance**      |

| Prefix   | Meaning        | Examples                                   |
| -------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **un-**  | not, the opposite | **unhappy** · **unkind** · **unfair**   |
| **im-**  | not (before m, p) | **impossible** · **impolite**           |
| **in-**  | not            | **incorrect** · **invisible**              |
| **dis-** | the opposite of an action | **disagree** · **disrespect** · **disobey** |
| **mis-** | badly, wrongly | **misunderstand** · **misconduct** · **misbehave** |

> ⚠️ **The reversing prefix is not chosen by a rule — it is learnt with the word.** English says _unkind_ but _impolite_, _disagree_ but _misunderstand_. There is no way to work it out from the sound; the only safe method is to meet the word inside a sentence and keep the pair together.

> 🗡️ Adverbs in **-ly** answer *how*, and they follow a verb: _She spoke **openly** about it._ Adjectives follow the verb **be** or stand before a noun: _She was **open** about it._ · _an **open** conversation_. Do not put an adverb where the sentence needs an adjective.

## 🔊 Pronunciation — the letters you do not say

English spells more letters than it says. Learn the silent letters in **families**, not one by one.

| Silent letter | Where                              | Words                        |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| **gh**        | after a vowel — **usually**        | ni**gh**t · dau**gh**ter · thou**gh**t — **but not always**: in lau**gh**ter, enou**gh**, cou**gh** it says /f/ |
| **k**         | before n                           | **k**now · **k**nee          |
| **b**         | after m, **and before t**          | com**b** · clim**b** · dou**b**t · de**b**t |
| **t**         | between s and le / en              | lis**t**en · cas**t**le      |
| **h**         | at the start of a few words, **and after w** | **h**our · **h**onest · **wh**ich · **wh**y |
| **l**         | before m, k, d                     | ca**l**m · wou**l**d         |
| **w**         | before r, **and in a few odd words** | **w**rite · **w**rong — ans**w**er · s**w**ord · t**w**o |

And the same letters do **not** always stand for the same sound. Compare:

- **love / above** — the same short vowel, the one in *but*.
- **daughter / laughter** — one letter apart on the page, two different vowels in the mouth.
- **the letters _ou_ hide several sounds** — c**ou**sin, y**ou**ng, c**ou**ntry take the vowel of *but*; h**ou**se and m**ou**th take the one of *now*; thr**ou**gh and gr**ou**p take the one of *too*; th**ou**ght and b**ou**ght take the one of *more*.
- **heart / heard** — the same four opening letters, two different vowels.
- **child / children** — the same root, a long vowel in the first, a short one in the second.

> 🗡️ Never trust the spelling to tell you the sound. Group words by **the sound you hear**, and write a familiar word beside each group as your reference.

## 💬 Saying it out loud — complaining, interrupting, disagreeing

A conversation about family life needs four kinds of phrase. Keep them ready.

| Function                | Phrases                                                                                     |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Complaining**         | _I am really…_ · _I feel like a…_ · _They want me to…_ · _Why should I…?_ · _I just wish…_    |
| **Interrupting**        | _Before you go on, let me…_ · _Could I stop you here for a moment?_ · _Could I question your last point?_ |
| **Disagreeing politely**| _You have a point, but…_ · _I see what you mean, but…_ · _Well, that might be true, but…_ · _What you say is interesting, however…_ |
| **Giving an opinion**   | _In my opinion,…_ · _To my mind,…_ · _As far as I am concerned,…_ · _I believe that…_          |

> 🗡️ Polite disagreement has **two moves**: first grant the other speaker something (_You have a point_), then object (_but…_). A reply that goes straight to the objection sounds like an attack, whatever the words.

**The idioms of the module** — they carry the whole topic:

| Expression                             | Meaning                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **see eye to eye (with)**              | agree entirely with somebody                                    |
| **get in somebody's hair**             | annoy somebody by being constantly around                       |
| **jump down somebody's throat**        | criticise or scold somebody far too quickly                     |
| **blow a problem out of proportion**   | treat something small as if it were huge                        |
| **barge in**                           | enter rudely, without being invited                             |
| **feel like a puppet**                 | feel that other people control everything you do                |
| **can't help it**                      | be unable to stop yourself                                      |
| **for heaven's sake**                  | an exclamation of impatience                                    |

## ⚠️ Three traps that cost marks

1. **The that-clause after want.** _"My mother wants that I stay"_ ✗ → _My mother **wants me to stay**_ ✓. And the pronoun is an object: _"wants she to stay"_ ✗ → _**wants her to stay**_ ✓.
2. **The stray `to` after make, let and have.** _"He makes me to work"_ ✗ → _He **makes me work**_ ✓ · _"They let us to go"_ ✗ → _They **let us go**_ ✓. Only *want* keeps its *to*.
3. **`will` inside an if-clause.** _"If you will help me, the work goes faster"_ ✗ → _**If you help** me, the work goes faster_ ✓.
