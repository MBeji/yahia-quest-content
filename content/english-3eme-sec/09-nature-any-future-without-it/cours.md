# ⚔️ Nature: Any Future Without It? — Reading a Disaster, and Speaking About What Comes Next

> 💡 "There is little we can do to predict natural disasters and even less to prevent them." Your book prints that sentence in a bubble and then spends twenty pages asking you whether you believe it.

This is the last module of the book, and it turns outwards. The topic is **ecology**; the two subtopics are **environmental issues** and **natural disasters**. You will sort the threats to the planet into the ones nobody caused and the ones we caused ourselves, listen to a mother tell what a tsunami did to her family, read a study that says global warming is human-made, argue about how green you really are, and write the story of a disaster.

And it gives you three instruments of language, each tied to a different way of standing in time. **`must have` + a past participle** concludes something about a past you did not witness. **`will`** and **`be going to`** divide the future between them. And the **past tenses**, held together by the link between a **cause** and an **effect**, are what turns a list of events into a story.

**What it means.** `must have` + a past participle expresses a **deduction about the past**: the speaker did not witness the event, finds the evidence afterwards, and states the conclusion he draws from it. Your grammar summary's own example is exactly that shape — *Everything is wet this morning. It must have rained last night.* The wetness is what you see; the rain is what you conclude.

**Three forms, three jobs, and they are constantly confused.**

| You want to say                                   | You write                     | Example                                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I conclude that something **is so now**            | **must** + base verb          | _Nobody answers. She **must be** out._                        |
| I conclude that something **happened earlier**     | **must have** + past participle| _The line is dead. The pole **must have come** down._         |
| Somebody **was obliged** to do it                  | **had to** + base verb        | _The family **had to** sleep in the school hall._             |

> ⚠️ **`must have` is not the past of the obligation `must`.** The past of *You must leave* is *You had to leave* — a real obligation that really applied. *You must have left* is a conclusion about you, drawn by somebody who was not there. Those are two different sentences about two different things, and choosing the wrong one changes who is speaking and what he knows.

> 🗡️ **A modal is always followed by a base form, so `have` never changes.** *must **have** gone* ✓ — never "must has gone" ✗, never "must had gone" ✗, and never "must went" ✗, which is the commonest fault of all: the past is carried by the **participle**, not by the modal.

**And when the conclusion is a negative one.** This module works the positive form, but you will need the other one as soon as you write. To conclude that something did **not** happen, English normally reaches for **can't have** or **couldn't have**: *She **can't have** seen the warning — she was already on the road.* Keep `mustn't` for what it does on its own, which is to **forbid**: *You mustn't go down to the beach.* (You will hear *must not have* used for a negative conclusion, especially in North America; **can't have** is the form to write here.)

> 🗡️ **Do not confuse `must have + participle` with `should have + participle`.** *The warning **must have** reached the village* concludes that it did. *The warning **should have** reached the village* says that it ought to have — and, nine times out of ten, that it did not. One is a deduction; the other is a reproach.

> ⚠️ **One booby trap in the reading, and it is not grammar at all.** The words *must have* also turn up in sentences where `have` is the ordinary verb: *We **must have** the road cleared before the lorries come* means we must **get somebody to clear** it. Read what follows. `must have` + a **past participle alone** (_must have left_) is the deduction. A **noun** after it means *have* is the ordinary verb — and the sentence then obliges only when a **past participle follows that noun**: _we must have the road **cleared**_ = we must get it cleared. A noun on its own settles nothing: _You **must have** a lot of patience_ concludes, it does not oblige.

## 🔮 `will` and `be going to` — two futures, and where each of them comes from

Your book prints eight uses, four for each form. Learn them as eight, because the examination asks you to name them.

| **will** + base verb (no *to*)                                                     | **be going to** + base verb                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a habitual action** — _These birds will come back next year._                       | **a prior plan** — _I'm going to meet them at the airport._                                          |

**Two questions sort almost every sentence you will meet.**

1. **When was the decision taken?** Before you opened your mouth → **be going to** (the prior plan). At the moment you open it → **will** (the offer, the promise). *I'm going to plant twenty trees on Saturday* — the trees are bought. *That box looks heavy. I'll carry it for you* — you had not thought of it a second ago.
2. **Where does the prediction come from?** From something in front of you that anybody could point at → **be going to**. From what you believe, work out or hope → **will**, very often with *I think*, *I expect*, *I'm sure*, *probably*. *The level has dropped nine metres; the reservoir is going to run dry.* against *If the drought holds, the town will be short of water.*

> 🗡️ **The two uses that are always forgotten.** *be going to* also carries an **order or a refusal** — *You're going to switch that tap off, like it or not* is not a prediction at all, it is an instruction wearing the clothes of one. And *will* also carries a **habit**, something that regularly happens: *These birds will come back to the lake every spring.*

> ⚠️ **Both forms are often possible in the same sentence, and your own book says so.** In the exercise at the end of the section it accepts, for one item, either *the Americans **are going to send** a camera into space* or *the Americans **will send** one — the plan and the expectation are both defensible there. So do not hunt for a rule that forbids one form; look at the situation and choose the form that says what the situation makes true. Where the situation is neutral, either will pass.

> ⚠️ **Three faults of form, and none of them is about meaning.** *will* takes the **base verb with no `to`**: "I will to help" ✗. *be going to* needs its **`be`**, which agrees: *I'**m** going to*, *he'**s** going to*, *they'**re** going to* — "he going to" ✗. And after *if* in an ordinary condition, English uses the **present**, not a future: *If the drought **holds**, the town will be short of water* ✓ — "If the drought will hold" ✗.

**And the phrases for reacting**, which your book sets out in three columns of rising strength. Word choice carries most of the difference, and the tone of voice carries the rest.

| **Mild surprise / interest**                                                                 | **Strong surprise / disbelief**                                                        | **Shock / dismay**                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Really? · That's interesting · How nice to + verb! · I didn't know that. · Fancy + verb-ing! · This is a surprise! | Wow, that's amazing! · That's unbelievable! · You're joking! · I can't believe it! · That's incredible! · You're pulling my leg! | Oh, no! · How shocking! · That's terrible! · That's awful! · That's horrible! |

> 🗡️ **The middle column and the third one are not the same reaction, and this is what the exercise tests.** *You're joking!* and *You're pulling my leg!* say **I find that hard to believe** — they question the fact. *That's awful!* and *How shocking!* accept the fact completely and **condemn it**. If a partner has just given you a figure he stands by, answering *You're pulling my leg!* tells him you think he is wrong; answering *That's terrible!* tells him you have understood.

> ⚠️ **A wording in your book's example dialogue that you should not copy.** It speaks of *the highest earthquake*. English does not measure an earthquake in height: say the **strongest** earthquake, or, if you mean the number of victims, the **deadliest**.

## 📖 Writing the story of a disaster — the tenses, and the links between events

| Tense                                       | What it carries                                                    | From your book's own texts                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **past simple**                             | the events of the story, one after another                          | the mud **swept** into the village · my wife **rushed** in · we **grabbed** each other and **ran** · the mudslide **caught** up with us · I **lost** my grasp |
| **past progressive** (*was / were* + -ing)  | what was already going on when an event dropped into it, and two things happening at the same time | my wife **was shivering** out of fear                            |
| **past perfect** (*had* + participle)       | what was already over before the moment the story has reached        | my wife told me that the school **had already been engulfed**    |

Look hard at that last one, because it does two things at once. **had already been engulfed** is a **past perfect** (the school was covered before she told him) **and a passive** (the school is the thing the mud did it to). Stack the two and *be* goes into the past perfect: `had been` + past participle.

> 🗡️ **How to decide between the past simple and the past progressive.** Ask which action **lasted**. The long one, already under way, takes the progressive; the short one that interrupts it takes the simple past: *I **was carrying** the last box out **when** the roof **went**.* Put both in the simple past and you get two events side by side, one after the other — a different story.

> ⚠️ **Do not over-mark the past perfect.** Your grammar summary says it plainly: when two actions are close in time or closely connected, the past perfect is not usually used. *When he arrived at the hotel, he asked for a room* — nobody could get that order wrong, so marking it adds nothing. Keep the past perfect for the moment when the story reaches back to something that was already finished.

### Cause and effect — the link that turns a list into a story

This is the second half of the section's grammar, and it is what the examiner means when he writes *your story has no shape*. A narrative that only says **what** happened is a list. A narrative that says **why** is a story.

English carries that link in four different kinds of word, and choosing the wrong kind is a grammar mistake, not a style one.

```
a conjunction  + a whole clause  :  because / as / since   the dam gave way
a preposition  + a noun group    :  because of / owing to / due to / out of   the pressure
a connector    + a new sentence  :  so / therefore / as a result / consequently / that is why
a verb         + its object      :  cause / bring about / lead to / result in
```

- _The village was flooded **because** the dam gave way._ — a conjunction: a subject and a verb follow it.
- _The village was flooded **because of** the pressure on the dam._ — a preposition: a noun group follows it, with no verb of its own.
- _The dam gave way, **so** the village was flooded._ — the effect follows.
- _The rain of the previous week **brought about** the collapse of the hillside._ — the verb itself does the work.

> 🗡️ **The swap, and it is the single most useful thing in this section.** *B **because** A* and *A**, so** B* say exactly the same thing — but the two halves change places, because **because** looks back at the cause and **so** looks forward to the effect. Write any cause-and-effect pair both ways when you revise; the examiner asks for the second one as often as the first.

> ⚠️ **result in and result from point in opposite directions, and one letter separates them.** *The rain **resulted in** the collapse* — the rain is the cause. *The collapse **resulted from** the rain* — the rain is still the cause, but now it comes after the verb. Get them the wrong way round and your sentence claims that a hillside brought down a week of rain.

> ⚠️ **since has two lives.** *We have lived here **since** 2019* fixes a starting point in time. ***Since** the drain was blocked, the water had nowhere to go* gives a reason. The word is the same; only what follows it and what the sentence is doing tell you which one you are reading.

**And the shape of the story itself.** Your book asks for fifteen to twenty lines about a natural disaster you witnessed, heard about, saw in a film or invented, and it prints its own checklist: correct narrative tenses · descriptive expressions · a clear division of the ideas and events · a chronological order · a logical link of causes and effects · punctuation · spelling. Then: read your draft through, improve it, exchange it with a classmate for proofreading, and write the final version.

> 🗡️ **Description and causation are two different jobs, and a good narrative does both.** *The water was cold and black* is description — it makes the reader see. *The drain under the road had been blocked since the spring* is causation — it makes the reader understand. A story with only the first is a photograph; a story with only the second is a report.

## 🔊 Rhythm and intonation — what you can actually check on the page

The pronunciation work of this module is **rhythm and intonation**, and your book practises it by making you read one anxious paragraph aloud while the recording plays. You have no recording here, so here is what you can see and check with your eyes.

**English is spoken on a beat.** The beats fall at roughly regular intervals, and they land on the words that carry the **meaning** — nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, demonstratives, negatives. The words that only hold the sentence together — articles, prepositions, ordinary pronouns, auxiliaries, conjunctions — are squeezed in between, fast and quiet.

> _The **wa**ter **came** through the **door** before we could **reach** it._

Four beats: *water*, *came*, *door*, *reach*. The number of small words between two beats changes nothing — they are compressed to fit, which is why they are so hard to catch when somebody speaks quickly. That is the whole difference between English and a language in which every syllable takes about the same time; it is not a detail of accent, it is the shape of the language.

> 🗡️ The beat moves only when the speaker is **contrasting** something: *I said the **back** door, not the front one.* Outside that, the meaning-words take it.

**Punctuation shows you the sense groups.** A comma, a semicolon and a full stop are places to breathe, and their spacing is the second half of the rhythm. That is why a writer describing panic writes short sentences one after another: each one is its own breath group, and the reading gets faster and harder. A long sentence with two or three commas slows the reader down. When your book gives you a passage to read aloud, count the full stops before you start — they tell you what tone the paragraph is in.

**Intonation — where the voice goes at the end.** Four patterns cover almost everything you will read aloud:

| At the end of                                          | The voice normally      | Example                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| a statement                                            | **falls**               | _The road is closed._ ↘                    |
| a question that begins with a WH word                  | **falls**               | _Where is the school?_ ↘                   |
| a yes/no question                                      | **rises**               | _Did you reach the hotel?_ ↗               |
| each item of a list, except the last, which falls      | **rises, then falls**   | _I rang the phone ↗, the hotel ↗, the office ↘_ |

And an exclamation of shock takes a **high fall** — the voice starts above the line and drops. That is the tone that turns *That's terrible!* into something you can hear the speaker mean, and it is why your book says, in the section on surprise, that word choice **and** intonation together decide how much you express.

> ⚠️ **One small thing your book prints two ways, and only one of them is English.** The passage you read aloud says *the news got **worse** by the minute*; the teacher's copy of the same passage prints *worst*. The sentence compares each minute with the one before it, so it needs the comparative: **worse**.
