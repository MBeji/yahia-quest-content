# 📜 Summary: Nature — Any Future Without It?

- **The frame** — the last module of the book. Topic: **ecology**; subtopics: **environmental issues** and **natural disasters**. It opens on a statement to be debated — *there is little we can do to predict natural disasters and even less to prevent them* — and gives you three instruments: **`must have` + past participle**, **`will` against `be going to`**, and the **past tenses** held together by the link between a **cause** and an **effect**. Pronunciation: **rhythm and intonation**.
- **Threats to the environment** — air: air pollution, **ozone depletion**, acid rain, global warming · water: sea and water pollution, **water scarcity** · land: **deforestation**, garbage dumping · living things: **extinction** of species, imbalance in the ecological system · the earth: **earthquake**, **volcanic eruption**, fire · water and wind: **flood**, **drought**, **tsunami**, tropical storms (**typhoons** in the Pacific, **hurricanes** in the Atlantic), **tornadoes** or **twisters**. Sorted into two columns: **natural** disasters happen whether or not anybody is there (earthquake, eruption, tsunami, drought, flood, storm, tornado); **man-made** ones exist because of what people do (pollution, deforestation, garbage dumping, an oil spill, acid rain). ⚠️ **Acid rain** is the hard one — the rain is natural, the acid in it comes from factories and vehicles. Test: *would this still happen if nobody lived here?*
- **The five definitions** — a **drought** = a long period of dry weather that damages the crops · a **flood** = a great flowing or overflowing of water over land · a **tsunami** (tidal wave) = an unusually large sea wave produced by a seaquake or an undersea eruption (Japanese *tsu* = harbour, *nami* = wave) · an **earthquake** = vibrations in the earth's crust caused when strained rocks break and rebound abruptly, rated on the **Richter scale** from 1 to 10 · a **volcano** = a **vent** (a hole) in the crust through which lava, steam and ash are **expelled**, continuously or at irregular intervals. A volcano is the opening, an **eruption** is what comes out; a tsunami is the **wave**, not what set it off. **A tsunami loses speed in shallower water, but its height grows.** Mount St Helens was **dormant** — sleeping, not dead — for 123 years before **erupting ferociously**. Words: **abrupt**, **alert**, **ash**, **catastrophe**, **expel**, **ferociously**, **lava**, **motto**, **predict** (say in advance) against **prevent** (stop it happening), **rate**, **rupture**, **shallow**, **species**, **steam**, **stumble**, **threat**, **tidal**, **vent**, **wave**. The **poster** of the section carries a **motto**: *If it's not far, don't go by car* · *Do it right, switch off the light* — an instruction, short, and rhymed.
- **The listening passage** — a real account published in a British newspaper in November 2005. **Sandra Bell** tells what happened to her daughter **Natalie** (Nat) and Nat's husband **Andy**, in four parts: (1) they met at a swimming club about ten years earlier, married on 22 May, **postponed** the honeymoon until Christmas when Andy's design company would be quiet, planned New Zealand and at the last minute booked a beachside bungalow in Thailand; (2) Nat telephoned on Christmas Day; the next morning **Ted**, Sandra's partner, put the television on and the news was about a tsunami where they were staying — Sandra rang the mobile, the foreign office and the hotel and searched the internet; (3) the hotel's website reported an extensive loss of life; *I refused to think the worst*; Andy was identified by the inscription in his wedding ring, and Sandra drew the conclusion that gives the module its grammar; (4) the family flew out, Natalie's body was found on 3 September, and they set up a **fund** for children **orphaned** by the tsunami and for the schools it destroyed, and hoped to buy boats for the **fishermen**. Words: **partner**, **wedding**, **honeymoon**, **postpone** (= put off until later, not *decide* and not *celebrate*), **book** (v), **smash**, **pound** (of a heart), **pray**, **glimpse**, **trap**, **turmoil**, **mourner** / **mourn**, **grief**, **sorrowful**, **dreadful**, **corpse**, **orphaned**, **evacuate**, **livelihood**, **rotting**, **spotlessly**. **Expressing sympathy**: *My heart goes out to…* (the book's own printed formula) · *I'm so sorry to hear that* · *That must be dreadful for you* · *Please accept my sympathy* — and never a bright side. The hurricane report of the homework: roads **flooded** and washed away, houses **swept** away, runway lights **damaged**, people **injured**, **travellers** whose **holidays** were cancelled, evacuation from the **hotels**, a **fisherman** who **disappeared** under the waves, the **devastation** caused — and most of its verbs are **passive**.
- **`must` + perfect infinitive** — form: **must + have + past participle**. Meaning: a **deduction about the past** — you did not see the event, you find the evidence afterwards, you state your conclusion. *Everything is wet this morning. It **must have rained** last night.* · *If Andy was dead, Nat **must have been** too.* Three forms to keep apart: **must** + base = a conclusion about **now** (*She must be out*) · **must have** + participle = a conclusion about **earlier** (*The pole must have come down*) · **had to** + base = a real **obligation** in the past (*The family had to sleep in the school hall*). ⚠️ `must have` is **not** the past of the obligation *must*; that is **had to**. A modal always takes a base form, so *have* never changes: *must have gone* ✓, "must has gone" ✗, "must had gone" ✗, "must went" ✗. The negative conclusion is **can't have** / **couldn't have** (*She can't have seen the warning*); **mustn't** on its own **forbids**. Do not confuse **must have** (it did happen — a deduction) with **should have** (it ought to have — a reproach). And beware the ordinary verb: *We **must have** the road cleared* = we must get it cleared. A **participle** after *must have* = deduction; a **noun** = obligation.
- **Global warming is human made** — researchers at the **Scripps Institute of Oceanography** found clear evidence of **human-produced warming in the world's oceans**, likely to affect water **resources** worldwide; the **findings** remove much of the **uncertainty** in the debate. Many world leaders have closed their ears to it **so that they do not have to limit their economic activity or introduce expensive pollution controls** — one of the reasons America has not signed the **Kyoto Treaty**; the US government prefers to believe the warming is natural, caused by volcanoes and solar energy. **Professor Tim Barnett** calls it the most **compelling** evidence yet and says the evolution can be **simulated**; the **implications** are about water — **accelerated** glacier **melting** in the South American Andes and western China, and millions at risk without **adequate** summertime water. The six synonyms: affect = **impact** · doubt = **uncertainty** · research = **findings** · consequences = **implications** · sufficient = **adequate** · quickened = **accelerated**. ⚠️ The text prints *sign up the Kyoto Protocol*; the correct English, printed on the next page, is *sign up **to***.
- **`will` against `be going to`** — the book's eight uses. **will** + base verb (no *to*): a **habitual action** (*These birds will come back next year*) · a **promise** (*I'll finish this work by seven tonight*) · an **offer** (*If you need that medicine now, I'll get it for you*) · **something expected** (*I think Brazilians will win the match*). **be going to**: a **prior plan** (*I'm going to meet them at the airport*) · a **prediction** from evidence in front of you (*Look at those clouds! It's going to rain*) · **commands and refusals** (*You are going to take that medicine whether you like it or not*) · a **permanent state** (*Their new house is going to look over the river*). Two sorting questions: **when was the decision taken?** — before you spoke → *be going to*; as you speak → *will*. **Where does the prediction come from?** — from something anybody could point at → *be going to*; from what you believe or work out, often with *I think* → *will*. ⚠️ Both forms are often possible in one sentence, and the book's own answer key accepts either in one item. Faults of form: *will* takes the base verb with no *to*; *be going to* needs its **be**, which agrees; and after *if* in an ordinary condition English uses the **present** ("If the drought will hold" ✗).
- **How green are you** — the quiz asks about transport, the **tap** left running while you **brush** your teeth, smokers, the lights as you move from room to room, the television left on **standby**, energy-saving **light bulbs**, and a dripping tap. ⚠️ The book prints **no key** and sends you to your teacher; a **bicycle**, a tap turned off and a television switched off completely are the three that are not in doubt. Words: **tap**, **light bulb**, **brush**, **switch off**, **spill**, **tanker**, **trash**.
- **Reacting — three columns of rising strength.** *Mild surprise / interest*: **Really?** · **That's interesting** · **How nice to +V!** · **I didn't know that.** · **Fancy +Ving!** · **This is a surprise!** — *Strong surprise / disbelief*: **Wow, that's amazing!** · **That's unbelievable!** · **You're joking!** · **I can't believe it!** · **That's incredible!** · **You're pulling my leg!** — *Shock / dismay*: **Oh, no!** · **How shocking!** · **That's terrible!** · **That's awful!** · **That's horrible!** The middle column **questions the fact**; the third **accepts it and condemns it**. ⚠️ The book's example dialogue says *the highest earthquake*; say the **strongest**, or the **deadliest**.
- **Writing a narrative** — two survivors' accounts: **Kioka Williams** (hurricane Katrina, New Orleans, 29 August 2005) and **Florencio Libaton** (the Philippines mudslide of 2006). Words: **chaos**, **scream**, **yell** (cry out in fright), **perish** (die), **float**, **disintegrate**, **crack**, **wipe** clear, **loot** / **looters** (steal goods by force in the disorder after a disaster), **sweep** (carry everything away), **rush**, **collapse**, **engulf** (swallow up, cover), **grab** (take roughly), **grasp**, **shiver**, **pin** (make unable to move), **current**, **bitterly**.
- **The three past tenses** — **past simple** for the events, one after another (*swept*, *rushed*, *grabbed*, *ran*, *caught*, *lost*) · **past progressive** for what was already going on when an event dropped into it (*was shivering*) · **past perfect** for what was already over before the moment the story has reached — and the module's own example stacks it with the **passive**: *the school **had already been engulfed*** = `had been` + past participle. Decide between simple and progressive by asking which action **lasted**: *I **was carrying** the last box out **when** the roof **went**.* ⚠️ Do not over-mark the past perfect: when two actions are close in time or closely connected, it is not usually used.
- **Cause and effect** — four kinds of word, and choosing the wrong kind is a grammar mistake: a **conjunction** + a whole clause (**because**, **as**, **since**) · a **preposition** + a noun group (**because of**, **owing to**, **due to**, **out of**) · a **connector** opening a new sentence (**so**, **therefore**, **as a result**, **consequently**, **that is why**) · a **verb** (**cause**, **bring about**, **lead to**, **result in**). **The swap**: *B **because** A* = *A**, so** B* — the halves change places, because *because* looks back at the cause and *so* looks forward to the effect. ⚠️ **result in** and **result from** point opposite ways: *the rain resulted in the collapse* = *the collapse resulted from the rain*. ⚠️ **since** is both a starting point in time and a reason. The writing task: 15–20 lines on a natural disaster, checked for narrative tenses, descriptive expressions, a clear division of ideas, chronological order, the logical link of causes and effects, punctuation and spelling. **Description** makes the reader see; **causation** makes the reader understand — a story needs both.
- **Rhythm and intonation** — English is spoken **on a beat**, and the beat lands on the words that carry the **meaning** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, negatives); articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries and conjunctions are squeezed in between: *The **wa**ter came through the **door** before we could **reach** it* has four beats. The number of small words between two beats changes nothing — they are compressed to fit. The beat moves only for **contrast** (*I said the **back** door*). **Punctuation shows the sense groups**: short sentences in a row make short breath groups, which is how a writer writes panic. **Intonation**: a statement ↘ · a WH question ↘ · a yes/no question ↗ · each item of a list ↗ except the last ↘ · an exclamation of shock takes a **high fall**. ⚠️ The passage read aloud says *the news got **worse** by the minute* — the comparative, not *worst*.
