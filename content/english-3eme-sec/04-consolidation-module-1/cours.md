# ⚔️ Consolidation Module 1 — Two Modules on One Table, and Thirteen Ways of Being Asked

> 💡 "You do not revise by reading the same page twice. You revise by being asked the same thing in a shape you have never met before."

Nothing new about the world is taught here. Module One gave you the household — who provides, who comforts, who decides, and what happens when the two generations under one roof stop seeing eye to eye. Module Two gave you the language of giving. This module puts both back on the table and turns them into **thirteen tasks**, each with its own shape: a word bank to place, a form to build, a verb to marry to its noun, a text to punctuate, a paragraph to reassemble, two tenses to choose between, four sentences to rewrite, a crossword to fill, sixteen phrasal verbs to tell apart, a row of modals, a biography with half its words missing, a paragraph in two halves, and a table with three columns. Learn the **shape** and the **notion** together: in the exam the notion never comes to you naked.

## 🏠 The words of the household

Twelve words carry the whole of the family text, and each one has a slot it fits and no other.

| Word                  | What it does                                                              |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **members**           | the people who belong to the family                                       |
| **within**            | inside — _roles are shared **within** the family_                         |
| **a household**       | everybody living under one roof, seen as one unit                         |
| **responsibilities**  | the things you are answerable for                                         |
| **to be assigned**    | to be given to somebody by somebody else — _the role **assigned** to her_ |
| **to be expected**    | what others count on you to do                                            |
| **to be required**    | what the situation obliges — stronger than *expected*                     |
| **to fulfil** a role  | to carry it out completely (American spelling *fulfill*)                  |
| **to arise**          | to come into being — _problems **arise**_                                 |
| **assistance**        | help                                                                      |
| **understanding**     | the grasp somebody has of something                                       |
| **overburdened**      | given far more to carry than one person can carry                         |

> ⚠️ **arise** is not *rise* and not *raise*. A problem **arises** (it comes into being, and it takes no object); the sun **rises** (it goes up, no object); you **raise** a question or a child (there is always an object). Three verbs, one family, three jobs.

> 🗡️ **required** and **expected** are not interchangeable. What is *expected* of you is what the others hope and assume; what is *required* is what the situation will not let you avoid. A mother is *expected* to be patient; a signature is *required* on the form.

## 🔗 The verb that goes with its noun

English marries certain verbs to certain nouns, and the pair has to be learnt whole. You cannot work it out; nothing in the meaning of *maintain* announces *discipline*.

| The pair                    | It means                                              |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **take out** the rubbish    | carry the household waste outside (American: *the trash*) |
| **make** decisions          | decide                                                |
| **maintain** discipline     | keep the rules working                                |
| **provide** affection       | give warmth and love                                  |
| **take care of** children   | look after them                                       |
| **overcome** a problem      | get past it — also *overcome a difficulty*            |

> ⚠️ **take care** keeps its **of**; **look after** takes none. _She **takes care of** the twins_ ✓ · _She **looks after** the twins_ ✓ — "She takes care the twins" ✗ · "She looks after of the twins" ✗. The two mean the same thing and are built differently, which is exactly why they are asked together.

## 🧱 One root, three columns

A word family is a row with three cells: **verb · noun · adjective**. Fill the cell the slot needs, and read the slot before you read the word.

| Verb           | Noun             | Adjective                     |
| -------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------- |
| decide         | decision         | decided                       |
| grow           | growth           | grown                         |
| behave         | behaviour        | behavioural                   |
| expect         | expectation      | expected                      |
| frustrate      | frustration      | frustrated / frustrating      |
| satisfy        | satisfaction     | satisfactory / satisfied      |
| cooperate      | cooperation      | cooperative                   |
| volunteer      | volunteerism     | voluntary                     |
| sacrifice      | sacrifice        | sacrificial                   |
| —              | charity          | charitable                    |
| —              | philanthropy     | philanthropic                 |

Three things the table teaches at once:

1. **A cell can be empty, and that is a fact of the language, not an oversight.** English has no ordinary verb on *charity* or on *philanthropy*: you say _to give to charity_, _to do philanthropic work_. Do not invent "to charity" ✗ or "to philanthropise" ✗.
2. **One verb can feed two adjectives that face opposite ways.** _frustrating_ describes what causes the feeling, _frustrated_ describes the person who has it: _a **frustrating** afternoon_ left him **frustrated**.
3. **The same form can sit in two columns.** *sacrifice* is the verb and the noun, unchanged.

And the derivations the family text lives on: _health → **healthy**_ · _understand → **understanding**_ · _support → **supportive**_ · _facility → **facilities**_ · _develop → **development**_ · _difficult → **difficulties**_ · _effective → **effectively**_.

> ⚠️ **good** builds its adverb from another root altogether: _good → **well**_. _The family works **well**_ ✓ — "works good" ✗. And *well* is also an adjective meaning *in good health*: _I hope you are **well**_.

> 🗡️ A verb used as the **subject** of a sentence goes into **-ing**: _**Establishing** clear roles helps the family to function._ The same -ing form follows a preposition: _instead of **making** things easier_ · _without **hurting** anybody_.

## ✒️ Punctuation and capitals

A text handed to you without a single mark is a test of whether you can hear where a sentence ends.

- Every sentence **opens on a capital** and **closes on a full stop** — or on a question mark, or on an exclamation mark.
- **Proper nouns** take a capital: people, towns, countries, months, days, languages.
- An **adjective built on a proper noun keeps the capital**: an *African* father, an *American* separatist, a *Tunisian* association.
- A **comma** goes after a long opening phrase, before the subject arrives: _Son of a slave who had bought his freedom, he became a seaman._
- A **comma** separates the items of a list.
- The **apostrophe** marks possession: _his **father's** freedom_.
- **I** is always a capital, wherever it stands.

> ⚠️ **An important word is not a proper noun.** _He became a seaman and a property owner_ — no capitals: those are ordinary jobs, however impressive. And a comma does not open a capital: only the **end of a sentence** does — a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark. The exceptions are the ones you already know: a **proper noun**, and the word **I**, keep their capital wherever they stand — _He spent August in Djerba, **T**abarka and **B**izerte._

## 🧵 Putting a paragraph back together

Six pieces are printed in the wrong order and you have to rebuild the paragraph. The order is never a matter of taste — each piece carries a mark that says where it can and cannot stand.

| The mark on the piece                          | What it tells you                                    |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| it opens on **and**, **then**, **so**          | it can never come first                              |
| it opens on an **-ing** form (*donating…*)     | it hangs on the sentence before it                   |
| it names a person **in full**                  | it comes before every piece that says *he* or *she*  |
| it opens on **the** + a list of names          | it completes a verb printed in an earlier piece      |
| it carries the **first date**                  | it usually opens the whole thing                     |

> 🗡️ The safe method is backwards. Find the only piece that can **open** — a full name, a date, no pronoun waiting for an antecedent — and put it down. Then ask of each remaining piece: *what has to be said before this one makes sense?* The paragraph builds itself.

## 🕰️ The two pasts — and how to say why

The choice between the present perfect and the simple past is the most asked question of your year. You met it in Module Two; here you must also be able to **say why**, because the consolidation asks you to justify as often as to fill in.

| The reason                                                | The tense       | Example                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| the period runs from a past point **up to today**          | present perfect | _He **has been** in hospital for the last three weeks._ |
| the result **counts now**                                  | present perfect | _She **has broken** her arm._ (it is still broken)   |
| **not yet** — expected, and still missing                  | present perfect | _He **hasn't recovered** yet._                       |
| a moment **named and finished**                            | simple past     | _He **fell** off a ladder last Tuesday._             |
| a chain of finished events, told in order                  | simple past     | _He **was taken** to hospital and **spent** a week there._ |

> ⚠️ **for** does not choose the tense on its own; the two ends of the period do. _He **has been** in hospital **for** three weeks_ (he is still there) against _He **stayed** in hospital **for** three weeks_ (and came out). **since**, on the other hand, opens a period that is still running, so it calls a present perfect: _They **have run** the same stall in the market ever **since** they **left** school._ — the clause after *since* usually stays in the simple past, and the main verb does not follow it there.

> 🗡️ **it's (high) time** takes a past form for something **overdue right now**: _It's high time we **planned** something different._ — "it's high time we plan" ✗. The past there is not a past at all; it is the same unreal past you use after *wish*, *if only*, *as if*, *suppose* and *would rather*.

## 🔄 Four rewrites you must be able to write

You are given a sentence and the **first words** of its replacement. The opening decides everything that follows.

| You are given                                                  | You must start with | And you write                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _I'm sorry I don't have enough money to donate._                | *I wish…*           | _I **wish I had** enough money to donate._                            |
| _Parents have to be understanding; otherwise they may have communication problems._ | *If parents…* | _**If parents aren't** understanding, they **may have** communication problems._ |
| _Shall I phone you at 3 pm?_                                    | *I'd rather you…*   | _I'd **rather you phoned** at 4 instead._                             |
| _Some parents don't allow their children to have their own way._ | *Some parents don't let…* | _Some parents **don't let their children have** their own way._ |

What each one costs you:

1. **wish + past** turns a regret about **now** into a past form: *I don't have* → *I had*. The sentence stays about today.
2. **otherwise → if + not**. *Otherwise* announces what happens if the first half fails, so the rewrite puts that failure into the if-clause and keeps **may** in the result.
3. **would rather + subject + past** when you want somebody **else** to act: _I'd rather **you phoned**_. When the subject does not change, no past and no *to*: _I'd **rather stay** at home._
4. **allow → let** costs you the **to**. _allow somebody **to** do_ ✓ · _let somebody **do**_ ✓ — "let them to have" ✗. And the negative is built on *do*: _don't let_.

## 🧩 The phrasal verbs of the consolidation

Sixteen of them are asked at once, each with its meaning printed beside it, so the task is not to recall a meaning but to tell close meanings apart.

| Phrasal verb      | Meaning                                    |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **decide on**     | settle on one choice, make a judgement     |
| **gaze at**       | look fixedly                               |
| **turn out**      | end in a particular way                    |
| **pick up**       | lift somebody or something from the ground |
| **look for**      | seek somebody or something                 |
| **dream of**      | have a fantasy of doing something          |
| **provide for**   | supply somebody's needs                    |
| **flow in**       | come in, from many places at once          |
| **set off**       | make somebody suddenly very angry          |
| **catch up**      | do the work you should already have done   |
| **give up**       | stop using, stop doing                     |
| **grow up**       | become mature                              |
| **look after**    | take care of                               |
| **take over**     | assume control of something                |
| **gossip about**  | talk maliciously about somebody            |
| **bring out**     | cause something in somebody to appear      |

Three of them are worth a warning each.

> ⚠️ **catch up** here is not the *catch up with* of Module Two. _I must **catch up**_ = I am behind with my work; _I **catch up with** old friends_ = I meet people I have not seen for a while. Same verb, two lives, and the preposition is what separates them.
>
> ⚠️ **set off** usually means *start a journey* (_they **set off** at dawn_) or *trigger a device or a reaction* (_the smoke **set off** the alarm_). This module works the sense in which what is triggered is somebody's **temper**, and there the object is a person: _one word about the money **set him off**_.
>
> ⚠️ **pick up** and **give up** **split around their object**, and a pronoun has to go in the middle: _help me **pick** this man **up**_ ✓ · _she **gave** coffee **up**_ ✓ · _she **gave** it **up**_ ✓ — "she gave up it" ✗. *Look for*, *look after*, *gaze at*, *dream of* and *provide for* never split at all.

## 🛡️ The modals — and *need* on both its faces

One activity puts six of them in a row and lets you choose.

| Modal           | What it says                                   | Example                                              |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **must**        | obligation, or advice given with force         | _You **must** obey me._                              |
| **should**      | advice, a recommendation                       | _We **should** give help to people in need._         |
| **should have** | the sensible thing that did **not** happen      | _She **should have helped** her little brother._     |
| **shall**       | with *I* and *we*: a future you commit to; in a question, an offer | _If it rains I **shall** stay at home._ · _**Shall** I phone you?_ |
| **may**         | permission, and also possibility                | _People **may** think that I'm rich._                |
| **need**        | necessity — and it has two forms, below         | _She **needs** somebody to look after her._          |

**need, the full verb** (Module Two): it takes an **-s**, it uses *do* for the negative and the question, and it goes before a noun or before **to** + verb: _She **needs** a nurse._ · _You **need to see** a doctor._ · _They **don't need** more blankets._

**needn't, the modal**: it expresses the **absence of necessity**, and it behaves like *must* — no *-s*, no *do*, and the bare verb after it.

```
needn't  +  base verb              → there is no necessity now
needn't  +  have  +  past participle → you did it, and it was not necessary
```

- _You **needn't send** the package by air. You can send it by train._ (nothing obliges you)
- _You **needn't have made** such a big cake. Only a few guests came to the party._ (the cake exists; the trouble was wasted)

> ⚠️ **needn't is not mustn't.** *You mustn't tell her* forbids you; *you needn't tell her* only says that nothing obliges you to. One closes the door, the other leaves it open.

> 🗡️ **needn't have + past participle** always tells you the thing **was** done. *didn't need to* says only that the necessity was absent and leaves open whether anything happened at all: _I **didn't need to** queue_ (there may have been no queue) against _I **needn't have queued**_ (I queued, for nothing).

> ⚠️ The bare verb is the border between the two *needs*. _You **must see** a doctor_ ✓ and _You **need to see** a doctor_ ✓ say almost the same thing, but only one of them takes *to* — so a printed *to* in front of the verb rules *must* out before you have even weighed the meaning. Two more of the same family carry their *to* everywhere: _you **have to** see a doctor_ and _you **ought to** see a doctor_. Of the four, **must** alone takes the naked verb. (*must* also serves to state a confident deduction — _the streets are wet, it **must have rained**_ — but that use belongs to a later module and is not asked here.)

## 📖 A life in a few words

The last reading of the module is a biography with half its words rubbed out, and the words that come back are the ones any life story needs: somebody loses her **sight** and her **hearing**, learns to **communicate**, **graduates** from her college, is remembered for her **accomplishments**, serves the **causes** she believes in, travels for speaking **engagements**, and is finally **awarded** a medal — the **highest** honour that can be conferred on a civilian.

That is the story of **Helen Keller** (1880–1968), who lost both senses before she was two, was taught by Anne Sullivan, graduated from Radcliffe College in 1904, campaigned for women's right to vote, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964.

> 🗡️ Two grammar points hide in that paragraph, and the exam knows it. **is awarded** and **can be conferred** are **passives** — the doer does not matter, only the honour does. And **the highest** is a superlative built on a short adjective, *high → higher → the highest*.

> 🏆 Two modules are behind you, hero, and you have now met them in thirteen different disguises. You can place a word in the only slot that will hold it, build the form a sentence asks for, marry a verb to its noun, punctuate a text that arrived bare, rebuild a paragraph from its pieces, choose between the two pasts **and say why**, rewrite a sentence from an opening somebody else chose, tell sixteen phrasal verbs apart, weigh six modals against one another — and say, in two different ways, that something simply was not necessary.
