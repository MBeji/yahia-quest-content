# ⚔️ Education Is Not Filling a Bucket but Lighting a Fire — Learning, Working, and the Words for Both

> 💡 "A school hands you a certificate. What you can do with it on the day somebody interviews you depends on something else entirely: the words you left with."

This module is about your own life. It asks what school is for, how people learn when there is no classroom, what happens to a pupil the school cannot hold, and what a job is worth beside the status it carries. The traditional Chinese proverb that opens it sets the scale: _if you are planning for a year, sow rice; if you are planning for a decade, plant trees; if you are planning for a lifetime, educate people._ And it gives you four instruments of language: the words that **strengthen a comparison**, the structure that says **how many times**, the way English **reports a question**, and the clause that tells you **which one** you are talking about.

## 🌱 From a slate to a screen — the words of learning today

Your book puts two pictures of school side by side. The old one is a room: a **blackboard**, chalk, a **slate**, books and copybooks, rows of desks, large classes, a hand raised to take part. The new one has no room at all.

| Term                                                   | What it names                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **distance learning** (DL)                             | studying for a qualification without attending a school in person                 |
| **electronic learning** (e-learning)                   | learning through a screen: lessons, exercises and marking delivered online        |
| **CALL** — Computer Assisted Language Learning         | the learner's side: using a computer to learn a language                          |
| **CALT** — Computer Assisted Language Teaching         | the teacher's side: using a computer to teach one                                 |
| **a non-credit course**                                | a course that counts for no qualification — you take it for the skill alone       |

**The qualifications** distance learning offers, in the order your book prints them and rising step by step: a **GED** (General Educational Development) test, an **associate degree**, a **bachelor's degree**, a **graduate certificate**, a **master's degree**, a **doctoral degree** — and, **outside that ladder altogether**, **training courses** that carry no credit at all. A **bachelor** here is somebody who holds a first university degree — not the other sense of the word, an unmarried man.

**Why people choose it.** Your book lists eight advantages and asks you to find five of them yourself: no formal class **attendance** · privacy · convenience · a flexible **schedule** · working at your own pace, going slowly or accelerating · a lower cost · no unnecessary travel · being able to balance a job and a family. A ninth idea sums the whole list up: **learn while you earn**.

And what a screen actually delivers: e-books and up-to-date references, lectures and clips you **listen** to, graphic **illustrations** you see, **assignments** and **quizzes** you send back, and discussion through e-mail and **chats**.

> 🗡️ Read the two sets of pictures the way the module wants them read. The question is never "which is better", it is **what each one gives that the other cannot**: a real teacher answers the question you did not know how to ask; a screen lets you take the lesson at two in the morning.

## ♿ Special education — naming a difficulty, and what mainstreaming means

**Special education** — also called **SPED**, and, in an older vocabulary, **defectology** — is the field that teaches pupils whose needs cannot be met **sufficiently** by ordinary programmes or **techniques**. Those needs may be **physical**, **behavioural** or medical, and the answer is an **alternative** to the usual classroom.

**The nine labels your book asks you to complete:**

| Label                        | What the pupil finds difficult                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **hearing impairment**       | hearing, wholly or partly                               |
| **speech impairment**        | producing speech clearly                                |
| **visual impairment**        | seeing                                                  |
| **physical impairment**      | moving, or using part of the body                       |
| **mental retardation**       | learning at the ordinary pace, across every subject     |
| **learning disabilities**    | one particular operation, such as reading or counting   |
| **emotional disturbances**   | managing feelings and behaviour                         |
| **chronic health problems**  | an illness that never fully goes away                   |
| **long-term health problems**| an illness that lasts a very long time                  |

An **impairment** is the difficulty itself; a **disability** is what that difficulty prevents you from doing. **Chronic** means lasting and recurring, not severe.

> 🗡️ **Mainstreaming** is the word the module wants: the practice of taking children with disabilities into **regular school programmes** instead of separate institutions. Do not confuse it with **special education**, which is the whole field; mainstreaming is one choice made inside it.

> ⚠️ **mental retardation** is your book's phrase, and it was standard when the book was printed. Current English says **intellectual disability**, and *retarded* is now offensive. Answer with the phrase the exercise prints, and use the current one when you write for yourself.

**The case the module makes you read** is a mother's. Her deaf son goes to an ordinary public school with a sign-language interpreter. Within months he is struggling to read lips, the other children have grown **impatient** with him, and his **self-esteem** — the value he puts on himself — has collapsed. She asks to **switch** him to a school for the hearing impaired; the officials resist; she demands an impartial hearing, and the department finds the boy's **plight** — his serious and difficult situation — grave enough to order the school to **transfer** him at once. Around that story stand three more words of the module: to **rely** on something is to depend on it; to **seek** is to look for; to be **willing** is to be ready to do something.

Two more belong to the list and behave differently from all of those: **any** and **no**. They do not measure a step — they ask whether there is one at all, or deny that there is. **any** lives in questions and negatives, **no** in statements: _Is the evening class **any** cheaper?_ · _The new timetable is **no** better than the old one._

> 🗡️ **The one thing this module adds that the previous one did not: many or much?** Before **more** and **less**, the choice follows the noun. A noun you can count takes **many more**; a noun you cannot count takes **much more** and **much less**. _**many more** educational opportunities_ · _**many more** books_ — but _**much more** time_ · _**much less** money_. Nothing about the comparison decides it; the noun does.

> ⚠️ **very much** is allowed, **very** alone is not. _very much bigger_ ✓ is on your book's own list, and _far more attractive_ ✓ beside it — but "very bigger" ✗ and "very more attractive" ✗ are outside the language. When *very* is what comes to mind, write **much** or **far**.

## ⚖️ Comparison of scale — saying how many times

This is the structure the module is built on, and it is new. A comparative tells you that one thing is above another. A **comparison of scale** tells you **by how many times**.

```
multiplier  +  as  +  adjective / adverb          +  as
multiplier  +  as  +  many / much  ( + noun )     +  as
```

- _A campus year costs **four times as much as** the same diploma online._
- _The online diploma costs **a quarter as much as** a campus year._
- _This house is **three times as expensive as** that one._
- _She reads **twice as quickly as** I do._
- _He will be earning only **half as much as** he used to._

**The multipliers.** For two, English normally says **twice**; from three upwards it counts in *times* — **three times**, **four times**, **ten times**. Downwards it uses a fraction: **half**, **a third**, **a quarter**.

**The noun in the middle is optional.** Name it when the sentence needs it — _twice as **much money** as_, _three times as **many pupils** as_ — and leave it out when the verb has already said what is being measured: _in some countries women earn **half as much as** men_.

> 🗡️ **many or much, again — and the same test.** Inside the structure, a countable noun takes **many** (_twice as **many** books as_) and an uncountable one takes **much** (_twice as **much** work as_). The multiplier changes nothing; the noun decides.

> ⚠️ **Three faults cost the mark here, and each of them is a different fault.** The adjective in the middle stays in its **base form**: "three times as bigger as" ✗ → _three times as **big** as_ ✓. The second word is **as**, never *than*: "twice as long than" ✗ → _twice as long **as**_ ✓. And the structure is not built on *more*: "three times more expensive as" ✗ → _three times **as** expensive **as**_ ✓.

> 🗡️ **Reading a scale backwards is half the exercise.** If A is three times as much as B, then B is **a third** as much as A — not "three times less". Write the pair both ways when you revise; the examiner asks for the second one as often as the first.

## 👔 Status and pay — white collars, blue collars, and a dustman's double life

The module now turns from school to what school leads to, and it opens on an awkward fact: manual workers often earn considerably more than the clerks in the offices above them.

- **white-collar workers** — people who work in offices. The name comes from the collar and tie they put on to go to work.
- **blue-collar workers** — people who do manual work: mechanics, carpenters, **dustmen**.
- **status** — the rank other people grant you. It is not the same thing as pay, and this module exists to show you the gap between them.

**The text your book asks you to retell** is L. G. Alexander's, and it is one man's case. Alfred Bloggs is a dustman for a corporation. When he married he was too **embarrassed** to tell his wife what he did, and said only that he worked "for the Corporation". Every morning he left the house in a smart black suit, changed into **overalls**, spent eight hours collecting rubbish, showered and put the suit back on before going home. He kept it up for more than two years, and his workmates kept the secret. His wife never found out, and never will: Alf has just taken another job. He is about to become a **junior clerk** in an office. He will earn only half as much as before, but he considers the **rise in status** worth the money — from now on he wears the suit all day, and he is called Mr Bloggs instead of Alf.

> 🗡️ Notice where the grammar of this module hides. _He will be earning only **half as much as** he used to_ is a comparison of scale, printed inside a story about status. That is how the exam works too: the structure never arrives with a label on it.

> ⚠️ The question the text leaves you is not rhetorical, and it has no printed answer: **does success in professional life depend on education alone?** Answer it with the module's own words — pay, status, qualifications, **self-esteem** — and take whichever side you can argue.

## 💼 Looking for work — the stages, and an interview to listen to

**The stages of a job search**, in the order your book puts them: you read the advertisements or go to an **employment agency** · you fill in an **application form** with your previous experience · if you have the right **qualifications** and training you are invited to an **interview** · you make a better impression if you have **found out** about the organisation first · you ask about the working conditions, the **wages** and the opportunities for **promotion** · and if you are unsuccessful you are notified by post and your **search** begins again.

**The interview the module makes you listen to** is with Mrs Perks, a product manager at a holiday company. She **joined** the firm in 1993 as a product **executive**, a month after her **graduation**, and now manages one region. Her job: she **checks out** destinations, finds hotels, contracts them, comes back and puts the product into the brochure. Her studies: she left school at 16, spent two years at a College of Further Education for a National Diploma in Business Studies with tourism, then went to university at 18 for a four-year course in the same field. Her languages: French and German from a bi-lingual secretarial course, plus Greek and Portuguese. Her benefits: very good wages, travel all over the world, meeting people and seeing places. Her day: there is no typical one — half past seven in the morning until eight at night, and **unpredictable**. Her family: her husband works long hours too and **copes** well with it, and the days off in the middle of the week are quiet ones for shopping. Would she do anything else? Never — she once found herself on a safari on foot, surrounded by animals, thinking *and this is supposed to be work*. **Magic**.

> 🗡️ Listening to an interview is **scanning with your ears**. You are not asked to understand every word; you are asked to catch the four facts the question wants and to let the rest go past. Decide what you are hunting for **before** the recording starts.

## ❓ Indirect questions — reporting a question without inverting it

A direct question is what somebody actually said. An **indirect question** is that question folded into another sentence — a journalist's write-up, a note in a file, a report to the class. It is introduced by **ask**, **inquire**, **wonder** or **want to know**.

**Three things change, and only three.**

1. **The opening.** A question that began with a **WH word** keeps it: _"**When** did you join the company?"_ → _The journalist wanted to know **when** she had joined the company._ A question with **no** WH word — a yes/no question — takes **if** or **whether** instead: _"Do you have any qualifications?"_ → _He asked her **if** she had any qualifications._
2. **The word order.** An indirect question has the word order of a **statement**: subject, then verb. The **do / does / did** that made the direct question is not carried over at all, and *be*, *have* and the modals stop inverting.
3. **The tense**, when the reporting verb is in the past. The same backshift you already know:

| Direct question    | Indirect question after a past verb |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| simple present     | simple past                         |
| present continuous | past continuous                     |
| present perfect    | past perfect                        |
| simple past        | past perfect                        |
| past progressive   | past perfect progressive            |
| **will**           | **would**                           |

The pronouns and the words of time and place move with the speaker, exactly as in reported speech: _my_ → _her_, _here_ → _there_, _today_ → _that day_.

> ⚠️ **The two faults that cost every mark on this exercise.** Keeping the inversion — "He asked where **did she** live" ✗ → _He asked where **she lived**_ ✓. And keeping the question mark — an indirect question is a **statement about** a question, so it ends in a full stop: "He wondered whether she spoke Greek?" ✗.

> 🗡️ **if or whether?** Both work after most reporting verbs. Prefer **whether** when two possibilities are named (_whether she stayed **or left**_) and after a preposition (_the question **of whether** to apply_). Never use either one for a question that already has a WH word: "He asked whether when she had joined" ✗.

## 🔊 Where the stress falls, and the sounds behind the letters

**Stress.** Say *graduation*, *qualifications*, *education*, *destination* aloud, and the same beat lands in all four. In almost every word ending in **-tion** or **-sion**, the main stress falls on the syllable **immediately in front of that ending**: gradu-**A**-tion · qualifi-**CA**-tions · edu-**CA**-tion · desti-**NA**-tion — and, with the words of this module, de-**TEN**-tion · sus-**PEN**-sion · ex-**CLU**-sion. (*Television* is the one common word that escapes it: most speakers say **TEL**-e-vi-sion.)

**Sounds.** The spelling never tells you the sound; the ear does. Four sets are worth carrying out of this module.

| Sound                | Written                          | Words of the module                    |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| /ð/ — voiced *th*    | *th*                             | **the**m · **th**is · toge**th**er     |
| /θ/ — voiceless *th* | *th*                             | sou**th** · **th**ought · mon**th**    |
| /ɔː/                 | *ough*, *our*, *au*              | th**ough**t · c**our**se · t**au**ght  |
| /aɪ/                 | *i*, *i…e*, *y*                  | b**i**-lingual · l**i**fe · m**y**     |

Two spellings hide more than one sound each, and both are in the module's own word lists:

- **th** is voiced /ð/ in the small grammar words (*the*, *this*, *they*, *them*, *there*) and in a handful of common ones (*mother*, *together*, *weather*); it is voiceless /θ/ in most other words (*thought*, *south*, *month*, *three*).
- **ch** is /tʃ/ in *bachelor*, *check*, *French* and *teacher*; /k/ in *chronic*, *school* and *technology*; /ʃ/ in *machine* and *brochure*.

> 🗡️ And one small regularity worth the ten seconds: **-tion** and a **-sion** that follows a consonant are said /ʃn/ — with one exception you meet in this very module: after **s** the ending is /stʃən/, as in *ques**tion*** and *sugges**tion*** — *educa**tion***, *suspen**sion*** — while a **-sion** that follows a vowel is said /ʒn/ — *exclu**sion***, *deci**sion***. Two of those are punishments in the next section, and they are not pronounced the same way.

## 🏫 School discipline — four punishments, and an article that argues about praise

**The vocabulary of an unruly class.** A pupil who will not obey is **disobedient**; one whose behaviour breaks up the lesson for everybody is **disruptive**; a pupil — or a whole class — that cannot be controlled at all is **unruly**, which is why your book's own headline says *unruly **pupils***. To **muck around** is to waste time instead of working; to **quarrel** is to argue angrily; **youngsters** are young people; to **praise** somebody is to say that they have done well, and to **heap** praise is to pile it on. **Prior to** something means before it; a **shift** is a change; **prestigious** means widely respected; **ridiculous** means deserving to be laughed at.

**The four punishments**, ranged from the lightest to the heaviest — a page to copy, then time taken from you, then lessons taken from you, then the school itself taken from you:

| Punishment     | What it costs the pupil                                                            |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **lines**      | he copies out the same sentence a hundred times on a sheet of paper                |
| **detention**  | he is kept back at the end of the day, or called in on Sunday, before he may leave |
| **suspension** | he may attend no lesson until the school has met about his case                    |
| **exclusion**  | he may not come back at all, and must find another school or another way of studying |

**The article the module makes you read** is a newspaper page, and it is **argumentative**: it does not tell a story, it defends a position and prints the objections to it. Its position is that classroom violence is better answered by **praising** disobedient pupils than by telling them off, because gentle compliments turn unruly pupils into orderly ones and disciplining a child in front of the whole class is the worst thing a teacher can do. Four voices then divide:

| Who                                | What they say                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| the researchers                    | you motivate a group by accentuating the positives, never by telling it off            |
| **Nick Seaton**, a campaigner      | the idea is ridiculous: heaping unearned praise sends exactly the wrong message, and youngsters need clear guidance on right and wrong |
| **Chris Keates**, a union secretary| praise does help to motivate — but saying that is all you have to do is far too simplistic, and some pupils stay disruptive however much praise they get |
| **Dr Swinson**, a psychologist     | teachers praise good work readily and good behaviour hardly ever, while spending much of their time telling pupils off for poor behaviour |

The psychologists then built a training programme, **Four Essential Steps to Managing Behaviour**: make every instruction to the class extremely clear, and look for the behaviour you want rather than the behaviour you do not want. The training lasted only two to three hours, and afterwards the average share of pupils doing what the teacher asked reached **94 per cent** — where there was more praise, fewer children were mucking around.

> 🗡️ Three questions this article is always asked, and each has a method. **What genre is it?** Read the shape: a narrative tells events in order, a descriptive text paints, an **argumentative** one defends a thesis and answers objections. **What is the topic sentence?** The sentence of a paragraph that all the others develop — usually the first. **What does *they* refer to?** Walk backwards to the nearest noun that fits both the number and the sense.

## 🔗 Relative clauses — the ones you cannot remove, and the ones you can

A **relative clause** adds information about something already named, and the pronoun that opens it is chosen by what that something is.

| The clause is about | Pronoun                    | Example                                            |
| ------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| a person            | **who**, **whom**, **that**| _the teacher **who** marked my paper_              |
| a thing or an animal| **which**, **that**        | _the skills **which** are needed_                  |
| possession          | **whose**                  | _the pupils **whose** parents came_                |
| a place             | **where**                  | _the school **where** she trained_                 |
| a time              | **when**                   | _September, **when** the timetable starts_         |

**whom** is the object form of *who*, and it is formal; in speech *who* does the job. **what** is a different animal altogether: it means *the thing that* and it has **no antecedent** — _I told him **what** happened_, _**what** matters most is how motivated you are_.

Then comes the distinction the module is really about.

- _Before the training, many teachers were unaware of the skills **which are needed** to deal with unruly classes._ — restrictive: which skills? those ones.
- _The training, **which only lasted between two and three hours**, showed an important shift._ — non-restrictive: there is only one training, and the clause merely tells you how long it took.

**When may the pronoun go?** Ask what job it does inside its own clause. If it is the **subject** — if the next word is the verb — it must stay: _the teacher **who** marked my paper_ ✓, "the teacher marked my paper" would mean something else entirely. If it is the **object** — if a new subject follows it — it may go: _the advice (**that**) my brother gave me_ ✓. And a non-restrictive clause keeps its pronoun whatever the job.

> ⚠️ **A misprint in your grammar summary, and you should know it before it confuses you.** The summary says: "If it is the **subject**, the restrictive pronoun is often left out" — and prints beside it _He did everything he could do to help_, where the word left out is the **object** of *do*. Two lines above, the same page states the rule correctly: keep the pronoun when it is the subject, leave it out when it is the object. **That earlier line is the one to learn**, and the example proves it.

> 🗡️ After a **superlative** and after *all*, *everything*, *nothing*, *anything*, *something*, *none*, *little*, *few*, *much* and *only*, English usually prefers **that** to *which*: _the worst play **that** has ever been performed_ · _the only thing **that** matters_.

## 🗣️ In the head teacher's office — disapproving, warning, apologizing

Section Four puts you in a room with a teacher, a head teacher and a pupil, and then at home with a school report nobody wanted to read. Each speaker needs a different set of phrases.

| The move                    | What you say                                                              |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **express disapproval**     | I don't like … · This is not what we wanted                               |
| **reproach what was done**  | You **shouldn't have** shouted · You **should have** told us · You **could have** telephoned |
| **advise**                  | You should … · Why don't we … ?                                            |
| **warn**                    | Don't … · Be careful                                                       |
| **concede, then object**    | It is true that … **However**, …                                           |
| **apologize**               | I do apologize · I am so sorry that …                                      |
| **express intention**       | I promise to … · I will … · I'm going to …                                 |

**The words of school life** that go with it: an **extra-curricular** activity is one outside the timetable — sport, music, theatre; to be **involved** in something is to take an active part in it; to **sit for** an exam is to take it; and a test gives you clear ideas about your **strengths** and your **weaknesses**.

> 🗡️ **The concession is the move that wins the argument**, and it has two halves in a fixed order: grant the other side something true, **then** object. _It is true that he has missed the lessons. However, nobody told us before today._ Starting with the objection makes an enemy; starting with the concession makes a hearing.

> ⚠️ A poor school report is a problem to be **explained**, not a verdict on a person. Your book's own questionnaire lists the real variables — absences, revision done daily or not, working with classmates, being able to talk to your parents, understanding your mistakes when a test is corrected, and being frightened when you sit for one. Every one of them is something that can be changed, which is exactly why the module asks you to list them before it asks you to react.

## 📖 Writing a report on a book — and the tense each part takes

The writing task of the module is a **report on a book you have read**, for the school magazine, whose purpose is to make other pupils want to read it. Before you write, the module asks what you **make use of** when you study: books, dictionaries, **encyclopaedias**, magazines, videos, cassettes, a computer, the internet — your **resources**.

**The plan, in three parts and in this order:**

1. **Introduction** — what type of book it is, where it is **set**, who the main characters are.
2. **Body** — the plot: the events, in the order in which they happen in the story.
3. **Conclusion** — what you thought of it, why, and whether you would recommend it.

**And the tense of each part.** This is where the simple present and the simple past divide the work between them, and the division is not a matter of taste.

| You are writing about                          | Tense              | Example                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| what the book **is**, and what happens **in it**| **simple present** | _This **is** a thriller. The story **is set** in a small port. The policeman **finds** the letter on page ten._ |
| what **you** did and felt as a reader           | **simple past**    | _I **read** it in three evenings. I **found** the ending a little disappointing._ |
| the recommendation you make now                 | **simple present** | _I **recommend** it. It **is** worth reading._      |

A story told in the present that way is completely normal English: a summary keeps the book alive on the page, so the plot is reported as if it were happening while the reader reads it. The past belongs to **your** experience of it, which really is over.

> ⚠️ Two faults follow from mixing the two. The first is inside one sentence: "The main character **was** a young teacher who **arrives** in the village" ✗ — one half in the past and the other in the present, about the same person. Choose the present for both. The second is the third-person **-s**, which the present demands and which candidates drop under pressure: _the story **takes** place_ ✓, "the story take place" ✗. And in the negative and the question the **-s** and the past ending move onto the auxiliary: _the author **does not explain**_ ✓, _**did** you **finish** it?_ ✓ — never "does not explains" ✗, never "did you finished" ✗.

**The language your book gives you for it**: *This is a love story / a thriller / a **classic*** · *The story is set in …* · *The book is about …* · *It is extremely well-written / original / informative* · *It is really imaginative / fascinating / full of suspense* · *Parts of it are funny / sad / exciting* · *The ending is surprising / unexpected* · *I found the ending a bit disappointing / predictable* · *The characters are very **life-like** / convincing* · *I enjoyed it / I was impressed* · *I recommend it — it is worth reading!*

> 🗡️ Four habits your book calls **useful tips**, and they are worth more than any list of adjectives: check what **sort** of text you were asked for and choose its **layout**; remember **who** will read it and pitch the language to them; plan the events **in order**, and decide the tense of each stage before you start; and, when the first draft is done, improve it by adding detail and linking ideas, then **proofread** it for spelling and punctuation.
