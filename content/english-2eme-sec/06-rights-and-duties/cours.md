## 🕯️ The words of a life without rights

Lesson 15 is an **autobiography**: a life story written by the person who lived it. A **biography** is a life story written by somebody else. One letter of difference in the word, all the difference in who is speaking.

| Word              | Meaning                                                                    |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a slave**       | a person who is owned by another person and forced to work for no pay      |
| **a master**      | the person who owns the slave and gives the orders                          |
| **property**      | what somebody owns; treating a person as property is the heart of slavery   |
| **a trader**      | a person who buys and sells — here, a person who bought and sold human beings |
| **to hire**       | to pay to use something, or to pay somebody to work for you                 |
| **a field hand**  | a worker who does the outdoor work of a farm                                |
| **labour**        | hard physical work, taken as a whole (uncountable)                          |
| **a whipping**    | a beating given as a punishment                                             |
| **a penalty**     | the punishment fixed for breaking a rule                                     |
| **hardship**      | difficult conditions of life, especially a lack of money or of food         |
| **burial**        | the ceremony of putting a dead person in the ground                          |
| **evidence**      | the facts that prove something is true (uncountable: "an evidence" ✗)       |
| **restless**      | unable to keep still or to be at peace                                       |

> ⚠️ Two of these words are **uncountable** and take no _-s_: **labour**, **evidence**. And do not confuse **to hire** (you pay) with **to employ** (you give somebody a job) or with **to own** (it is yours).

## 🔁 The passive voice — the same fact, seen from the other end

Read these two sentences. They report exactly the same event.

> **The trader** separated **the child** from his mother. → this is the **active** voice.
> **The child** was separated from his mother. → this is the **passive** voice.

Nothing about the event has changed. What has changed is **which noun the sentence starts with**. The active sentence starts with the one who **does**; the passive sentence starts with the one who **receives**. English lets you choose, and the choice is not decoration: it decides what the sentence is about.

## 🧱 The form — *be* + past participle

There is one formula, and it never changes.

> **Passive subject + be (in the tense of the active verb) + past participle (+ by + agent)**

Only **be** moves. The past participle is frozen: it is the third column of your verb list — _taken_, _given_, _told_, _written_, _paid_, _beaten_, _made_, _known_ — or the verb + **-ed** for a regular verb.

| Tense                | be becomes… | Example                                                   |
| -------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **present simple**   | am / is / are | _This right **is protected** by the law._                 |
|                      |             | _The workers **are paid** at the end of the month._         |
| **past simple**      | was / were  | _The Declaration **was adopted** in 1948._                  |
|                      |             | _The two brothers **were sent** to different farms._        |

The negative and the question are built on **be**, exactly as they always are — you already know how, because it is the same **be** you have used since your first year.

| Form           | Present                                | Past                                    |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **affirmative**| _The report **is published**._         | _The report **was published**._          |
| **negative**   | _The report **is not published**._     | _The report **was not published**._      |
| **question**   | _**Is** the report **published**?_     | _**Was** the report **published**?_      |

> ⚠️ **Three broken forms, seen every year.** "The law was pass in March" ✗ — the participle has lost its _-ed_ (_was **passed**_). "The men were took to hospital" ✗ — _took_ is a past simple; the passive needs the **participle**, _were **taken**_. "The report published yesterday by the association" ✗ — **be** is missing altogether; without it there is no passive at all, and the sentence, read as it stands, says that the report did the publishing (_was **published**_).

## 🔧 From active to passive — three steps, always the same three

> _The association_ **published** _the report_ **in May**.

1. The **object** of the active sentence becomes the **subject** of the passive one: _the report_ moves to the front.
2. The verb becomes **be + past participle**: _published_ → _be published_.
3. **be** takes the **tense of the active verb**: the active verb was a past simple, so **be** becomes _was_.

> → _**The report was published** in May (by the association)._

The test works in both directions. Give it a passive sentence and it gives you the active one back:

| Active                                            | Passive                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _A committee **writes** the annual report._       | _The annual report **is written** by a committee._           |
| _The court **cancelled** the contract._           | _The contract **was cancelled** by the court._               |
| _Somebody **stole** the files._                   | _The files **were stolen**._                                 |
| _They **do not allow** visitors here._            | _Visitors **are not allowed** here._                         |

> ⚠️ A plural subject brings a plural **be**: _the files **were** stolen_, not "the files was stolen". And the tense of the active verb decides everything: _publishes_ → _is published_, _published_ → _was published_. Nothing else in the sentence may change tense.

## 👤 *by + the agent* — keep it, or drop it

The person or thing that does the action is called the **agent**. In a passive sentence the agent is not obliged to appear; if it does appear, it comes after **by**, at the end.

> _The steam engine **was invented by James Watt**._ = _**James Watt** invented the steam engine._

The two sentences carry the same information; **by + agent** simply gives, at the end, what the active subject gave at the beginning. So you must decide, every time, whether that information is worth the words.

**Keep _by + agent_** when the agent is **news**: the reader does not know it, and it is the interesting part.

> _The new labour law **was written by three young lawyers from Sfax**._

**Drop it** in three cases:

| The agent is…                       | Example                                              | Why you drop it                          |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **unknown**                          | _My bag **was stolen** at the station._               | nobody knows who took it                  |
| **obvious**                          | _The two men **were arrested** the same evening._     | of course by the police — who else?       |
| **unimportant**, or everybody        | _English **is spoken** in all the hotels._            | "by people" adds nothing at all           |

> ⚠️ Dropping an empty agent is a matter of **style**, not of grammar. _My bag **was stolen by somebody**._ breaks no rule at all: it simply spends two words saying what every action already implies, and a careful writer cuts them. Never mark a sentence like that **wrong** — mark it as a sentence that can be **shorter**.

There is a fourth case, and your book uses it constantly: the agent is dropped because the writer wants the sentence to be **about the person who suffered** the action, not about the one who caused it. The autobiography of lesson fifteen writes _"I was not allowed to make any inquiries"_: the sentence puts the child first and leaves the master out of it. That silence is a choice, and it is part of the meaning.

The passive is also the normal voice of two kinds of writing you will meet in exams: **scientific writing** (_The sample **is heated** to 80 degrees._) and **reports of accidents or disasters** where the result matters more than the cause (_Forty houses **were destroyed** by the floods._).

## 🔍 Reading a passive sentence — subject, and doer

When you meet a passive sentence in a text, two different questions have two different answers. Do not mix them.

> _In 1994, a school for girls **was opened** in the village **by a local association**._

- **What is the subject?** Ask which noun the verb **agrees** with, and which noun the action was **done to**: **a school for girls**. Do not simply take what stands in front of the verb: _In 1994_ stands there too, and a **time phrase** is never a subject. Neither is a **place phrase** such as _in the village_ — a group that opens with a preposition (_in_, _at_, _by_, _for_, _of_) can never be the subject of a sentence. The subject of a passive sentence is what **receives** the action, never what performs it.
- **Who does the action?** Look after **by**: **a local association**.

Now remove the last three words:

> _In 1994, a school for girls **was opened** in the village._

The subject has not changed — it is still **a school for girls**. But the second question now has a third possible answer: **the sentence does not say**. That is not a mistake in the sentence; it is the whole point of the passive. When there is no **by**, you may not invent an agent.

> ⚠️ The classic trap: in _The children **were taught** by a volunteer_, the children are the subject but they **teach nobody**. Whoever answers "the children" to "who teaches?" has read an active sentence that is not there.

## 😠 Talking about your life, and saying that something is wrong

An autobiography reports facts; it also lets the writer say that a situation is unacceptable. This is **expressing displeasure**, and it is not the same thing as saying that you did not enjoy your holiday: it judges a situation, it does not rate an experience.

| Function                       | What you say                                                                 |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **telling your own life**      | _I was born in…_ · _I grew up in…_ · _At the age of ten I was sent to…_        |
| **expressing displeasure**     | _It is **unacceptable** that children should work._ · _I **object to** this rule._ |
|                                | _What **shocks** me most is the silence._ · _I **strongly disapprove of** it._  |
| **softening it in writing**    | _I am **deeply concerned about** the situation._ · _This **cannot be justified**._ |

## 🎯 Expressing intention

Lesson 16 asks what people **mean to do** about inequality. English has a small ladder of intention, from the loosest to the firmest:

| Structure                      | Example                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **be going to** + base verb    | _We **are going to** publish the results in June._              |
| **intend to** + base verb      | _The association **intends to** open two more centres._         |
| **plan to** + base verb        | _They **plan to** train fifty women a year._                    |
| **be determined to** + base verb| _She **is determined to** finish her studies._                 |
| **Our aim is to** + base verb  | _**Our aim is to** give every girl a place at school._          |

## 🥄 *a little*, *a few*, *a lot of* — how much, how many, and how it feels

Page 92 of your book prints this rule in full — it is one of the rare places where it does. Here it is, joined to what the grammar summary at the end of your book adds about the **meaning** of each quantifier, and to the form of the noun each one takes.

| Quantifier                                    | Goes in front of…                       | Meaning                       |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **a few** + noun in the **plural**            | a **countable** noun: _minutes, countries, women, laws_ | some — **not many, but enough** |
| **a little** + noun with **no plural**        | an **uncountable** noun: _time, money, work, help, water_ | some — **not much, but enough** |
| **a lot of** + either                          | **countable** _(a lot of mistakes)_ or **uncountable** _(a lot of work)_ | **more than enough**, plenty of |

The four sentences your book prints under the table:

> • We have **a few minutes** to spare; there's no need to hurry.
> • There's no need to hurry; we still have **a little time** left.
> • I've made **a lot of mistakes**. I won't get a good mark.
> • We've got **a lot of work** to do.

**How to choose, in two moves.** First ask: *can I count this noun?* If you can put a number in front of it and add _-s_, it is **countable** → _a few_. If you cannot — you never say "two works", "three informations", "four money" — it is **uncountable** → _a little_. Then ask: *is it a small quantity or a large one?* Small → _a few_ / _a little_. Large → **a lot of**, which never asks the first question at all, because it takes both kinds.

> ⚠️ **The four mistakes that cost marks.** "a few money" ✗ → _a **little** money_ (money cannot be counted). "a little friends" ✗ → _a **few** friends_. "a lot of informations" ✗ → _a lot of **information**_ (uncountable, no _-s_ — and the same goes for _advice_, _work_, _progress_, _evidence_). "a lot people" ✗ → _a lot **of** people_; **of** is part of the expression and never disappears.

> 🗡️ One more shade, worth knowing before an exam: **a few** and **a little** are positive — they say *enough*. Take away the **a** and the meaning turns round: _**few** countries have signed_ means almost none have, and _**little** progress was made_ means almost none was.

## 🧩 Building the words of rights

The same idea gives a verb, a noun and an adjective. Ask what **job** the word must do in the sentence — and, when two nouns exist, which one names a **person** and which one names an **idea**.

| Verb              | Noun (the idea or the action) | Noun (a person)             | Adjective            |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------- |
| to **oppress**    | oppression                     | an oppressor                 | oppressive            |
| to **empower**    | empowerment                    | —                            | —                     |
| to **commit** (oneself) | commitment               | —                            | committed             |
| to **declare**    | a declaration                  | —                            | —                     |
| to **promote**    | a promotion                    | —                            | —                     |
| to **employ**     | employment                     | an **employer** (gives the work) / an **employee** (does it) | employed · unemployed |
| to **obey**       | obedience                      | —                            | obedient              |
| to **equal**      | equality                       | —                            | equal                 |
| to **free**       | freedom                        | —                            | free                  |

> ⚠️ **-er** and **-ee** cut the word family in two: the **employer** hires, the **employee** is hired; the **trainer** teaches, the **trainee** learns; the **interviewer** asks, the **interviewee** answers. And **employment** is neither of them: it is the fact of having work.
