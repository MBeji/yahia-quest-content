## 📚 The words of the library

Lesson 23 argues that a library is not a shelf of paper but the memory of a **civilisation** — the whole way a society organises itself and passes on what it has learnt. Books are called its **lifeblood**: the one thing without which it cannot go on living. To read is to **explore** a subject, and a good book **enlightens** you, that is, it explains to you something you did not understand. Read in **depth** and you stop repeating opinions you have not checked.

The other words of the lesson name what happens around the reading. An **era** is a long period of history with a character of its own. An **expansion** is a growing bigger — of a collection, of a town, of an idea. **Literacy** is the ability to read and write, and the noun for its absence is **illiteracy**. **Crafts** are the skills of the hand — pottery, weaving, carpentry — which the writer puts in the same list as science and mathematics. To **wonder** is either to ask yourself a question you cannot yet answer, or to be filled with admiration. To **pay tribute to** somebody is to thank and praise them publicly; the **to** is part of the expression. And a **shortage** is the situation the whole argument fears: not having enough of something. A shortage of teachers, a shortage of books.

_The town of Zaghouan paid tribute to the woman who had kept its reading room open for thirty years. She never spoke of a shortage of money; she spoke of a shortage of readers._

## 🎧 The words of the market that fell

Lesson 24 is a press article about the sale of records, and its vocabulary is the vocabulary of figures going down. A **decline** is a fall that is slow and steady, spread over years. A **plunge** is sudden and steep. A **drop** is the plain word for either. A **casualty** is whoever suffers because of an event — the small shops that close when a market changes are the first casualties of that change.

On the other side: a **performance** is either what an artist does in front of an audience, or how well something sells; the article uses it in the second sense, and calls one week the worst performance in thirty-five years. A **track** is one recorded piece on a disc. To **release** a record is to put it on sale — you release something, plainly, with no preposition after the verb. **Supremacy** is the position of being above everybody else, which is what the companies in the article are fighting for. A **passion** is a very strong liking. **Doubt** is the state of not being sure. To **blame** somebody **for** something is to say they are responsible for it. And to **shun** somebody or something is to keep away from it on purpose.

> 🗡️ **to blame** trips everybody. You blame **a person for a thing**: _They blamed the companies for the fall._ You blame **a thing on a person**: _They blamed the fall on the companies._ Both exist; "blame on the companies for the fall" mixes the two and exists in neither.

## 📈 Parallel change: the more…, the more…

Here is the rule your book leaves blank. **Parallel change** says that two things move together: as one changes, so does the other. English builds it out of **two comparatives**, and each of them keeps its own **the**.

**The + comparative … , the + comparative …**

- _The larger the hall, the more chairs the club has to carry._
- _The longer she practised, the calmer she felt on stage._
- _The better a town knows its own history, the more carefully it looks after it._

Four things decide whether the sentence is right.

1. **Two comparatives, never two superlatives.** _The more…, the more…_ ✓ · "The most…, the most…" ✗. A superlative ranks one item above all the others; parallel change compares nothing to anything — it links two movements.
2. **Both halves keep _the_.** _The harder you work, the faster you finish._ ✓ · "Harder you work, faster you finish" ✗. Without the two _the_, the two halves fall apart.
3. **Each comparative is built normally.** A short adjective takes **-er** (_the higher_, _the later_, _the cheaper_), a long one takes **more** (_the more expensive_). "The more high" ✗ marks the comparison twice.
4. **The second half may fall while the first rises.** The pattern says _together_, not _the same way_. _The higher the price, the **fewer** copies a shop sells._ · _The more he explained, the **less** anybody understood._ — and note which word goes where: **fewer** before what you can count (copies, tracks, readers), **less** before what you cannot (money, time, noise).

### The short form

When the two halves would only contain **be** and a subject, English drops both and leaves the two comparatives alone.

- _The more, the merrier._ (the more people come, the merrier the evening is)
- _The sooner, the better._
- _The less said about it, the better._

> ⚠️ Do not confuse parallel change with **gradual increase** (_more and more expensive_, _less and less useful_), which you will meet in Theme X. Parallel change has **two** things moving and needs **two** halves; gradual increase has **one** thing that keeps going in the same direction.

## ⚖️ some, any, no, none: the question is polarity

**some** and **any** both mean _a certain quantity or number_, they both stand in front of a plural or an uncountable noun, and they can both be pronouns as well as adjectives. They are not two words for two quantities. They are two words for two **polarities**.

| Use **some** | Use **any** |
| --- | --- |
| in an **affirmative** statement: _I can lend you some maps._ | after a **negative** verb: _He hasn't any change._ |
| in a question that is really an **offer** or an **invitation**: _Will you have some tea?_ | after the near-negatives **hardly**, **barely**, **scarcely**: _There is hardly any petrol left._ |
| in a question that is really a **request**: _Will you carry some of these boxes for me?_ | in a **genuine question**, one that really asks: _Have you got any photos of the trip?_ |
| in a question where the answer **yes** is expected: _I heard a knock; is there someone at the door?_ | after **if**: _If you find any old letters, keep them._ |
| | after an expression of **doubt**: _I doubt whether there is any bread left._ |

There is one more use of **any**, and it is the one that surprises everybody, because it sits in an **affirmative** sentence: **any** can mean _practically every_ — it does not matter which one.

_**Any** pupil will show you the way._ (choose whichever pupil you like; the sentence still holds)

Finally, **no** and **none** say the same thing with an affirmative verb.

- **no** is only an **adjective**: it stands in front of a noun. _I have **no** apples._
- **none** is only a **pronoun**: it stands alone. _He has **none**._
- Each of them replaces a whole _negative verb + any_: _I haven't any apples_ = _I have no apples_ · _He hasn't any_ = _He has none_.

## 🚫 The one negative, and only one

That last line contains the law of this whole lesson. **English marks a negative once.** Once the verb is negative, every word after it takes the **any-** form; if you want the negative inside the word instead, the verb goes back to being affirmative.

_I didn't see **anybody**._ ✓ — the negative is in the verb
_I saw **nobody**._ ✓ — the negative is in the word
"I didn't see nobody." ✗ — the negative is in both, and two negatives do not make a stronger no

The same choice comes back every time: _We haven't got **anything**_ / _We have **nothing**_ · _She couldn't find it **anywhere**_ / _She could find it **nowhere**_. Both columns are correct English. Writing one word from each column into the same clause is not.

> 🗡️ Your own language allows the double negative and will push you towards it every time. Check every sentence you write with **not**, **never**, **without** or **hardly** in it: the word after them must begin with **any-**.

## 🧩 The compounds

The compounds of _some_, _any_ and _no_ follow exactly the rule of the word they are built on. Three series, four columns.

| | person | person | thing | place |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| affirmative | **someone** | **somebody** | **something** | **somewhere** |
| negative / question | **anyone** | **anybody** | **anything** | **anywhere** |
| the negative itself | **no one** | **nobody** | **nothing** | **nowhere** |

_Somebody left a bag by the door._ · _Did anybody see who it was?_ · _Nobody had noticed._

Two notes. A compound of the third row is **singular**: _Nothing **has** changed._ · _Nobody **was** waiting._ And a compound of the third row makes the clause negative all by itself, so its verb stays affirmative: _Nobody came_ ✓, "Nobody didn't come" ✗.

> ⚠️ Your Student's Book prints **no one** in two words and never prints **nobody** at all on these pages, although the official guide announces it. Both are correct English and both are asked for. The appendix table gives them side by side, which is why they are side by side here.

## 📰 Reading a short cultural press article

Lesson 24 is journalism, and journalism argues with figures. Three habits get you through it.

- **Separate the claim from the evidence.** An article says something is happening (the claim) and then gives a detail meant to prove it (the evidence). Ask of every claim: which sentence here actually supports it? A figure about ticket sales does not support a statement about posters.
- **A comparison needs both of its terms.** "Sales fell by half" is only readable if you know _from what_ and _over what time_. Four hundred thousand copies means nothing until the article adds _in one week_ and _the worst in thirty-five years_.
- **Conclude only what the text says.** If the piece does not name the cause, the honest answer is that the piece does not name it — not the cause you find likeliest.

## 💬 Saying what you think, and saying what you feel

Lesson 23 asks you to **express an opinion** and to **sensitize** people — to make them aware that something matters. _In my opinion… · I believe that… · What worries me is… · We ought to remember that…_ An opinion that sensitizes always ends in something to do: _if we do nothing, the reading room will close._

Lesson 24 asks for something else: **feelings** and **likings**. _I am fond of… · I can't stand… · What I like best about it is… · It leaves me cold._ A liking becomes interesting the moment you say **why**, and the why is a fact about the thing, not about you.

## 🎯 Career research project — step 2: the interview

Step 1 was your own written interview. Step 2 sends you to somebody who already does the job. Your book prints four instructions and nothing else, so here they are with what each one costs you if you skip it.

1. **Find out about the field and about the person before you meet them.** Questions whose answers are on the first page of any website waste the only half-hour you will get.
2. **Write your list of questions in advance.** Open questions — _what does a normal day look like?_ — not questions answered by yes or no.
3. **Be ready to take notes.** You will not remember a figure an hour later.
4. **Show interest, and thank them.** Step 3 of the project is a formal letter, and this is where you learn the tone it needs.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The most you read, the most you learn." | _The more you read, the more you learn._ | parallel change takes two comparatives, never superlatives |
| "More you practise, better you play." | _The more you practise, the better you play._ | each half keeps its own _the_ |
| "The more cheap the ticket, the more people come." | _The cheaper the ticket, the more people come._ | a short adjective builds its comparative with _-er_ |
| "The higher the price, the less copies they sell." | _The higher the price, the fewer copies they sell._ | _fewer_ before a countable plural, _less_ before an uncountable |
| "He didn't buy some tickets." | _He didn't buy any tickets._ | after a negative verb, _some_ becomes _any_ |
| "Would you like any coffee?" | _Would you like some coffee?_ | a question that is an offer takes _some_ |
| "There is hardly some room left." | _There is hardly any room left._ | _hardly_ is almost a negative, so _any_ follows |
| "I don't know nothing about it." | _I don't know anything about it._ / _I know nothing about it._ | one negative per clause, in the verb or in the word |
| "Nobody didn't answer the phone." | _Nobody answered the phone._ | _nobody_ is already the negative; the verb stays affirmative |
| "We couldn't find it nowhere." | _We couldn't find it anywhere._ | after a negative verb, the place word takes _any-_ |
| "I have none money." | _I have no money._ / _I have none._ | _no_ goes before a noun, _none_ stands alone |
| "They blamed on the radios for the fall." | _They blamed the radios for the fall._ | _blame somebody for something_, or _blame something on somebody_ |
