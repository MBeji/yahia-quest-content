## 📖 The argument for libraries

Lesson 23 is an **argumentative** text: it defends a position, and you can tell from the opening of each paragraph. The author, **Lois E. Hole**, argues in five steps:

1. **Librarians are the heroes of civilization** — they have quietly kept one of its great traditions, the gathering of human life experience. Libraries are the **engines** of progress.
2. **Without them, that progress would be impossible** — we can **explore** the thoughts and actions of others, and that gives a remarkable power to change and grow.
3. **Books go deeper than screens** — good films and programmes have their place, but understanding a question *in depth* means turning to books. And because books are expensive, libraries keep reading from becoming a hobby for the rich.
4. **Books are the lifeblood of the library** — they **enlighten** the world and encourage the **expansion** of culture. The future of a country depends on a **literate** population.
5. **The gift she would give** — a good education, rich in literature, science, mathematics, history, **crafts**, sport and the fine arts, with no **shortage** of excellent teachers. And a closing appeal: **pay tribute** to teachers, take care of libraries.

### How she argues

Your book asks what means the writer uses to support her argument, and it says "tick the correct answer**(s)**" — plural, because there is more than one.

- **Reasoning** — she moves from cause to consequence: books are expensive, *therefore* libraries matter; a country's future needs literate people, *therefore* schools and libraries need support.
- **Quotation** — she closes on a saying, printed between quotation marks.
- **Not statistics** — there is not a single figure in the whole text.
- **Not imagination** — nothing is invented; every step is an argument about the real world.

## ⚖️ Parallel change — two things moving together

> *The **more** you read, the **more** you know.*

Two comparatives, tied to each other: as one rises, so does the other. Your book calls this structure **parallel change**, and the name matters, because the movement is not always upwards:

> *The **more** I forget, the **less** I know.*

**The formula**:

> **The + comparative + subject + verb , the + comparative + subject + verb**

| Adjective / adverb | Comparative to use |
| --- | --- |
| short word (*fast*, *old*, *cheap*) | *the **faster** …, the **older** …* |
| long word (*expensive*, *interesting*) | *the **more** expensive …, the **more** interesting …* |
| quantity, countable (*books*, *ideas*) | *the **more** books …, the **fewer** books …* |
| quantity, uncountable (*money*, *time*) | *the **more** money …, the **less** money …* |
| irregular | *good* → *the **better**…* · *bad* → *the **worse**…* |

> ⭐ **The two halves are not two sentences.** They are joined by a comma and neither can stand alone: *"The more you read"* is not English by itself. Both halves need **the**, and both need a **verb**.

> ⚠️ **Do not call this structure "parallel increase".** Your teacher's guide uses that name, but your own book prints **parallel change** on page 127 — and the book is right, because the last line of its own exercise goes **down**: *the more I forget, the **less** I know*. An "increase" that decreases is not a useful name.

> ⚠️ In the next chapter you will meet `more and more` and `less and less`, which look similar and do something else: **`the more… the more…`** links **two** changes to each other; **`more and more`** describes **one** thing changing steadily. Keep them apart from the start.

## 💿 Death of the single — an analysis dated 2002

Lesson 24 explains why the pop **single** — a record with one song on each side, as your book's own glossary says — stopped selling. Everything in it describes **2002**, and it should be read as a document of that year, not as a description of today.

- **§1 — what the single was**: the passion of teenagers, the first thing they spent their pocket money on. Programmes like *Top of the Pops* lived on it. It was killed, the article says, by **the internet, MTV and mobile phones**.
- **§2 — the figures in the UK**: in the third week of January 2002, **400,000** singles were sold — the worst week in **35 years**, and half the figure of the same week four years earlier. Only **10** singles passed 10,000 copies, against three times as many five years before.
- **§3 — the figures in the USA**: sales fell by nearly **9 %**. The one exception was **Eminem**, the only artist of 2002 to pass five million. Industry-wide, total sales fell **8.7 %** — the second yearly **decline** in a row.
- **§4 — the radio presenter's explanation**: Dr Fox says that when he was a child there was nowhere else to hear a new song, so you had to buy it. Now there are ten music channels and three hundred radio stations. And he **blames** the record companies themselves: almost every single released appears on a compilation album the same week. Why spend three pounds on one song when forty tracks cost a little more? No wonder people are **shunning** singles.
- **§5 — the last cause**: the pop world has become fiercely competitive, and the fall in singles is the first **casualty** of a battle for **supremacy**. You can now download a track, buy it as a ringtone, or record it from television. And radio stations play songs up to **two months** before release, so the buyer is tired of them before they reach the shops.

### The words of the article

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **a casualty** | a victim — somebody or something harmed in a conflict or disaster |
| **a passion** | a very strong love or enthusiasm |
| **to blame** | to say that somebody is responsible for something bad |
| **to shun** | to reject, to avoid deliberately |
| **a decline** | a fall, a drop |
| **to plunge** | to fall suddenly and steeply |

> ⭐ Three of these carry the whole argument: sales **plunge**, the single becomes a **casualty**, and the companies are to **blame**. If you can use those three, you can summarise the article in one sentence.

## 🔀 `some` and `any` — and the honest truth about the rule

Your book's rule is the usual one, and it is a good place to start:

| | Used in | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **some** | affirmative statements | *They found **some** letters in the mail box.* |
| | offers and requests | *Would you like **some** coffee?* / *Will you carry **some** of these bottles?* |
| | questions expecting **yes** | *Did you see **some** tall men with long beards?* |
| **any** | negative sentences | *The children don't want **any** rice.* |
| | ordinary questions | *Is she taking **any** instruction in music this term?* |
| | after **if** | *If you want to get **any** coffee, buy it now.* |
| | after restrictive adverbs (*hardly*, *scarcely*, *barely*) | *There was hardly **any** sunshine yesterday.* |
| | after expressions of **doubt** | *I doubt that there is **any** coffee in the kettle.* |

The same rules apply to the compounds: **someone / somebody / something / somewhere** against **anyone / anybody / anything / anywhere**.

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence your book prints twice, and why.** *Would you like to have **some** coffee?* appears in its list of `some`; *Would you like to have **any** coffee?* appears in its list of `any`. This is not a misprint. **Both are correct, and they mean slightly different things**: *some* makes it an **offer** — the speaker expects you to accept; *any* makes it a **neutral question** — the speaker has no idea what you will say.
>
> That is why the table of the exercise cannot be filled without contradicting itself, and why you should learn the **meanings** rather than the columns. The columns say *any* goes in interrogatives — but the very next sentence in your book is *Did you see **some** tall men…?*, an interrogative with *some*, used because the speaker already believes the men were there.

> ⚠️ Two traps of form, and both appear in the exercise:
> **"I haven't got no money"** ✗ — after a negative verb English uses **any**: *I haven't got **any** money.*
> **"There's hardly no paper"** ✗ — *hardly* is already negative, so it too takes **any**: *There's hardly **any** paper left.*

## 🪶 The limerick, and the two ways to be funny in five lines

Your book prints the rule of a **limerick**: a funny little poem of **five lines**, where the last words of lines **1, 2 and 5** rhyme with each other, and the last words of lines **3 and 4** rhyme with each other.

> **A A B B A**

Lines 1, 2 and 5 are usually longer; lines 3 and 4 are short and quick. The first line very often names a person and a place — *There was an old man from…* — and the last line delivers the joke.

> ⭐ You are asked to **write** one, not to learn one. Pick a place whose name rhymes easily, invent a person there, give them one absurd problem in lines 3 and 4, and end on the surprise.
