# 🖥️ Science and Technology — Reporting What Was Said, and What We Are Becoming

> 💡 "In the future, then, both men and women are likely to be bald." — Dr Franklin Gruber, in the recording of Lesson 26.

Theme X is the only theme of the book with **no Arts lesson**: the two lessons run straight on from one another.

- **Lesson 25 — Internet addiction**: four readers of a website disagree about how much time online is too much. The grammar is the one you need whenever you repeat what somebody else said: **reported speech**.
- **Lesson 26 — What will man be like?**: a specialist in "futuristic studies" describes the human being of fifty thousand years from now. The grammar is the one that describes something changing steadily: **`more and more`**, **`less and less`**, and their short cousin **`-er and -er`**.

## 🌐 The words of a life online

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **online addiction** | the condition of being unable to stop using the internet |
| **to surf the net** | to move from page to page on the internet |
| **cyberspace** | the imagined space in which online communication happens |
| **to chat** | to talk informally — online, to exchange written messages in real time |
| **a chat room** | an online space where several people write to each other at once |
| **the escape key** | the key marked *Esc*, used to interrupt what a program is doing |
| **to survive** | to stay alive, or to get through something difficult |
| **depression** | a lasting state of deep sadness and loss of energy |
| **irrational** | not based on reason |
| **to break up** | to end a relationship |
| **to conceal** | to hide |
| **to acknowledge** | to admit that something is true |

> ⚠️ **to chat** has moved house. In older English it means to talk lightly, face to face; online it means to exchange typed messages in real time. Both meanings are alive, and this lesson uses the second.

## 💬 Four readers, four positions — and one that will not be sorted

The lesson prints four short messages sent to a website. Your book asks you to sort them into *for* and *against* excessive internet use.

| Reader | Position | What they say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ian**, England | **against** | He can live without it; his **wife** cannot. Five hours or more from the moment she gets home, three chat rooms at once, the mobile ringing all the while. The marriage is suffering, and so is her relationship with her son. His question is how to make somebody **acknowledge** a problem when raising it makes them aggressive and defensive. |
| **Sam**, UK | **for** | Seven hours in front of a computer is no worse than seven hours in front of a television — and better, since the television offers **no interactivity** at all. |
| **Janet**, UK | **for** | Hours on the telephone make her look sociable and caring; the same hours spent writing or chatting online make her a geek "out of touch with real life". She calls those **irrational prejudices** and asks that communication be valued in all its forms. |
| **Manel Ali**, Egypt | ⚠️ **not decidable** | She met her fiancé online. Soon after they met in person, her excessive love of the internet cost her the engagement: he was jealous, jealousy became mistrust, he asked her to stop chatting with other men — and she could not, because she needs to feel closer to the rest of the world. They broke up. |

> ⭐ **Manel is the honest difficulty of the exercise, and it is worth naming.** Her message **tells the story of a harm** — she lost her engagement — and **defends the need** that caused it. Read the story, she is a warning; read her last sentence, she is a defence. A question that forces her into one column is a question that has not read her.

> ⚠️ Lesson 25 also prints a **self-test** of seven questions, ending with "if you answered yes to at least five, you are an addict". Do not treat that as a diagnosis of anybody, including yourself. It is a **classroom prompt for discussion**, not a medical instrument, and a label of that kind should never come from a schoolbook. What the lesson can teach you is the **vocabulary** of the debate and how to **argue** in it.

## 🗣️ Reported speech — repeating what somebody said

When you report words instead of quoting them, three things move.

> **Direct:** Manel says, "**I am** one of those who **met** my fiancé online."
> **Reported:** Manel said that **she was** one of those who **had met** her fiancé online.

### 1. The person moves

| Direct | Reported |
| --- | --- |
| I, me, my, mine | he/she, him/her, his/her |
| we, us, our | they, them, their |
| you (the listener) | I, me, my — *or* he/she, depending on who is reported to whom |

### 2. The tense steps back

When the reporting verb is in the past (*said*, *told*, *explained*), the reported verb moves one step further into the past:

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
| --- | --- |
| present simple — *I **am** tired* | past simple — *she **was** tired* |
| present progressive — *I **am working*** | past progressive — *she **was working*** |
| past simple — *I **met** him* | past perfect — *she **had met** him* |
| present perfect — *I **have finished*** | past perfect — *she **had finished*** |
| will — *I **will** come* | would — *she **would** come* |
| can — *I **can** swim* | could — *she **could** swim* |
| must — *I **must** go* | had to — *she **had to** go* |

> ⭐ **Two tenses never move, because they cannot go further back**: the **past perfect** stays as it is, and **would**, **could**, **should**, **might** stay as they are. Once you are as far back as the language goes, there is nowhere left to step.

### 3. The words of time and place move

| Direct | Reported |
| --- | --- |
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day |
| yesterday | the day before |
| here | there |
| this | that |

> ⚠️ **`say` and `tell` are not interchangeable.** You **say** something, and you **tell** *somebody* something: *She **said** that she was tired.* / *She **told me** that she was tired.* "She said me" ✗ and "She told that…" ✗ are the two mistakes this rule exists to prevent.

> ⚠️ **When the backshift does not happen.** If what was said is still true — a permanent fact, a rule, a habit that has not changed — English often keeps the present: *He said that water **boils** at a hundred degrees.* The backshift reports a moment; keeping the present reports a truth.

> ⚠️ **A warning about your book's own sentence.** The model it gives is *I am one of those who met her fiancé through cyberspace*. The relative clause depends on *those*, which is plural, so correct English would be *one of those who met **their** fiancés*. Use this sentence to practise the **tense change** — that part is exactly right — and not as a model of how a relative clause agrees.

## 🔭 What will man be like?

Lesson 26 is a listening. **Dr Franklin Gruber**, head of the department of futuristic studies at Durban University, is asked what the human being will look like in five thousand — or fifty thousand — years. His answer rests on one idea: **man is slowly changing all the time.**

| What changes | Why, according to the recording |
| --- | --- |
| **height** — we grow taller | five hundred years ago people were shorter; men are now about **three inches** taller, and five hundred years is a **relatively short** period |
| **the head** — the forehead grows larger | we use only about **20 %** of the brain's capacity, and we shall use it **more and more** often, so the brain becomes **more and more substantial** |
| **the eyes** — they grow stronger | they are in constant use; over a very long period they will improve, even though today that same use weakens them and forces us into glasses |
| **arms and legs** — they weaken | we use them **less and less**, so they become **less and less powerful** |
| **the fingers** — they grow more sensitive | modern life uses them a great deal |
| **hair** — it disappears | it no longer serves a useful purpose; both men and women are likely to be **bald** |
| **what does not change** | future man will still be a human being, with **thoughts and emotions** similar to our own |

> ⭐ **The last line is the point of the whole passage.** After a list of losses — hair, strong limbs, an attractive face — the speaker turns round and says what stays. A listening comprehension always asks about the exception.

### The words of the future

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **futuristic** | concerned with the future |
| **obvious** | easy to see or understand; needing no proof |
| **to assume** | to accept something as true without proof, in order to reason from it |
| **substantial** | large, solid, considerable |
| **bald** | having no hair on the head |
| **emotions** | feelings such as joy, fear or sadness |
| **capacity** | the amount something can hold or do |

## 📈 Gradual change — one thing moving steadily

> *They will become **more and more** substantial.*
> *They are likely to grow **less and less** powerful.*

This is the structure for **one thing changing steadily in one direction**. Your book prints the rule in a `GRAMMAR SUMMARY` box on page 144 — but the box is incomplete, so here it is in full.

| Adjective | Structure | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **long** (two syllables or more) | **more and more** + adjective | gradual **increase** | *The exam is getting **more and more** difficult.* |
| **long** | **less and less** + adjective | gradual **decrease** | *The streets are becoming **less and less** safe.* |
| **short** (one syllable) | adjective + **-er and -er** | gradual **increase** | *The days are getting **longer and longer**.* |
| irregular | **better and better**, **worse and worse** | | *Her English is getting **better and better**.* |

> ⚠️ **The box on page 144 stops halfway.** It prints the line "Adjective + er + and + Adjective + er" and leaves the meaning column empty, with no example anywhere. The meaning is **gradual increase**, exactly like *more and more* — the only difference is the length of the adjective. *taller and taller*, *bigger and bigger*, *colder and colder*.

> ⚠️ You cannot use both at once. **"more and more taller"** ✗ — the *-er* already does the work, so a short adjective takes the ending and nothing else.

### The distinction that decides half the marks

| Structure | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **more and more** + adjective | **one** thing changing steadily | *The library is getting **more and more** crowded.* |
| **the more … the more …** | **two** changes tied to each other | *The **closer** the exams get, the **more** crowded the library becomes.* |

> ⭐ Your book puts this distinction into an exercise itself: it asks whether *more and more substantial* expresses "a gradual increase/decrease", "a parallel change" or "a comparison of superiority" — and offers **parallel change**, the name it gave the *the more… the more…* structure in Lesson 23, as the trap. Count the changes: **one** → *more and more*; **two** → *the more… the more…*.

## 🔊 Where the stress falls

Lesson 26 also asks you to sort words by their **stressed syllable**. The stress is not decoration: put it in the wrong place and an English listener may not recognise the word at all.

| Stress on the… | Words |
| --- | --- |
| **1st** syllable | **IN**ternet · **HEALTH**ier · **MED**icine · **NEWS**paper |
| **2nd** syllable | ad**DIC**tion · re**SEARCH** · re**PEAT**edly · sup**PORT**ed · scien**TIF**ic |
| **3rd** syllable | edu**CA**tion · technolo**GIC**ally · mathe**MAT**ics |
| **4th** syllable | environ**MEN**tal · communi**CA**tions · availa**BIL**ity · impossi**BIL**ity |

> ⭐ **Two patterns worth keeping.** Words ending in **-tion**, **-sion**, **-ic** and **-ity** put the stress on the syllable **immediately before** the ending: educa-**TION** → edu**CA**tion, scien-**TIF**-ic, availa-**BIL**-ity. And adding a suffix often **moves** the stress: **TECH**nology → techno**LOG**ical → technolo**GIC**ally.
>
> ⚠️ *research* is stressed on the second syllable in British English and often on the first in American English. Say it either way; do not build an examination question on it.

## 🎯 What this chapter is testing

- **reported speech**: the three shifts (person, tense, time and place), the tenses that do not move, and the difference between **say** and **tell**;
- **`more and more`**, **`less and less`** and **`-er and -er`** for a single steady change — with the meaning the printed box leaves blank;
- the distinction between **one** change (*more and more*) and **two** linked changes (*the more… the more…*);
- the vocabulary of **online life** and of **the future body**;
- listening for the **exception** in an argument — what does *not* change;
- **word stress**, and the endings that fix it.

> ⚠️ Two things in this chapter should not be learnt from the book. Its list of active vocabulary contains the word **microslave**, which appears nowhere in the lesson — ignore it. And its self-test is a discussion prompt, not a diagnosis: no set of seven questions decides whether a person is an addict.
