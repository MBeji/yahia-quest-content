# ⚔️ Communicating with Others — Friends, Messages and the Words that Link Them

> 💡 "A message costs nothing to send. What it costs is the silence it breaks."

Theme I asked where you come from. Theme II asks **who you talk to, and how**: the friend you find again (lesson 3), the friend you comfort (lesson 4), and the thousand small messages you send every day (lesson 5). Two weapons run through all three: the **future**, and the **linkers of cause and result**. Both come back in every exam paper.

## 🤝 The words of friendship

A friendship begins, it fades, sometimes it comes back to life. **To keep in touch** is to go on writing, phoning or seeing each other; **to lose touch** is to stop being in contact, usually without deciding to; **to renew** is to start something again after a long break. An **intimate** friend is a very close one, the one you tell everything to — not the same thing as an **honest** friend, who tells the truth even when it costs. Add a **website** (a set of pages on the internet), a **password** (the secret word that keeps your account yours), a **waitress** (a woman who serves food and drink in a cafe; a man is a **waiter**) and **incredible** (so surprising that it is hard to believe).

_Anis and Zied left primary school together and **lost touch** for eleven years. Last winter Zied found his name on a school **website**, typed his **password** and wrote to him. The friendship they **renewed** is stronger than the first one — **incredible**, but true._

To judge a friend, English gives you three warm adjectives and three cold ones. Warm: **faithful / loyal** (stays with you and never betrays you), **sincere** (really means what they say), **open-minded** (ready to accept ideas and people that are not like them). Cold: **negligent** (careless, does not look after what matters), **indifferent** (does not care at all what happens to you), **narrow-minded** (refuses every idea that is not already their own).

> 🗡️ Learn them in pairs, it costs half the effort: **open-minded** ↔ **narrow-minded**.

## ⏪ Telling how it started — the past simple and its markers

> Rim and Faten **met** in a summer camp six years **ago**. Nobody **had** a smartphone **at that time**. They **exchanged** two letters, and **then** they **lost** touch.

- **ago** comes **after** the length of time and counts backwards from today: _two months **ago**_ ✓, "ago two months" ✗. It dates a finished moment, so it lives with the **past simple** only: "I have met her two years ago" ✗.
- **at that time** points back to the moment the sentence has **just named**, and it is the opposite of _now_: _Petrol was cheap **at that time**._ For the present you would write _today_ or _nowadays_; for a whole stretch of time running up to now, _since then_ with a present perfect.
- **then** means either _at that moment_ or _after that_: _We were fifteen **then**._ · _**Then** she phoned me._

> 🗡️ **at that time** and **then** never stand alone: they point back to a moment already named. If your reader cannot see which moment, the sentence is broken.

## ⚖️ Speaking about the future

This is the heart of the chapter. Last year you were told not to lose sleep over the difference between _will_ and _be going to_; from now on the examiner counts it. English chooses its form according to **when the decision was taken**.

| The situation                                                     | The form        | Example                                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| the decision was taken **before** you spoke — a plan, an intention | **be going to** | _I**'m going to** repaint my room this summer._   |
| the decision is born **while** you speak — a promise, an offer     | **will**        | _That bag looks heavy. **I'll** take it._         |

**be going to** = **am / is / are + going to + base verb** (_I **am not going to** write._ · _**Are** we **going to** write?_). Besides the plan already made, it predicts what you can already see coming: _Look at that sky — it **is going to** rain._ For a prediction the two forms differ: _It **will** rain tomorrow_ is an opinion, _it **is going to** rain_ is what you say when the first drops are on your hand.

**will** = **will + base verb**, the same for every subject; short form **'ll**, negative **won't**, question _Will you…?_ It does four jobs: a **decision** taken as you speak (_It's heavy — **I'll** carry it._), a **promise** (_Don't worry, **I'll** be there at seven._), an **offer** (_**I'll** lend you my notes._) and a **prediction** (_I think Sabri **will** pass._).

Two other forms also look ahead, and you already know them: the **present continuous** of an arrangement fixed with somebody, with a time and a place (_I**'m meeting** Dorra at six._), and the **present simple** of a timetable fixed by an organisation (_The train **leaves** at 7.15._). Neither of them ever announces a decision — and a present simple with no timetable behind it describes a **habit**, nothing more (_I read my messages every evening._).

> ⚠️ Three mistakes cost marks every year. Dropping _be_: "I going to call him" ✗, "I go to call him" ✗ — this last one translates your own language word for word and lands on a habit; English says _I **am** going to call him_ ✓. Adding _to_ after _will_: "I will to help you" ✗, _I **will help** you_ ✓. And marking the future twice: "I'll going to call him" ✗ — choose one form.

## 💙 Attitudes and feelings — the language of comfort

Lesson 4 is built on the song **"Bridge Over Troubled Water"** by Simon and Garfunkel. You will not be asked to sing it. You will be asked to speak like it: **to say how somebody feels, and to say what you will do about it.**

**to be weary** = worn out, extremely tired, in body or in spirit · **hopeless** = seeing no way out of a situation · **depressed** = deeply and lastingly sad · a **tear** = a drop of water that runs from the eye when you cry · **to feel small** = to feel ashamed and unimportant, as if you counted for nothing · **to comfort somebody** = to make somebody feel less sad or less afraid · **to take somebody's part** = to side with somebody, to defend them against the others · **to ease somebody's mind** = to calm an anxious mind · a **bridge** = what lets you cross over · **to sail** = to travel on water · **to shine** = to give out light, and of a person, to be at your best.

_Ghassen was **weary** and close to **tears** after his interview. When the others laughed at him, Hamza **took his part**; then he **eased his mind** with one sentence: "**I'll** phone the company myself tomorrow." Nobody should be left to **feel small**._

When you comfort somebody, English often reaches for **will**: you promise on the spot, and the promise is the comfort. Not every _will_ said to a friend in trouble is a promise, though — what you cannot control yourself, you can only predict (_You**'ll** pass it next time._).

## 🔁 Cause and result — naming the link

Lesson 5 is the only place in this theme where the book names its own grammar point: the **Cause / Result relation**. Start from two facts.

> The post office was closed. Sabri sent the documents by e-mail.

One link, and English lets you enter it from either end.

**Naming the cause.** **because** + a **clause** (subject + verb): _Sabri sent the documents by e-mail **because the post office was closed**._ · **since** and **as** + a clause, when the reason is already known to both of you, _as_ being a little more formal: _**Since** the post office was closed, he sent them by e-mail._ · **because of** + a **noun** or a noun phrase: _He sent them by e-mail **because of the strike**._ Never mix the two: "because of it was raining" ✗ · _because it was raining_ ✓ · _because of the rain_ ✓.

> ⚠️ **since** has a second job you already know, the one of time (_since 2016_). Only the words around it tell you which one is at work.

**Naming the result.** **so** joins two clauses: _The post office was closed**, so** he sent them by e-mail._ · **therefore** and **as a result** open a **new sentence**: _The post office was closed. **As a result,** he sent them by e-mail._ · **that's why** opens the second half: _The post office was closed. **That's why** he sent them by e-mail._

### The punctuation, which the examiner does count

| Pattern                                      | How it is written                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| cause **second**, with _because_             | _He stayed at home **because** it was raining._ (no comma)                     |
| cause **first**, with _since / as / because_ | _**Since** it was raining**,** he stayed at home._ (comma after the cause)      |
| result with **so**                           | _It was raining**, so** he stayed at home._ (comma **before** _so_, never after) |
| result with **therefore / as a result**      | _It was raining. **Therefore,** he stayed at home._ (full stop, then a comma)   |

> ⚠️ **One linker per link, never two.** Your own language may well allow the double frame; English forbids it. "**Because** he was late, **so** he missed the bus" ✗ → _Because he was late, he missed the bus._ ✓ or _He was late, so he missed the bus._ ✓

### The two long forms: _so … that_ and _such … that_

They say the cause and the result in **one** movement, and they insist: the cause was so strong that the result could not be avoided.

- **so + adjective (alone) + that** → _The queue was **so** long **that** he gave up._
- **such + a / an + adjective + noun + that** → _It was **such a** long queue **that** he gave up._
- **such + adjective + plural or uncountable noun + that** → _They were **such** long queues **that** everybody gave up._

> ⚠️ **so** takes a bare adjective, **such** takes a noun. "It was so a long queue" ✗ · "The queue was such long" ✗.

## 📱 The e-mailer and the texter — two behaviours, two registers

Lesson 5 puts two people side by side and asks you to **describe and analyse** what they do.

an **inbox** = the folder where the messages you receive arrive · a **smiley** = a small face made of signs, which shows a feeling in a message · **concise** = saying a lot in very few words · **discreet** = careful not to be noticed and not to say too much · **anonymity** = the state of not being known by name or by face · **appeal** = the quality that makes something attractive · **hooked** = unable to stop, addicted · **needy** = asking for attention and reassurance all the time · **desperate** = feeling there is no hope left · **shy** = uncomfortable with people you do not know well · **to confess** = to admit something you would rather have hidden · a **conflict** = a serious disagreement between people · **to handle** = to deal with something difficult without losing control.

A texter writes fast and short; the **anonymity** of a screen makes even a **shy** person bold, and that is part of its **appeal** — but somebody who checks the **inbox** every two minutes is **hooked**, and a message that begs for an answer sounds **needy**. An e-mailer keeps control: **concise**, organised, one subject at a time — but an e-mailer who announces bad news by e-mail is usually somebody who cannot **handle** a **conflict** face to face.

Several of these words lead a double life: the same shape is a noun in one sentence and a verb in the next. Two signs settle it every time — a noun follows an article or a possessive (_an **answer**_, _his **answer**_), while a verb takes its person straight after it and can open an order (_**Answer** him tonight._). The same double life is led by _a call / to call_, _a text / to text_ and _a message / to message_.

### The register: the same news, two ways

The choice is not between good English and bad English. It is between the English you write **to a friend** and the English you write **to somebody you owe respect**.

|                       | Text to a friend    | E-mail to a teacher, a company, an office |
| --------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| opening               | nothing, or _hi_    | _Dear Mr Trabelsi,_ · _Dear Sir or Madam,_ |
| forms                 | _I'm_, _can't_, _won't_ | _I am_, _cannot_, _will not_           |
| shortcuts and smileys | normal              | never                                       |
| sentences             | pieces are enough   | complete, with full stops                   |
| closing               | nothing, or _c u_   | _Yours sincerely,_ · _Kind regards,_       |

_"cant come 2moro sorry :-("_ to your best friend, and _"I am afraid I will not be able to come tomorrow."_ to your head teacher. **Same news. Same person writing. Two languages.** What decides is the language itself — never the length of the message, never the tense it uses, never the machine it was typed on.

### Reading a shortcut

| Rule                                                          | Examples                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a **letter** stands for the word it sounds like               | **U** = you · **R** = are · **C** = see · **Y** = why · **B** = be, so **B4** = before                        |
| a **figure** stands for the sound it makes, or for the number itself | **2** = to / too · **4** = for · **8** = the sound _-ate_ (**L8R** = later, **GR8** = great); but in **M8, RING ME AT 6** the first figure gives _-ate_ and the second is simply the number six |
| the rest is **spelt as it sounds**                            | **LUV** = love · **THRU** = through                                                                          |
| the **vowels are dropped**                                    | **PLS** = please · **THX** = thanks · **MSG** = message                                                       |

_**GR8 NEWS! THX 4 THE MSG** = Great news! Thanks for the message._ Useful between friends — and out of place everywhere else.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                            | Right                                                         | Why                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I go to call him tonight."                      | _I**'m going to** call him tonight._                            | _be going to_ keeps the verb **be**                          |
| "I will to help you."                            | _I **will help** you._                                          | **will** is never followed by _to_                           |
| "I'll going to send it."                         | _I**'ll** send it._ / _I**'m going to** send it._               | one future form at a time                                    |
| "Because of it was a strike, the post was late." | _**Because there was** a strike…_ / _**Because of** the strike…_ | _because_ + clause, _because of_ + noun                     |
| "Because he was late, so he missed the bus."     | _Because he was late, he missed the bus._                       | one linker per link, never two                               |
| "It was so a long message that I stopped."       | _It was **such a** long message that I stopped._                | **so** + adjective alone, **such** + a + adjective + noun    |
| "Dear Sir, hi! whats up? :-)"                    | _Dear Sir, I am writing to…_                                    | an e-mail to a stranger keeps the formal register            |

_I'm going to help you tomorrow_ said to a friend who has just burst into tears is the second most frequent mistake of this chapter — not because the grammar is wrong, but because the moment calls for a promise: _**I'll** help you._
