# ⚔️ Communicating with Others — Friends, Messages and the Words that Link Them

> 💡 "A message costs nothing to send. What it costs is the silence it breaks."

Theme I asked where you come from. Theme II asks **who you talk to, and how**. Three lessons, three moments of a conversation: the friend you find again, the friend you comfort, and the thousand small messages you send every day without ever asking yourself what they say about you.

- **Lesson 3 — Friendship**: talk about yourself and about your friends, tell how it started, and announce what you **intend** to do — **be going to**.
- **Lesson 4 — Bridge Over Troubled Water**: say how somebody **feels**, and say what you **will** do about it.
- **Lesson 5 — The E-mailer Versus the Texter**: describe and analyse a behaviour, and name the **cause** and the **result**.

The two weapons of this chapter are the **future** — which is not one form but four — and the **linkers of cause and result**. Both come back in every single exam paper.

## 🤝 Talking about your friends — the words of friendship

A friendship has a shape: it begins, it fades, sometimes it comes back to life. English has a verb for each stage.

| Word / expression   | What it means                                                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **to keep in touch** | to go on writing, phoning or seeing each other                    |
| **to lose touch**   | to stop being in contact, usually without deciding to               |
| **to renew**        | to start something again after a long break                         |
| **intimate**        | very close — an **intimate friend** is the one you tell everything to |
| **a website**       | a set of pages on the internet                                      |
| **a password**      | the secret word that opens your account and keeps it yours          |
| **a waitress**      | a woman who serves food and drink in a café or a restaurant (a man is **a waiter**) |
| **incredible**      | so surprising that it is hard to believe                            |

_Anis and Zied left primary school together and **lost touch** for eleven years. Last winter Zied found his name on a school **website**, typed his **password** and wrote to him. They have **kept in touch** every week since, and the friendship they **renewed** is stronger than the first one — **incredible**, but true._

And when you judge a friend, English gives you a whole shelf of adjectives. Three of them are warm, three are cold, and each one has an exact meaning.

| Adjective           | It describes somebody who…                                    |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **honest**          | tells the truth even when it costs them something                |
| **faithful / loyal**| stays with you and never betrays you                             |
| **sincere**         | really means what they say                                       |
| **open-minded**     | is ready to accept ideas and people that are not like them       |
| **negligent**       | is careless and does not look after what matters                 |
| **indifferent**     | does not care at all what happens to you                         |
| **narrow-minded**   | refuses every idea that is not already their own                 |

> 🗡️ Learn them in pairs, it costs half the effort: **open-minded** ↔ **narrow-minded**, and **keep in touch** ↔ **lose touch**. And do not confuse **intimate** (very close) with **honest** (truthful) — a friend can be one without being the other.

## ⏪ Telling how it started — the past simple and its three markers

To tell what happened, you already know the tense: the **past simple**. What this lesson adds is the little words that fix the moment.

> Rim and Faten **met** in a summer camp in Nabeul six years **ago**. Nobody **had** a smartphone **at that time**. They **exchanged** two letters, and **then** they **lost** touch.

| Marker           | Where it stands and what it does                                      | Example                                    |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **ago**          | **after** a length of time; counts backwards from today                 | _three years **ago**_, _ten minutes **ago**_ |
| **at that time** | at the moment the sentence has **just named** — the opposite of _now_   | _Petrol was cheap **at that time**._         |
| **then**         | either _at that moment_ or _after that_                                 | _We were fifteen **then**._ / _**Then** she phoned me._ |

> ⚠️ **ago** comes **after** the duration, never in front of it: _two months **ago**_ ✓, "ago two months" ✗. And **ago** dates a finished moment, so it lives with the **past simple** only: _I met her two years ago_ ✓, "I have met her two years ago" ✗.

> 🗡️ **at that time** and **then** never stand alone: they point back to a moment already named. If your reader cannot see which moment, the sentence is broken.

## 🎯 Announcing an intention — _be going to_

Start with a sentence.

> Malek looks up from his phone: **"I'm going to open an account on that website tonight."**

Ask the three questions of the lesson. **Which form is used?** _am + going to + open_. **What does it express?** Not the present: something Malek has **already decided**, before he opened his mouth. **How do you know?** Because _going to_ announces a decision that was taken **earlier** — the plan exists, only the action is still to come.

**Form: am / is / are + going to + base verb.**

| Subject | Affirmative                | Negative                        | Question                        |
| ------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| I       | _I **am going to** write._ | _I **am not going to** write._  | _**Am** I **going to** write?_  |
| he / she / it | _she **is going to** write._ | _she **isn't going to** write._ | _**Is** she **going to** write?_ |
| you / we / they | _we **are going to** write._ | _we **aren't going to** write._ | _**Are** we **going to** write?_ |

**be going to** does two jobs.

1. **An intention already formed, a plan.** _We **are going to** spend the holidays in Béja._ (it is decided)
2. **A prediction you read in what you can see now.** _Look at that sky — it **is going to** rain._ (the clouds are the proof)

> ⚠️ The commonest mistake is to drop the verb _be_: "I going to call him" ✗, "I go to call him" ✗ — this last one translates your own language word for word, and the present simple it produces can only describe a habit. English says _I **am** going to call him_ ✓.

## 🔮 _will_ — the decision you take as you speak

Another sentence, another moment.

> The phone rings in the next room. Nour stands up: **"I'll answer it."**

Nour had no plan. The phone rang, and the decision was born **inside the sentence**. That is what **will** is for.

**Form: will + base verb — the same for every subject.** Short form **'ll**; negative **will not / won't**; question **Will you…?**; short answers _Yes, I will. / No, I won't._

**will** does four jobs.

| Job                            | Example                                                | How you recognise it                     |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| a **decision** taken as you speak | _It's heavy — **I'll** carry it for you._               | something has just happened                |
| a **promise**                  | _Don't worry, **I'll** be there at seven._               | you are answering somebody's worry         |
| an **offer**                   | _**I'll** lend you my notes if you like._                | you give, nobody asked                     |
| a **prediction** or an opinion  | _I think Sabri **will** pass his exam._                 | often after _I think_, _I'm sure_, _perhaps_ |

> ⚠️ **will** is never followed by _to_: "I will to help you" ✗, _I **will help** you_ ✓. It never takes an _-s_ either: "he wills come" ✗, _he **will** come_ ✓. And you never mark the future twice: "I'll going to call him" ✗ — choose one form.

## ⚖️ Four ways to speak about the future — the decision table

This is the heart of the chapter. English chooses its future form according to **when the decision was taken** and **who took it**.

| The situation                                                        | The form              | Example                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| the decision was taken **before** you spoke — a plan, an intention    | **be going to**       | _I**'m going to** repaint my room this summer._ |
| the decision is born **while** you speak — a promise, an offer         | **will**              | _That bag looks heavy. **I'll** take it._      |
| an arrangement **already fixed with somebody**, with a time and a place | **present continuous** | _I**'m meeting** Dorra at six in front of the library._ |
| a **timetable** fixed by an organisation, not by you                   | **present simple**    | _The train **leaves** at 7.15._                |

Read the first two lines together, because the exam lives there. _I'**m going to** buy a new phone_ = I decided last week. _I**'ll** buy you a new phone_ = I am deciding now, and I am promising it to you. Nothing in the sentence changes except the form — and everything in the meaning changes with it.

> 🗡️ For a **prediction**, both forms exist and they do not say the same thing. _It **will** rain tomorrow_ is an opinion, a forecast. _It **is going to** rain_ is what you say when the first drops are already falling on your hand: the proof is in front of you.

## 💙 Attitudes and feelings — the language of comfort

Lesson 4 is built on the song **"Bridge Over Troubled Water"** by Simon and Garfunkel. Its subject is simple and it is the subject of this section: a friend who is exhausted, alone and in tears, and a voice that promises to be the safe crossing over the rough water — the **bridge**. The last part of the song turns towards hope and encouragement.

You will not be asked to sing it. You will be asked to speak like it: **to say how somebody feels, and to say what you will do about it.**

| Word / expression        | What it means                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **to be weary**          | to be worn out, extremely tired — in body or in spirit             |
| **hopeless**             | seeing no way out of a situation                                    |
| **depressed**            | deeply and lastingly sad                                            |
| **a tear**               | a drop of water that runs from the eye when you cry                 |
| **to feel small**        | to feel ashamed and unimportant, as if you counted for nothing      |
| **to comfort somebody**  | to make somebody feel less sad or less afraid                       |
| **to take somebody's part** | to side with somebody, to defend them against the others        |
| **to ease somebody's mind** | to calm an anxious mind                                          |
| **a bridge**             | what lets you cross over — here, the image of the friend who carries you across a bad moment |
| **to sail**              | to travel on water                                                  |
| **to shine**             | to give out light; said of a person, to be at your best and be noticed |

_Ghassen was **weary** and close to **tears** after his interview. When the others laughed at him, Hamza **took his part**; then he **eased his mind** with one sentence: "**I'll** phone the company myself tomorrow." Nobody should be left to **feel small**._

Notice the tense in that last quotation. When you comfort somebody, English almost always reaches for **will**: you are promising, on the spot, and the promise is the comfort.

## 🔁 Cause and result — naming the link

Lesson 5 is the only place in this theme where the book names its own grammar point: the **Cause / Result relation**. Start, once more, from two facts.

> The post office was closed. Sabri sent the documents by e-mail.

Two facts, one link — and English lets you enter it from either end. You can **name the cause**, or you can **name the result**.

### Naming the cause

| Linker            | What follows it            | Example                                                      |
| ----------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **because**       | a **clause** (subject + verb) | _Sabri sent the documents by e-mail **because the post office was closed**._ |
| **since**         | a clause — the reason is already known to both of you | _**Since** the post office was closed, he sent them by e-mail._ |
| **as**            | a clause — same job as _since_, a little more formal | _**As** the post office was closed, he sent them by e-mail._ |
| **because of**    | a **noun** or a noun phrase | _He sent them by e-mail **because of the strike**._            |

### Naming the result

| Linker            | Where it stands                                | Example                                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **so**            | joins two clauses, with **a comma before it**    | _The post office was closed**, so** he sent them by e-mail._  |
| **therefore**     | opens a **new sentence**, and takes a comma after it | _The post office was closed. **Therefore,** he sent them by e-mail._ |
| **as a result**   | same place as _therefore_                        | _The post office was closed. **As a result,** he sent them by e-mail._ |
| **that's why**    | opens the second half, after a full stop or a comma | _The post office was closed. **That's why** he sent them by e-mail._ |

### The two long forms: _so … that_ and _such … that_

They say the cause and the result in **one** movement, and they insist: the cause was so strong that the result could not be avoided.

- **so + adjective (alone) + that** → _The queue was **so** long **that** he gave up._
- **such + a / an + adjective + noun + that** → _It was **such a** long queue **that** he gave up._
- **such + adjective + plural or uncountable noun + that** → _They were **such** long queues **that** everybody gave up._

> ⚠️ **so** takes a bare adjective, **such** takes a noun. "It was so a long queue" ✗ · "The queue was such long" ✗.

### The punctuation, which the examiner does count

| Pattern                          | How it is written                                     |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| cause **second**, with _because_ | _He stayed at home **because** it was raining._ (no comma) |
| cause **first**, with _since / as / because_ | _**Since** it was raining**,** he stayed at home._ (comma after the cause) |
| result with **so**               | _It was raining**, so** he stayed at home._ (comma before _so_) |
| result with **therefore / as a result** | _It was raining. **Therefore,** he stayed at home._ (full stop, then a comma) |

> ⚠️ **One linker per link, never two.** Your own language may well allow the double frame; English forbids it. "**Because** he was late, **so** he missed the bus" ✗ → _Because he was late, he missed the bus._ ✓ or _He was late, so he missed the bus._ ✓

> ⚠️ **because** ≠ **because of**. _because_ + subject + verb, _because of_ + noun. "because of it was raining" ✗ · _because it was raining_ ✓ · _because of the rain_ ✓.

## 📱 The e-mailer and the texter — two behaviours, two registers

Lesson 5 puts two people side by side and asks you to **describe and analyse** what they do.

| Word           | What it means                                                     |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **an inbox**   | the folder where the messages you receive arrive                    |
| **a smiley**   | a small face made of signs, which shows a feeling in a message      |
| **concise**    | saying a lot in very few words                                      |
| **discreet**   | careful not to be noticed and not to say too much                   |
| **anonymity**  | the state of not being known by name or by face                     |
| **appeal**     | the quality that makes something attractive                         |
| **hooked**     | unable to stop — addicted                                           |
| **needy**      | asking for attention and reassurance all the time                   |
| **desperate**  | feeling there is no hope left, and ready to do anything             |
| **shy**        | uncomfortable with people you do not know well                      |
| **to confess** | to admit something you would rather have hidden                     |
| **a conflict** | a serious disagreement between people                               |
| **to handle**  | to deal with something difficult without losing control             |

A texter writes fast and short; the **anonymity** of a screen makes even a **shy** person bold, and that is part of its **appeal** — but somebody who checks the **inbox** every two minutes is **hooked**, and a message that begs for an answer sounds **needy**. An e-mailer keeps control: **concise**, organised, one subject at a time — but an e-mailer who announces bad news by e-mail is usually somebody who cannot **handle** a **conflict** face to face.

### The register: the same news, two ways

The choice is not between good English and bad English. It is between the English you write **to a friend** and the English you write **to somebody you owe respect**.

| | Text to a friend | E-mail to a teacher, a company, an office |
| --- | --- | --- |
| opening | nothing, or _hi_ | _Dear Mr Trabelsi,_ · _Dear Sir or Madam,_ |
| forms | _I'm_, _can't_, _won't_ | _I am_, _cannot_, _will not_ |
| shortcuts and smileys | normal | never |
| sentences | pieces are enough | complete, with full stops |
| closing | nothing, or _c u_ | _Yours sincerely,_ · _Kind regards,_ |
| subject line | none | short, precise, **concise** |

_"cant come 2moro sorry :-("_ to your best friend, and _"I am afraid I will not be able to come tomorrow."_ to your head teacher. **Same news. Same person writing. Two languages.**

### Reading a shortcut

Text shortcuts are not random: they follow three rules, and once you know them you can read any of them.

| Rule                                             | Examples                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| a **letter** stands for the word it sounds like  | **U** = you · **R** = are · **C** = see · **Y** = why |
| a **figure** stands for the sound it makes       | **2** = to / too · **4** = for · **8** = the sound _-ate_ (**L8R** = later, **GR8** = great, **M8** = mate) |
| the **vowels are dropped**                       | **PLS** = please · **THX** = thanks · **MSG** = message |

_**CU 2MORO B4 8** = See you tomorrow before eight._ Useful between friends — and out of place everywhere else.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                          | Right                                                  | Why                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I go to call him tonight."                    | _I**'m going to** call him tonight._                    | _be going to_ keeps the verb **be**                          |
| "I will to help you."                          | _I **will help** you._                                  | **will** is never followed by _to_                           |
| "I'll going to send it."                       | _I**'ll** send it._ / _I**'m going to** send it._       | one future form at a time                                    |
| "I have lost touch with him two years ago."    | _I **lost** touch with him two years ago._              | **ago** dates a finished moment → past simple                |
| "Ago three months she changed her number."     | _She changed her number three months **ago**._          | **ago** comes **after** the duration                         |
| "Because of it was a strike, the post was late." | _**Because** it was a strike…_ / _**Because of** the strike…_ | _because_ + clause, _because of_ + noun            |
| "Because he was late, so he missed the bus."   | _Because he was late, he missed the bus._               | one linker per link, never two                               |
| "It was so a long message that I stopped."     | _It was **such a** long message that I stopped._        | **so** + adjective alone, **such** + a + adjective + noun    |
| "Dear Sir, hi! whats up? :-)"                  | _Dear Sir, I am writing to…_                            | an e-mail to a stranger keeps the formal register            |

_I'm going to help you tomorrow_ said to a friend who has just burst into tears is the second most frequent mistake of this chapter — not because the grammar is wrong, but because the moment calls for a promise: _**I'll** help you._
