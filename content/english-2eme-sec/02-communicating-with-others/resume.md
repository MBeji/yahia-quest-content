# 📜 Summary: Communicating with Others

- **The words of friendship** — **keep in touch** (go on being in contact), **lose touch** (stop being in contact), **renew** (start again after a break), **intimate** (very close), **honest** (tells the truth), a **website**, a **password**, a **waitress** (a man is a **waiter**), **incredible** (hard to believe); and to judge a friend, **faithful / loyal** (never betrays you), **sincere** (means what they say), **open-minded** (accepts what is not like them), against **negligent** (careless), **indifferent** (does not care) and **narrow-minded** (refuses every idea that is not their own). The clean pair to remember: **open-minded** ↔ **narrow-minded**.
- **Telling how it started: the past simple and its markers** — **ago** comes **after** the length of time and counts back from today (_two months ago_, never "ago two months"), and it only ever goes with the past simple ("I have met her two years ago" ✗); **at that time** points back to the moment just named and is the opposite of _now_ (for the present, _today_ or _nowadays_; for a stretch running up to now, _since then_ with a present perfect); **then** means _at that moment_ or _after that_.
- **The future: which form, and why** — the form depends on **when the decision was taken**. Taken **before** you speak, a plan or an intention → **be going to** (**am / is / are + going to + base verb**), which also predicts what you can already see coming (_it's going to rain_). Born **while** you speak → **will** (**will + base verb**, same for every subject, short form **'ll**, negative **won't**), and its four jobs are a **decision** on the spot, a **promise**, an **offer** and a **prediction**. Of all the ways of answering, a plain _I'll be there_ is the one that commits you most firmly.
- **The two other forms that look ahead, and the three mistakes** — the **present continuous** of an arrangement fixed with somebody, with a time and a place (_I'm meeting Dorra at six._), and the **present simple** of a timetable (_The train leaves at 7.15._); neither announces a decision, and a present simple with no timetable behind it is just a **habit**. Never drop **be** ("I go to call him" ✗), never put _to_ after **will** ("I will to help you" ✗), never mark the future twice ("I'll going to call him" ✗).
- **Attitudes and feelings** — **to be weary** (worn out), **hopeless**, **depressed**, a **tear**, **to feel small** (ashamed, unimportant), **to comfort somebody**, **to take somebody's part** (defend them), **to ease somebody's mind** (calm it), a **bridge** (what lets you cross over), **to sail**, **to shine** (of a person, to be at your best). When you comfort, English often reaches for **will**: the promise is the comfort — but what you cannot control yourself, you can only predict.
- **Naming the cause** — **because** + subject + verb; **since** and **as** + subject + verb, when the reason is already known; **because of** + a **noun**. "because of it was raining" ✗ → _because it was raining_ ✓ or _because of the rain_ ✓. ⚠️ **since** has a second job you already know, the one of time (_since 2016_); only the words around it tell you which one is at work.
- **Naming the result, and the punctuation the examiner counts** — **so** joins two clauses and takes a comma **before** it, never after (_It was raining, so he stayed in._); **therefore** and **as a result** open a new sentence and take a comma **after** them (_It was raining. Therefore, he stayed in._); **that's why** opens the second half. A cause placed **first** takes a comma after it (_Since it was raining, he stayed in._); a cause placed **second** with _because_ takes **no** comma. And **one linker per link, never two**: "Because he was late, so he missed the bus" ✗.
- **The two long forms** — **so + adjective alone + that** (_The queue was so long that he gave up._) and **such + a + adjective + noun + that** (_It was such a long queue that he gave up._). "so a long queue" ✗, "such long" ✗.
- **Describing behaviour** — an **inbox**, a **smiley**, **concise** (much in few words), **discreet**, **anonymity**, **appeal**, **hooked** (addicted), **needy**, **desperate**, **shy**, **to confess**, a **conflict**, **to handle** (deal with without losing control). Several of these words are a noun in one sentence and a verb in the next: a noun follows an article or a possessive (_an answer_, _his answer_), while a verb takes its person straight after it and can open an order (_Answer him tonight._).
- **Register, and reading a shortcut** — to a friend, contractions, shortcuts and smileys are normal; to a teacher, a company or an office, an e-mail keeps **Dear Sir or Madam**, full forms (_I am_, _cannot_, _will not_), complete sentences and **Yours sincerely**, and never a smiley. What decides is the language itself, never the length, never the tense, never the machine. In a shortcut, a letter stands for the word it sounds like (**U** = you, **R** = are, **C** = see, **B** = be, so **B4** = before); a figure stands for the sound it makes (**2** = to, **4** = for, **8** = _-ate_, so **L8R** = later, **GR8** = great) **or keeps its own value**, in which case it is simply the number; the rest is spelt as it sounds (**LUV**, **THRU**); and the vowels are dropped (**PLS**, **THX**, **MSG**).
