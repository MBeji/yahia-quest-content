# 🎓 Education — Wearing the Same Clothes, Sitting the Same Exam

> 💡 "I think that by putting us all in the same uniform, they suppress our identities." — a pupil of an English girls' school, quoted in Lesson 18.

Theme VII is the theme of **school seen from the inside**. Two lessons, and both of them are about a pressure you already know:

- **Lesson 18 — School uniforms**: five pupils of an English school disagree about the clothes they are made to wear. You learn to say what you think and to **hold your ground**, and you meet a small family of pronouns that turn a verb back on the person who did it: **the reflexive pronouns**.
- **Lesson 19 — Coping with exams**: seven pupils are in trouble in an examination room, and seven pieces of advice are waiting to be matched to them. This is where the language of **advice** lives — `had better`, `ought to`, `should` — and its opposite, `needn't`, which says that something is **not worth doing at all**.

> ⚠️ Your book prints a table of reflexive pronouns with **one** cell filled in and eight left empty, under the note "Complete the table with your teacher's help." This chapter is that help. The whole table is below, and so is the reason the word `You` appears in it **twice**.

## 👗 The words of the uniform debate

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **a uniform** | the set of clothes everybody in one school (or one job) must wear |
| **to distinguish** | to recognise the difference between one thing and another |
| **smart** | tidy and well dressed — *not* "clever" in this sense |
| **individuality** | what makes you yourself and not somebody else; your personality |
| **to suppress** | to put an end to something, to stop it existing |
| **to conform to** | to follow a rule, a custom, a standard |
| **a community** | a group of people who belong together |
| **competition** | the situation where people try to do better than each other |
| **an identity** | who a person is |

> ⚠️ **smart** is a false friend twice over. In this lesson it means **well dressed**, not intelligent — and never "elegant" in the French sense of *élégant* alone: a smart pupil is neat, pressed and clean. Read it from the context: if the sentence is about clothes, it is about clothes.

## 🗣️ Five pupils, three positions

Lesson 18 is a set of five short opinions collected in an English girls' school, published by Linguapress. You are not asked to learn them by heart; you are asked to **sort** them, and to see that a debate has more than two sides.

| Speaker | Position | The argument, in one line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rachel** | for | You do not have to decide what to wear each morning — and there is **no competition** between the girls. |
| **Anita** | for | It **distinguishes** you from other schools and makes you look like a **community** — and people say you look **smart**. |
| **Sue** | for | You do not lose your **individuality** by wearing the same clothes as everybody else. |
| **Katie** | against | Putting everyone in the same uniform **suppresses** their identities. |
| **Sarah** | **both** | She was proud of hers when she arrived from primary school — and she still sees faults in it. |

> ⭐ **Sarah is the interesting one.** Four speakers land on one side; she lands on both, and says so openly ("there are faults in it, but I think there are the advantages as well"). In an argument, admitting the other side's point is not weakness — it is what makes the rest of what you say believable.

Notice also **what kind of text** this is. Your book asks you to decide, from the openings alone, whether it is *descriptive*, *argumentative* or *expository*:

- a **descriptive** text tells you what something looks like;
- an **expository** text explains how something works, without taking a side;
- an **argumentative** text defends a position.

Every one of the five speakers is defending a position. The text is **argumentative** — and you could tell from the first line of each paragraph, which is exactly what the exercise wanted you to notice.

### Saying what you think, and standing for it

| To open an opinion | To agree | To disagree |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *I think that…* | *I agree that…* | *I disagree that…* |
| *I believe that…* | *That's true.* | *I don't think so.* |
| *In my opinion,…* | *Exactly.* | *I'm not so sure.* |

> ⚠️ *I think so* and *I don't think so* stand **alone**, as a whole answer: — *Will the rule change?* — *I don't think so.* You cannot say "I don't think so that…". Once you add a clause, you drop *so*: *I don't think that the rule will change.*

## 🪞 Reflexive pronouns — when the doer and the receiver are the same person

Look at the sentence your book takes from Sue:

> There's still something special about **yourself** as a person.

*Yourself* is not a personal pronoun (*you*), and not a relative pronoun (*who*, *which*). It is a **reflexive pronoun** — the form English uses when the action comes back to the person who started it.

> **I cut myself.** — one person, doing and receiving.
> **I cut him.** — two people.

### The whole table

| Personal pronoun | Reflexive pronoun |
| --- | --- |
| I | **myself** |
| You *(one person)* | **yourself** |
| He | **himself** |
| She | **herself** |
| It | **itself** |
| One | **oneself** |
| We | **ourselves** |
| You *(more than one)* | **yourselves** |
| They | **themselves** |

> ⭐ **Why `You` appears twice.** English has one word for *you* whether it speaks to one person or to twenty — but it has **two** reflexives: **yourself** for one, **yourselves** for several. This is the only place in the language where the singular and the plural *you* are visibly different, and it is why your book prints the row twice.

Three things about the spelling, and they are worth ten marks between them:

1. The singular forms end in **-self**; the plural forms end in **-selves**. *ourselves*, *yourselves*, *themselves* — never "ourself", never "themself".
2. *my-*, *your-*, *him-*, *her-*, *it-*, *our-*, *them-* — the possessive for the first and second persons, the object pronoun for the third. So it is **himself** (never "hisself") and **themselves** (never "theirselves").
3. **oneself** goes with the impersonal **one**, in a formal, general statement: *One should look after oneself.*

### Where the reflexive pronoun goes

Straight after the verb, in the place the object would take:

> *You have to blame **yourself** for the accident.*
> *He's talking to **himself**.*
> *The children look after **themselves** while their parents are away.*

And watch the person the sentence is really about, not the person the sentence starts with:

> — *Did you enjoy **yourselves** on the holidays?* — *Yes, we enjoyed **ourselves** very much.*

The question is put to a group, so it takes **yourselves**; the answer comes from *we*, so it takes **ourselves**. The reflexive always agrees with **its own subject**, not with the sentence before it.

> ⚠️ Do not confuse **themselves** with **each other**. *They looked at themselves* = each person looked at his own reflection. *They looked at each other* = they exchanged looks. Same number of people, opposite direction.

## 😰 The examination room — seven situations, seven answers

Lesson 19 puts seven pupils in an examination and lets each of them say what is going wrong. Then it gives you seven pieces of advice and asks you to put each one where it belongs. The whole exercise turns on **reading the complaint closely**, because two pairs of it are deliberately close.

| Pupil | What goes wrong | The advice that answers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paul** | Not sure of two answers, and **no time left** to check them. | Always leave enough time to check all your answers. |
| **Jane** | Revised what she already knew, and **not** the passive. | Use your revision plan; spend most of your time on what you are unsure of. |
| **Mary** | The paper is easy, so she **won't bother** checking. | Use all the time available; if you finish early, go back and check everything. |
| **Beth** | **Ten minutes** on one question, and still stuck. | Don't waste time on one question — leave it and come back later. |
| **Andy** | Revised everything, and now his **mind has gone blank**. | Keep calm. If you've done your revision, there's no need to panic. |
| **Barbara** | Started revising **last week**, was up late, could not sleep. | Don't revise heavily at the last minute. Get a good night's sleep. |
| **Steve** | Time almost gone and a whole section **unread**. | Plan your time: look through the whole paper first. |

> ⭐ **The trap of the exercise, and it is a good one.** Paul and Mary both talk about **checking answers** — but Paul has **no time left** to check, and Mary has **time to spare** and refuses to use it. One needs to make room; the other needs to stop wasting it. Read what the pupil *has*, not only what the pupil *says*.

### The words of examinations

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **to sit / to take an exam** | to do the examination |
| **to pass an exam** | to succeed in it |
| **to fail an exam** | not to succeed in it |
| **to cope with** | to manage something difficult without breaking down |
| **to check** | to look again to make sure something is right |
| **blank** | empty — *my mind's gone blank* = I can suddenly remember nothing |
| **to revise** | to go back over what you have learnt |
| **revision** | the work of revising (uncountable — "a revision" ✗) |

> ⚠️ **to pass an exam** is not *passer un examen*. In English, *passing* an exam means **succeeding**. To say only that you were there, use **to sit** or **to take**. A pupil who "passed the exam" got through it; a pupil who "took the exam" may have failed it.

## 😟 Saying that you are afraid — the other function of Lesson 19

The teacher's guide gives Lesson 19 **two** functions, not one: advising, and **expressing fear and worry**. The seven pupils in the examination room are doing the second all the time, and the language is worth having.

| To say you are afraid | Followed by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **I'm afraid of** | a noun or **-ing** | *I'm afraid **of** failing.* |
| **I'm afraid (that)** | a whole clause | *I'm afraid **that** I'll forget everything.* |
| **I'm worried about** | a noun or **-ing** | *I'm worried **about** the last section.* |
| **I'm anxious about** | a noun or **-ing** | *I'm anxious **about** tomorrow.* |
| **What if…?** | a clause | ***What if** my mind goes blank?* |
| **I can't stop thinking about…** | a noun or **-ing** | *I can't stop thinking **about** the results.* |

> ⚠️ **`afraid of` and `afraid that` are not interchangeable.** *of* is followed by a **noun or an -ing form** — *afraid of the dark*, *afraid of failing*; *that* is followed by a **subject and a verb** — *afraid that I will fail*. "afraid of I will fail" ✗, "afraid that failing" ✗.
> ⚠️ And **`I'm afraid`** on its own is not fear at all: it is a polite way of announcing bad news — *I'm afraid the library is closed.*

And to calm somebody down, the language your book puts in the advice column doubles as reassurance: *There's **no need to** panic.* · *Don't **worry**.* · *You'll **be fine**.* · ***Keep calm**.*

## 🧭 Six ways to give advice, to reproach, and to conclude

This is the grammar of the chapter, and the exercise of Lesson 19 uses **six** forms — two more than the teacher's guide lists. Here they all are, sorted by what they actually do.

### Advice: `had better`, `ought to`, `should`

All three are followed by the **base verb**, with no *to* except after *ought*.

| Form | Example | The shade of meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **had better** + base verb | *You **had better** revise the last chapter.* | urgent — there is a bad consequence if you don't |
| **ought to** + base verb | *You **ought to** read the whole test once first.* | it is the right thing to do |
| **should** + base verb | *You **should** plan your time.* | the ordinary, everyday advice |

Three points of form that examiners look for:

- **had better** is written with **had**, never with *have*, and it stays *had* for everybody: *I had better*, *he had better*, *they had better*. It is usually shortened to **'d better** — and that apostrophe is why so many pupils write "you better" and lose the mark.
- The negative is **had better not** + base verb: *You'd better not leave it to the last minute.* Not "hadn't better".
- **ought** is the only one of the three that keeps **to**: *ought to go*, but *should go* and *had better go*.

### Lack of necessity: `needn't`

**needn't** + base verb says that an action is **not necessary** — that you may do it, but there is no point.

> *You **needn't** turn off the computer in the lab. It is programmed to switch off automatically.*

> ⚠️⚠️ **The mistake that costs most marks in this chapter.** `needn't` is **not** the negative of *must*. Compare:
> *You **mustn't** open the door.* → it is **forbidden**.
> *You **needn't** open the door.* → it is **not necessary** — nobody is stopping you.
> One closes the door; the other says the door does not matter. They are opposites, not neighbours.

### Regret and reproach about the past: `should have`, `shouldn't have`

Add **have + past participle** and the whole thing moves into the past — and stops being advice, because it is too late for advice.

| Form | Example | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **should have** + past participle | *I know I **should've** studied last night, but I watched TV instead.* | **regret** — the speaker blames himself |
| **shouldn't have** + past participle | *You **shouldn't have** left it so late.* | **reproach** — the speaker blames somebody else |

The only difference between the two is **who is being blamed**. Both describe something that **did not happen the right way**, and neither can be repaired.

### A conclusion about the present: `must`

**must** + base verb is not always an obligation. When you look at evidence and draw a conclusion, *must* measures **how sure you are**:

> *Mr Bean looks anxious. He **must** be worried about his sick mother.*

Nobody is obliging Mr Bean to worry. The speaker sees the anxious face and concludes. That is why the exercise puts *could*, *must* and *couldn't* side by side: *could* = it is possible; **must** = I am sure it is so; *couldn't* = I am sure it is not.

### Offering: `will`

> *I **will** be glad to help you with the revision any time you ask.*

This is neither advice nor obligation: it is an **offer of help**, decided at the moment of speaking. `will` is the form English uses for a decision taken **as you speak** — an offer, a promise, an instant reaction.

> ⚠️ Your teacher's book files this sentence under "future". That is not wrong about the time, but it misses what the sentence *does* — and the table you have to tick has no column called "future". The column it belongs in is **offering help**. Once you put it there, the six columns of the table are all used exactly as they were meant to be: regret · offering help · reproach · unnecessary action · advice (twice) · deduction.

### The one-line test

| Ask yourself… | …and the answer names the form |
| --- | --- |
| Am I telling someone what to do **now**? | should / ought to / had better |
| Am I saying it is **pointless**? | needn't |
| Am I saying it is **forbidden**? | mustn't |
| Am I sorry about **my own** past? | should have |
| Am I blaming **somebody else's** past? | shouldn't have |
| Am I **concluding** from evidence? | must |
| Am I **offering** something? | will |

## ✍️ Writing as a process — the last two steps

Lessons 18 and 19 close the chain your book has been building all year. Lesson 18 asks for the **first draft**; Lesson 19 asks for the **final draft**.

1. **Gather** your ideas — anything, in any order.
2. **Organise** them — group what belongs together, throw away what does not.
3. **First draft** (Lesson 18) — write it through without stopping to polish. Then **exchange it with a classmate** for proofreading.
4. **Final draft** (Lesson 19) — read it once more yourself, correct what your classmate marked, and only then hand it in.

> ⭐ Step 3 says *exchange*, not *show*. A reader who has to write comments finds things the writer cannot see, because the writer already knows what he meant. Keep the corrected copy in your file: your book asks for it "for later uses", and it means it.
