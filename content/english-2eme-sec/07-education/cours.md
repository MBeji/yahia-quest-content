# 🎓 Education — Coming Back on Yourself, and Being Told What to Do

> 💡 Two questions run through this theme: *who am I when everybody wears the same clothes?* and *what should I have done before the exam?* English answers the first with a pronoun and the second with a modal.

Theme VII is the school looking at itself. Two lessons, and nothing else:

- **Lesson 18 — School uniforms**: a file of opinions for and against the uniform, the words of that debate, and a grammar point the book makes you meet inside somebody else's sentence before naming it — the **reflexive pronouns**.
- **Lesson 19 — Coping with exams**: seven pupils in trouble in an exam room, seven pieces of advice, and the small family of **modals** that turn a piece of advice into a warning, a duty, an unnecessary effort or a reproach.

## 🏫 The words of the debate, and the words of the exam room

| Word                       | Meaning                                                                            |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a uniform**              | the set of clothes that every pupil of one school has to wear                       |
| **to conform to** (a rule) | to follow a rule, to do what the group or the law expects                           |
| **to distinguish**         | to see or to show the difference between two things; *this badge distinguishes us from the other schools* = it sets us apart from them |
| **smart**                  | **tidy and well dressed** — this is the meaning of the lesson, not "clever"          |
| **to suppress**            | to put an end to something, to stop it from showing itself                          |
| **individuality**          | what makes one person different from every other — their personality                 |
| **to cope with**           | to deal with a difficult thing successfully, without breaking down                   |
| **to check**               | to look at your work again to make sure it is right                                  |
| **to fail**                | not to pass; *to fail an exam* is not to reach the mark you needed                   |
| **blank**                  | empty, with nothing written on it; *my mind went blank* = for a moment I could remember nothing |
| **to revise / revision**   | to go over what you have learnt before an exam                                       |
| **to panic**               | to lose your head suddenly because you are afraid                                    |

> ⚠️ **Four collocations to learn whole, because English fixes them and your own language does not.**
> _to **cope with** a problem_ (never "cope a problem") · _to **conform to** a rule_ (never "conform a rule") · _to **fail** an exam_ (never "fail in an exam" or "fail at an exam") · _to **check** your answers_ (never "check on your answers" when you simply mean to look at them again).

> 🗡️ **smart** is the word that catches everybody. In this lesson a pupil who looks smart is **well dressed**; a pupil who is clever is **clever**, **bright** or **intelligent**. Saying "she is very smart at maths" is not the English of your book.

## 🪞 Reflexive pronouns — the sentence that comes back on itself

A reflexive pronoun is what you use when the person who **does** the action and the person who **receives** it are **the same person**. The action leaves the subject and comes straight back to it, like a ball off a wall.

| Personal pronoun | Reflexive pronoun | Personal pronoun | Reflexive pronoun |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | ---------------- | ------------------ |
| I                | **myself**         | we               | **ourselves**      |
| you (one person) | **yourself**       | you (a group)    | **yourselves**     |
| he               | **himself**        | they             | **themselves**     |
| she              | **herself**        | one              | **oneself**        |
| it               | **itself**         |                  |                    |

Read the table twice. The singular ends in **-self**, the plural ends in **-selves**. And **you** is the only pronoun with two reflexives: **yourself** for one person, **yourselves** for a group. A teacher speaking to the whole class says *sign **yourselves** in at the door*; the same teacher speaking to one pupil says *sign **yourself** in*.

> ⚠️ Three spellings that are simply wrong, and that a corrector marks every year: "himselfs", "theirselves", "hisself". There are nine forms in the table above and no others. And **myself** is one word, never "my self".

### 1. The obligatory reflexive — the sentence needs it to stand up

Here, the reflexive fills the **object** place. Remove it and the sentence collapses or changes its meaning completely.

> _Hedi **burnt himself** on the laboratory hotplate._ — he burnt a person, and that person is Hedi.
> _Emna **blamed herself** for the mark she got._ — she is at once the one who blames and the one who is blamed.
> _The two boys **behaved themselves** all through the visit._
> _You will have to **express yourself** clearly in the oral._

The test is one question: **who receives the action?** If the answer is the subject, you need the reflexive.

> ⚠️ The mistake this rule exists to stop: _"Emna made **her** a cup of tea."_ says the tea was **for somebody else**. _"Emna made **herself** a cup of tea."_ says she made it for her own use. Same verb, two different stories, and only the pronoun tells them apart.

### 2. The emphatic reflexive — you can cross it out

Now the opposite case. The reflexive is not the object of anything; it simply insists that **this person and nobody else** did the action. Cross it out and the sentence still stands, and still means the same thing — it has only lost its emphasis.

> _The club members built the shelves **themselves**._ → *The club members built the shelves.* Still true. The reflexive only says: no carpenter was called in.
> _I wrote the letter **myself**._ → *I wrote the letter.* Still true.
> _The mayor **himself** handed out the certificates._ — the emphatic reflexive may also stand right after the subject.

**So you have a test that never fails.** Take the reflexive out of the sentence. If what is left is still a full, true sentence, the reflexive was **emphatic**. If what is left has a hole in it, or says something else, the reflexive was **obligatory**.

### 3. *by* + reflexive — with nobody's help

> _Mouna revised the whole chapter **by herself**._ = alone, without help from anyone.

**by myself / by yourself / by himself** answers the question *who helped you?* with *nobody*. It is not the same as the emphatic reflexive, which answers *who did it, then?* with *this person and no other*.

### 4. *each other* against *themselves* — two people, two very different pictures

This is the finest distinction of the lesson, and the one an exam likes best.

> _The two winners congratulated **each other**._ — the first congratulated the second **and** the second congratulated the first. The action crosses between them.
> _The two winners congratulated **themselves**._ — each one was pleased with **his own** result. Nothing crosses; there are two separate actions.

Draw the arrows if it helps: **each other** draws two arrows that cross, **themselves** draws two little loops. Use **each other** whenever the meaning is *one another*: _they help each other_, _they write to each other_, _they look at each other_.

### 5. The verbs that take a reflexive in your language and none at all in English

This is where your own language pushes hardest, and it costs marks every year. These English verbs take **no** pronoun after them:

| Wrong                              | Right                            |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| "I feel myself tired."             | _I **feel** tired._               |
| "She concentrated herself on question three." | _She **concentrated** on question three._ |
| "Hurry yourself, the bell has gone." | _**Hurry up**, the bell has gone._ |
| "We relaxed ourselves after the test." | _We **relaxed** after the test._ |
| "They met themselves at the library." | _They **met** at the library._ (or *they met **each other***) |
| "I woke myself up at five."        | _I **woke up** at five._          |

The rule of thumb: in English the reflexive appears only when you can honestly answer the question **"did what to whom?"** with the same name twice.

## 🧭 Giving advice — four ways, four different forces

Your book makes you rewrite seven pieces of advice with **had better**, **ought to** and **needn't**, and never says what any of them means. Here is the whole family, with the form of the verb that follows.

| Modal              | Form after it            | What it really says                                          | Example                                              |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **should**         | base verb (no *to*)      | ordinary advice, the everyday one                             | _You **should** draw up a revision timetable._        |
| **ought to**       | ***to*** + base verb     | advice as a **duty** — what is right, what is expected of you | _We **ought to** return the keys we borrowed from the office._ |
| **had better**     | base verb (no *to*)      | an **urgent** piece of advice, with a bad result hanging over it if you do not follow it | _You **had better** leave now, or you will miss the bus._ |
| **needn't**        | base verb (no *to*)      | this is **not necessary** — you may do it, but there is no need | _You **needn't** bring a dictionary, because one is provided in every room._ |

**Three forms to get right, because they are what a corrector looks at first.**

- **had better** takes a bare verb: _you had better **revise**_, never "had better to revise". It is very often contracted: _you**'d** better revise_. Its negative is **had better not** + bare verb: _You **had better not** argue with the examiner._ Never "you hadn't better", never "you had better don't".
- **ought to** keeps its **to**: _you ought **to** check_, never "you ought check". Its negative is _ought **not to**_.
- **needn't** is one modal word: _you needn't **come**_, never "you needn't to come" and never "you don't needn't".

> ⚠️ **needn't is not mustn't.** _You **needn't** book a seat on the school bus_ says there is always room, so do as you please. _You **mustn't** book a seat on the school bus_ says it is forbidden. One frees you, the other stops you, and confusing them turns a helpful notice into a ban.

> 🗡️ **had better against ought to.** _You had better hand in your file before Friday_ warns you: something unpleasant follows if you do not. _You ought to hand in your file before Friday_ tells you it is the right thing to do. The first has a threat behind it, the second has a duty.

## ⏳ *should have* + past participle — the duty that was not done

Now move all of this into the past. The action did **not** happen, and it should have.

> **should have / shouldn't have + past participle**

The form never varies: **should** + **have** + the third column of your verb list. It is often heard as *should've*, but in writing you keep the three words.

| Sentence                                              | Who is it about? | What it expresses |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------ |
| _You **should have started** your revision in April._ | somebody else     | a **reproach**     |
| _I **should have started** my revision in April._     | yourself          | a **regret**       |
| _You **shouldn't have stayed** up all night._         | somebody else     | a **reproach** for a thing that **was** done |
| _I **shouldn't have spent** an hour on question one._ | yourself          | a **regret**       |

**The same form carries both values, and only the subject decides which.** Turn it on yourself and it is a regret; turn it on somebody else and it is a reproach. That is exactly what the empty table on page 107 was asking you to see.

> ⚠️ Do not confuse the two time zones. _You **shouldn't revise** all night_ is advice about **next time**, and the night is still to come. _You **shouldn't have revised** all night_ is about a night that is over: it is too late, and the sentence blames. A question that describes something already done and asks for a reproach can never be answered with a present modal.

> ⚠️ And never write "should of". It only sounds like *should've*. The word is **have**.

## 💬 Saying what you think, and saying that you are afraid

Lesson 18 asks you to take a side in a debate; lesson 19 asks you to name what you feel before an exam. Two small stocks of expressions, worth learning by heart.

| Function                    | What you say                                                                            |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **giving an opinion**       | _I think that…_ · _I believe that…_ · _In my opinion,…_ · _As far as I am concerned,…_    |
| **agreeing**                | _I agree that…_ · _That is exactly my view._ · _You have a point there._                   |
| **disagreeing politely**    | _I disagree that…_ · _I don't think so._ · _I see what you mean, but…_                     |
| **expressing fear / worry** | _I am **worried about** the second paper._ · _I am **afraid (that)** I will run out of time._ |
|                             | _I am **anxious about** the results._ · _What **if** I forget everything?_                  |
| **calming somebody**        | _Take it one question at a time._ · _Breathe, and start with what you know._               |

> ⚠️ **worried about** a thing, **afraid of** a thing, but **afraid that** + a whole clause. "I am worried **for** the exam" and "I am afraid **from** the exam" are both calques; neither is English.

## 📋 Reading a school notice — three different forces in one paragraph

A regulation is not a list of orders. In the same six lines a school will **forbid** one thing, **advise** another and tell you that a third is **not necessary** — and the whole meaning of the notice lies in telling them apart.

| The notice says…                         | The force it has  | You may read it as…                    |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| _Pupils **must not** use the side gate._  | **forbidden**      | it is not allowed, ever                 |
| _Bags **are not allowed** in the lab._    | **forbidden**      | the passive says the same thing         |
| _Candidates **are advised to** arrive early._ | **advised**    | strongly recommended, not compulsory    |
| _You **had better** bring your own pen._  | **advised**        | with a warning attached                 |
| _There is **no need to** bring a calculator._ | **not necessary** | = _you **needn't** bring one_           |
| _Pupils **do not have to** wear the tie in June._ | **not necessary** | permission to skip it, not a ban        |

> 🗡️ Read the verb, not the tone. A notice can be polite and still forbid; it can be firm and still be only advice. **must not / are not allowed** forbid. **are advised to / should / ought to / had better** advise. **need not / do not have to / there is no need to** release you.

## ⚖️ For and against — building an honest argument

The uniform file of lesson 18 is a debate, and a debate has a shape.

- An **argument for** the uniform: it saves you the morning question of what to wear; it removes the competition over clothes; it makes a school look like one community.
- An **argument against**: it suppresses individuality; it makes everybody look and, some say, think alike; it costs a family money it may not have.
- A **concession** is the sentence where a writer admits that the other side has one good point, before going on with their own: _**It is true that** a uniform saves time in the morning, **but** it takes away the one choice a pupil makes alone._

Spotting the concession is how you find out where a writer really stands: the concession is the sentence that **does not** follow their conclusion. A text is *for* the uniform if its conclusion is for it, even when it admits a defect on the way — and the reverse.

> ⚠️ In a debate you attack the **rule**, never the person. "Pupils who wear a uniform have no personality" is not an argument, it is a judgement on people. "A uniform leaves fewer ways of showing who you are" is an argument, and it can be answered.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                       | Right                                    | Why                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "The girls helped theirselves."             | _The girls helped **themselves**._       | there is no such word as "theirselves"                       |
| "Class, prepare yourself for the test."     | _Class, prepare **yourselves**…_         | a group takes the plural reflexive                            |
| "The two friends helped themselves."        | _The two friends helped **each other**._ | if the help crosses between them, it is *each other*          |
| "I feel myself tired."                      | _I **feel** tired._                      | *feel* takes no reflexive in English                          |
| "He bought him a new bag." (for his own use) | _He bought **himself** a new bag._      | the object pronoun sends the bag to somebody else             |
| "You had better to sleep."                  | _You **had better sleep**._              | *had better* takes the bare verb                              |
| "You hadn't better waste time."             | _You **had better not** waste time._     | the negative goes after *better*                              |
| "You ought check your answers."             | _You **ought to** check your answers._   | *ought* keeps its *to*                                        |
| "You needn't to come on Saturday."          | _You **needn't come** on Saturday._      | *needn't* takes the bare verb                                 |
| "You mustn't bring a calculator." (when there is one on each desk) | _You **needn't** bring one._ | *mustn't* forbids; *needn't* releases        |
| "I should of revised."                      | _I **should have** revised._             | *should've* is *should have*                                  |
| "You should have revise earlier."           | _You should have **revised** earlier._   | after *have* comes the past participle                        |
| "He failed in the exam."                    | _He **failed** the exam._                | *fail* takes its object with no preposition                   |
| "She cannot cope the pressure."             | _She cannot **cope with** the pressure._ | *cope* always keeps *with*                                    |
