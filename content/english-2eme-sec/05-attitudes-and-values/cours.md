# ⚔️ Attitudes and Values — Money, Freedom and the Little Question at the End

> 💡 "What you do when nobody is counting is what you are."

Theme V asks the hardest question of the year: not what you **can** do, but what you are **willing** to do. Lesson 12 puts money on trial, lesson 13 gives the floor to freedom, lesson 14 follows somebody who was paid to lie. Three lessons, three weapons: the **linkers that name a result**, the **imperative**, and the **question tag**.

## 💰 Money on trial — the words of lesson 12

The **root** of something is where it starts, its origin. **Evil** is great wrongdoing — a noun (_money is not an evil in itself_) and an adjective (_an evil habit_). **Corruption** is the use of money or power to obtain what one has no right to; a person who does that is **corrupt**. **Tyranny** is the cruel and unjust use of power over people who cannot answer back. **Dignity** is the respect every human being is owed and the self-respect nobody can take from them without their consent. **Decent** means good enough and honest: _a decent salary_, _a decent flat_, _a decent man_. The **appeal** of something is the pull it has on you, the reason you want it. **Cheating** is breaking the rules in secret to gain something. **Revenge** is harm returned for harm received. **Anarchy** is the state of a place where no law is obeyed and nobody is in charge. A **calamity** is a disaster, a very great misfortune.

Three expressions of the lesson are worth as much as the words:

- **to make ends meet** = to earn just enough to pay for what you need, and nothing more.
- **right away** = immediately, at once. It has nothing to do with distance: _far away_ is distance, _right away_ is time.
- **money is a good servant and a bad master** = it is useful while you control it, dangerous the moment it controls you.

_Sami works two jobs in Sfax and still only **makes ends meet**. He was offered an envelope to close his eyes on a delivery and refused it **right away**: he said the money would buy a fridge and cost him his **dignity**._

> 🗡️ Many of these words travel in pairs, an adjective on one side and a noun on the other: _decent → decency_, _cruel → cruelty_, _superior → superiority_, _corrupt → corruption_, _worried → worry_. Learning the pair costs barely more than learning one of the two.

## 🗣️ Saying what you think — and telling it apart from what is true

Lesson 12 asks you to **express opinions** and to defend them. Opening an opinion is easy: _I think…_, _In my opinion…_, _I strongly believe…_, _I honestly feel…_.

What is not easy is seeing the border. A **fact** is something anybody can check — a date, a sum, a number, an event. An **opinion** is a judgement somebody else may refuse. _"Three shops in our street closed last year"_ is a fact; _"a rich man is never as free as he thinks"_ is an opinion. In a debate you may build on a fact, but the fact is not your point of view — and an examiner who asks for your opinion is not asking for a number.

## 🔗 Naming a result — the conjunctive adverbs

In Theme II you learnt to name the **cause** (_because_, _since_, _as_, _because of_) and to name the **result** with **so**. Here you look at the same link from the other end, and at a higher level of language: the **conjunctive adverbs** — the name the book itself gives them.

| Linker          | Relation           | Example                                                             |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **therefore**   | cause → result     | _It began to rain; **therefore**, I opened my umbrella._            |
| **as a result** | cause → result     | _It has rained heavily; **as a result**, many houses are flooded._  |
| **that's why**  | cause → result     | _It's Sunday; **that's why** most shops are closed._                |

### They are adverbs, not conjunctions — and that changes everything

**so** is a **conjunction**: it holds two clauses together all by itself, so a comma in front of it is enough. **therefore** and **as a result** are **adverbs**: they only *point back* at what was said before, they hold nothing together. The first clause must therefore be **closed** first — with a full stop or with a **semicolon** — and the adverb takes a **comma after it**.

| Pattern                          | How it is written                                                          |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| with the conjunction **so**      | _The line was cut**, so** we waited._ (comma before, nothing after)         |
| with **therefore / as a result** | _The line was cut**;** **therefore,** we waited._ (semicolon, then a comma) |
| with **therefore / as a result** | _The line was cut**.** **Therefore,** we waited._ (full stop, then a comma) |
| with **that's why**              | _The line was cut**.** **That's why** we waited._ (no comma after it)       |

> ⚠️ **The comma splice — the mistake that costs most marks here.** "The line was cut, therefore we waited." ✗ Two complete clauses separated by nothing but a comma. Your own language allows exactly this; English does not. Close the first clause: **;** or **.**

### Where the adverb may stand

An adverb moves; a conjunction never does. **therefore** and **as a result** have three legal slots, and one forbidden one.

- at the **head** of the clause: _**Therefore,** the coach turned back._
- between the **subject and the verb**: _The coach **therefore** turned back._
- between **two commas** around the subject: _The coach**, therefore,** turned back._
- ✗ **never** between the verb and what follows it: "The coach turned **therefore** back at Fernana." ✗ — that slot belongs to your own language, not to English.

**that's why** is the exception: it is a whole little clause (_that is why_), so it stays at the **head** of its sentence and moves nowhere.

### Which one, and where — the register

The three linkers say the same relation and do **not** live in the same places.

| Linker          | Where it belongs                                                                        |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **therefore**   | careful **written** English — a report, a composition, an examination paper, a formal letter |
| **as a result** | written English too, a little less formal; frequent in news writing                       |
| **that's why**  | **speech** and friendly writing; it carries a contraction, so it never fits a formal report |

_In her report Mouna wrote: "The workshop lost two of its three customers. **Therefore,** it closed in June." In the corridor she told her friend: "They lost two customers out of three — **that's why** they closed in June." Same fact, same link, two registers._

> 🗡️ **One link, one linker.** "Because the coach was ill, that's why the training was cancelled" ✗ names the same link twice. Choose one end and stay there.

## 🕊️ Songs of freedom — the words of lesson 13

Lesson 13 is a listening lesson built on a song, and its vocabulary is the vocabulary of a community that decides how to live together.

**freedom** = the state of being able to act, speak and think without being prevented · **rights** = what the law and justice allow you to claim · **duties** = what you owe in return, to others and to your country · **peace** = the state of a place where there is neither war nor violence · a **community** = a group of people who live in the same place or share the same life · **to forgive** = to stop holding against somebody the wrong they did you · **precious** = of very great value, and not only in money · **love** = the strongest of the words in this list, and the one the lesson sets against money.

> 🗡️ **rights** and **duties** are two halves of one idea. What you may claim on one side, what you owe on the other. A community that only counts the first half stops being one.

### Three sounds to keep apart

| Sound    | Heard in                          | The trap                                                             |
| -------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **/ʌ/**  | l**o**ve, **o**ther, m**o**ney, tr**ou**ble | the letter _o_ very often says /ʌ/, never the /o/ of your own language |
| **/aʊ/** | s**ou**nd, ar**ou**nd, d**ou**bt, pr**ou**d | a wide-open mouth that closes: two sounds run into one                |
| **/ʃ/**  | pre**c**ious, expre**ss**ion, ma**ch**ine, **s**ure | the same hushing sound written _c_, _ss_, _ch_ or _s_          |

Notice that _sound_ and _money_ are both written with an **o**, and share nothing at all.

## ❗ The imperative — giving an instruction

This is the form English uses to tell somebody to do something. It is the shortest sentence in the language, and the only one with **no subject**.

| What you want              | The form                       | Example                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| tell somebody to do it     | **base verb**                  | _**Close** the window._          |
| tell somebody not to do it | **don't + base verb**          | _**Don't close** the window._    |
| include yourself in it     | **let's + base verb**          | _**Let's close** the window._    |
| exclude yourself from it   | **let's not + base verb**      | _**Let's not** waste the time._  |

The subject is not written because it is understood: the person in front of you. Add **please** at the head or at the end to soften it (_**Please** sit down._ · _Sit down, **please**._), and **do** in front of the verb to insist (_**Do** be careful._).

**Four values, one form.** The words are the same; the situation decides.

- an **instruction**: _Take one tablet after each meal._
- a piece of **advice**: _Revise the irregular verbs first._
- a **prohibition**: _Don't leave your bag in the doorway._
- an **invitation or a suggestion** that includes the speaker: _Let's start again from the top._

**Who is included.** A bare imperative speaks to the others: _Arrive earlier tomorrow._ **let's** — short for _let us_ — puts the speaker inside the order: _Let's arrive earlier tomorrow._ One word, and the person giving the order has agreed to obey it too.

**Who you may say it to.** Between friends, from a teacher to a class, on a notice or in a recipe, the bare imperative is perfectly normal. To a stranger who owes you nothing it lands like an order, and English softens it into a question: _**Could you** keep this book for me until Friday, **please**?_ Half-way between the two, a tag makes an order friendly without turning it into a question: _Keep it for me, **will you**?_

> ⚠️ Four mistakes to bury. "You don't leave your bag here" ✗ is a **statement**, not an order — drop the subject: _Don't leave your bag here._ ✓ · "Not leave your bag here" ✗ forgets **don't**. · "Don't to leave your bag here" ✗ adds a _to_ that no imperative ever takes. · "Let's to start" ✗ and "Let's don't start" ✗ → _Let's start._ ✓ and _Let's not start._ ✓

## 🎯 The question tag — the little question at the end

A **question tag** is the short question stuck to the end of a statement to ask the listener to agree or disagree: _You are from Gafsa, **aren't you**?_ It belongs to **speech** far more than to writing.

**The rule in three moves.**

1. **The auxiliary of the statement comes back** in the tag — _be_, _have_, _will_, _would_, _can_, _should_, _must_. If the statement has **no auxiliary**, the tag borrows **do / does / did**: _The bank lent him the money, **didn't it**?_
2. **The polarity turns over**: positive statement → **negative** tag; negative statement → **positive** tag.
3. **The subject becomes a pronoun**: _Wassim and Rania have left, haven't **they**?_

| Statement                          | Tag                |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| The letter has come,               | **hasn't it?**     |
| The letter hasn't come,            | **has it?**        |
| You shouldn't sign that,           | **should you?**    |
| He isn't coming back,              | **is he?**         |

**The special cases the summary does not print — and the exam does ask.**

- **I am** → **aren't I**: _I'm late, **aren't I**?_ (there is no _amn't_ in English). But _I'm not late, **am I**?_
- an **imperative** → **will you**: _Close the door, **will you**?_ · a **let's** sentence → **shall we**: _Let's leave at six, **shall we**?_
- **there is / there are** keep **there**: _There's a problem, **isn't there**?_
- **everybody, somebody, nobody, no one** → **they**, even though the verb was singular: _Everybody has signed the form, **haven't they**?_
- **nothing, this, that** → **it**: _Nothing has changed, **has it**?_
- a statement carrying **never, hardly, nobody, nothing, seldom** is already **negative**, so its tag is **positive**: _He never writes, **does he**?_

**The voice decides what you are really doing.** Same words, two jobs:

- the voice **falls** at the end → you are sure, and you only want the other person to confirm: _You finished the report yesterday, didn't you?_ (↘)
- the voice **rises** at the end → you do not know, and you are really asking: _You finished the report yesterday, didn't you?_ (↗)

> ⚠️ **Answering a negative tag.** _"You didn't lock the door, did you?"_ — the door **is** locked. English answers the **fact**, never the shape of the question: _**Yes, I did.**_ ✓ Your own language answers the question here, and that is why "No, I locked it" ✗ comes out — an English ear hears the _no_ and understands that the door is open.

## 🧭 Judging a character — the words of lesson 14

Lesson 14 asks you to describe behaviour, and hands you sixteen adjectives to do it with. Sort them once, keep them for life.

**What we praise** — **honest** (tells the truth, does not cheat) · **truthful** (says what really happened) · **trustworthy** (can be trusted with what matters) · **reliable** (does what was promised, on time, every time) · **faithful** (stays loyal and never betrays) · **tolerant** (accepts people and ideas unlike their own) · **loving** · **correct** (behaves the way one should).

**What we blame** — **insincere** (says what they do not mean) · **jealous** (cannot bear that somebody else has what they want) · **suspicious** (believes without proof that others are doing wrong) · **deceitful** (lies and hides the truth on purpose) · **shameful** (so bad that it brings shame) · **indifferent** (does not care what happens to others) · **immoral** (against what is right) · **corrupt** (takes or gives money for what is not deserved).

And two words the reading of the lesson turns on: an **emergency** is a sudden dangerous situation that has to be dealt with right away, and **to talk somebody round** is to bring somebody who was refusing to say yes.

_Hedi promised his neighbour he would drive her to the clinic at seven. His own car would not start, so he walked to the taxi rank at half past six and was at her door at seven exactly. **Reliable** is the word — not kind, not tolerant: he did what he had said he would do._

## 💬 Holding your ground in a debate

Lesson 14 closes on a discussion, and gives you the three moves it needs.

| Move                    | What you say                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **give your opinion**   | _I think…_ · _In my opinion…_ · _I strongly believe…_ · _I honestly feel…_          |
| **disagree politely**   | _I don't think that…_ · _I'm afraid I don't agree._ · _You have a point, but…_ · _I see what you mean, but…_ |
| **give your reasons**   | _To start with…_ · _That's the reason why…_ · _For this reason…_ · _When you consider that…_ |

Notice the shape of a real disagreement: you never begin with the refusal. You grant something (_You have a point_), then you turn (_but_), then you explain (_To start with…_). Refusing without explaining is not a viewpoint; it is a wall.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                          | Right                                                    | Why                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "The line was cut, therefore we waited."       | _The line was cut**;** **therefore,** we waited._        | an adverb cannot join two clauses — close the first one       |
| "The coach turned therefore back."             | _The coach **therefore** turned back._                   | never between the verb and what follows it                    |
| "In my report: that's why it closed."          | _**Therefore,** it closed._                              | _that's why_ is spoken English, _therefore_ is written        |
| "You don't leave your bag here."               | _**Don't leave** your bag here._                         | an imperative carries no subject                              |
| "Let's to start again."                        | _**Let's start** again._                                 | nothing comes between _let's_ and the base verb               |
| "I'm late, amn't I?"                           | _I'm late, **aren't I**?_                                | English has no _amn't_                                        |
| "Everybody has signed, hasn't he?"             | _Everybody has signed, **haven't they**?_                | _everybody_ comes back as **they**                            |
| "You didn't lock it, did you?" — "No, I did."  | _**Yes, I did.**_                                        | yes and no answer the fact, not the question                  |

> 🏆 You can now name a result the way a written report does, punctuate it, move it, give an order and soften it, and hang the little question at the end of anything you say. Next quest: **Rights and Duties** — what a society owes the people who live in it.
