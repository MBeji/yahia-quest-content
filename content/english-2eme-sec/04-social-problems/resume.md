# 📜 Summary: Social Problems

- **The words of conflict and of peace** — a **conflict** (a serious disagreement) becomes a **fight**; on the way you meet an **insult**, **calling names**, **yelling**, **threatening** somebody, **hostility** (a lasting unfriendly feeling), **anger**, **hooliganism** and **crime**. Facing them: **peace**, **friendship**, **tolerance** (accepting what is not like you), **flexibility** (being ready to move your own position), **fair play**, a **compromise** (each side gives up something) and **to apologize**.
- **The two classes of nouns** — a **countable** noun can take a number in front of it (_one conflict, two conflicts_); an **uncountable** noun cannot ("two violences" ✗). Test any doubtful noun with **one**: if _one \_\_\_\__ is impossible, it is uncountable, and every other rule follows.
- **The six lines of the rule** — a countable noun has a singular and a plural form, takes a singular or a plural verb, takes **a / an / one** in the singular, **many** or **few** in the plural, **some** only in the plural, and **a number of** in the plural. An uncountable noun has one form only, takes a **singular verb** whatever it means, never takes _a / an / one_, takes **much**, takes **some** as it is, and takes **an amount of**. ⚠️ **a number of** counts heads, **an amount of** measures a mass: "a large number of work" ✗.
- **The question words, and _too_** — **how many** and **too many** with a countable plural, **how much** and **too much** with an uncountable.
- **The nouns that trap you** — **information · advice · news · furniture · equipment · luggage · homework · work · progress · knowledge · research · money · traffic · weather · damage · violence · behaviour · evidence** are uncountable in English: never **-s** ("informations" ✗, "advices" ✗), never **a / an** ("an advice" ✗), always a **singular verb** (_The news **is** good._ — yes, even ending in _-s_).
- **Counting an uncountable: the partitives** — name the unit first: **a piece of** news / advice / information / furniture, **an item of** clothing or equipment, **a bit of** luck, **a slice of** bread, **a glass of** water. _Three **pieces of advice**_ ✓, "three advices" ✗.
- **The same word, two lives** — _paper_ (the material) against _a paper_ (a newspaper, an exam paper); _glass_ against _a glass_; _work_ (activity) against _a work_ (one piece of art); _experience_ (what life teaches you) against _an experience_ (one event you lived). The column decides the meaning.
- **The words of the report** — **to estimate** (calculate approximately), **labour** (hard work), **manufacturing** (making goods industrially), **trade** (buying and selling), **to endure** (suffer over time), **deprivation** (not having what you need), **to hinder** (hold back), **survival**, **to contribute to**, **access to**, **to drop out** (leave school for good — not "be absent for a few days"); and for lesson 11, **siblings** (brothers and sisters), **consent** (agreement freely given), a **foster home**, **to come to a head** (reach the point where it can no longer go on).
- **Reading a table of figures** — read the **title** first; a **percentage** is a share of a total, not a number of people; read a row **across** for an increase or a decrease and a column **down** for the highest and the lowest; **na** means not available, so nothing at all can be concluded from that cell. A conclusion is a sentence the figures support — never an opinion about a country or a group.
- **Comparative or superlative?** — the **comparative** compares **two**, the **superlative** ranks **three or more**. Short adjectives take **-er / -est** (_fast, faster, the fastest_; _big, bigger, the biggest_; _easy, easier, the easiest_), adjectives of three syllables or more and past participles take **more / most** (_more important, the most important_), and two-syllable adjectives split: **-y** and **-er** endings take _-er / -est_, **-ful** and **-re** endings take _more / most_.
- **The six irregular pairs** — **good** → better → the best · **bad** → worse → the worst · **little** → less → the least · **much** and **many** → more → the most · **far** → farther / further → the farthest / the furthest. The superlative always keeps **the**, takes **in** before a place (_the largest town in the north_) and **of** before a number or a period (_the largest of the three_), and works on adverbs too (_the longest hours_). Never mark twice: "more better" ✗, "the most largest" ✗.
- **Causative verbs: make, let, have** — **make** somebody do something = **obligation** (_The coach made us run twice round the pitch._); **let** somebody do something = **permission** (_Her parents let her join the club._); **have** somebody do something = **request** or weak obligation, arranged with somebody whose job it is (_The head teacher had the caretaker open the hall._). After all three, the second verb is a **bare infinitive**, without _to_: "He made me to write it" ✗. ⚠️ **allow** means the same as _let_ and is built the other way: _They allowed him **to** leave_ ✓, _They **let** him leave_ ✓.
