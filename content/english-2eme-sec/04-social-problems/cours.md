# ⚔️ Social Problems — What You Can Count, What You Cannot, and Who Decides

> 💡 "A problem you can measure is a problem you can begin to answer."

Theme III took you travelling. Theme IV keeps you at home and asks harder questions: what turns a disagreement into violence (lesson 9), why children work instead of going to school (lesson 10), and what happens to children who grow up without their parents (lesson 11). The book treats all three **soberly**, with figures and facts rather than with strong words, and it expects you to do the same. Three tools carry the theme: **countable and uncountable nouns**, **the superlative**, and the **causative verbs**.

## 🕊️ The words of conflict and of peace

A **conflict** is a serious disagreement; a **fight** is what it becomes when hands get involved. Along the way you meet an **insult** (a word said to hurt), **calling names** (insulting somebody by what you call them), **yelling** (shouting angrily), **threatening** somebody (telling them you will hurt them), **hostility** (a lasting unfriendly feeling), **anger** (the feeling itself), **hooliganism** (organised violence, usually around a stadium) and **crime**.

On the other side stand **peace**, **friendship**, **tolerance** (accepting what is not like you), **flexibility** (being ready to move your own position), **fair play**, a **compromise** (an agreement where each side gives up something) and **to apologize** (to say you are sorry for what you did).

_When Hedi lost his temper in the corridor, Nizar did not answer the **insult**. He waited, then **apologized** for his own share of the quarrel and offered a **compromise**. **Flexibility** ended in one minute what **hostility** would have kept alive for a term._

> 🗡️ Sort every word of this theme into two columns as you learn it: **violent behaviour** on the left, **non-violent behaviour** on the right. The book opens the theme with exactly that task, and an examiner can ask for it in any form.

## 🔢 Countable and uncountable nouns

Here is the rule the book prints at the end of lesson 9. **All English nouns fall into two classes.** A **countable** noun is one you can put a number in front of: _one conflict, two conflicts, three conflicts_. An **uncountable** noun is one that no cardinal number can precede: not "one violence", not "two violences" — just **violence**.

The book gives the rule as six lines of contrast. Learn them as six, because each line is a different mistake.

| Countable nouns                                               | Uncountable nouns                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| have a singular **and** a plural form: _a joke / jokes_       | have **one** form only: _anger_                        |
| take a singular **or** a plural verb: _The joke **was** old._ | take a **singular** verb only: _Anger **is** useless._ |
| take **a**, **an** or **one** in the singular: _an insult_    | never take _a_, _an_ or _one_: "an anger" ✗            |
| take **many** or **few** in the plural: _few insults_         | take **much**: _much anger_                            |
| take **some** only in the plural: _some insults_              | take **some** as they are: _some anger_                |
| take **a number of** in the plural: _a number of insults_     | take **an amount of**: _a large amount of anger_       |

> ⚠️ The last line is the one examiners love. **A number of** counts heads or objects; **an amount of** measures a mass. _A large **number of** volunteers_ ✓ · _a large **amount of** work_ ✓ · "a large number of work" ✗.

### The question word follows the same split

**How many** goes with a countable plural (_How many leaflets did you print?_), **how much** with an uncountable (_How much time do we need?_). And after _too_, the same pair: _too **many** mistakes_, _too **much** noise_.

### The nouns that trap you

Your own language counts several nouns that English refuses to count. Learn this list; it is the same one every year.

**information · advice · news · furniture · equipment · luggage · homework · work · progress · knowledge · research · money · traffic · weather · damage · violence · behaviour · evidence**

- Never add **-s**: "informations" ✗, "advices" ✗, "furnitures" ✗, "homeworks" ✗.
- Never put **a** or **an** in front: "an information" ✗, "an advice" ✗.
- Always a **singular verb**: _The news **is** encouraging._ · _The equipment **was** donated._ Yes, _news_ ends in _-s_ and is still singular.

> 🗡️ Test any doubtful noun with **one**: if _one \_\_\_\__ sounds impossible, the noun is uncountable, and everything else follows from that.

### How to count an uncountable: the partitives

You can still count one item of a mass — you just have to name the unit first. This is what a **partitive** does.

- **a piece of** news · **a piece of** advice · **a piece of** information · **a piece of** furniture
- **an item of** clothing · **an item of** equipment · **a bit of** luck · **a slice of** bread
- **a glass of** water · **a bar of** soap · **a game of** chess

_Mouna gave me **three pieces of advice** before the debate._ ✓ — never "three advices".

### The same word, two lives

A few nouns sit in both columns, and the meaning changes with the column.

| Uncountable — the material or the idea | Countable — one object, one example      |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| _paper_ = the material                 | _a paper_ = a newspaper, or an exam paper |
| _glass_ = the material                 | _a glass_ = something you drink from      |
| _work_ = activity                      | _a work_ = one piece of art               |
| _experience_ = what life teaches you   | _an experience_ = one event you lived     |

So _She has **experience**_ (she has been doing it for years) and _She had **an experience**_ (one thing happened to her) are two different sentences. The column decides the meaning, not the other way round.

## 👷 Child labour — the words of the report

The reading text of lesson 10 is an institutional report, and its vocabulary is the vocabulary of every report you will ever be given.

**to estimate** = to calculate approximately, without an exact count · **labour** = work, especially hard physical work (a **labourer** is the person who does it) · **manufacturing** = making goods by an industrial process · **trade** = buying and selling · **to endure** = to suffer something painful over time · **deprivation** = the state of not having what you need · **to hinder** = to slow something down or hold it back · **survival** = staying alive · **to contribute (to)** = to help to cause or to help to provide · **access (to)** = the possibility of reaching or using something · **to drop out (of school)** = to leave school before the end.

And lesson 11 adds the words of a childhood without parents: **siblings** (your brothers and sisters), **consent** (agreement given freely), a **foster home** (a family or a house that takes in a child who cannot live with its parents), **to come to a head** (to reach the point where a situation can no longer go on).

> ⚠️ **to drop out** is not "to be absent for a few days". It means leaving school **for good**, usually because there is no other way.

## 📊 Reading a table of figures

Lesson 10 opens on a statistical table, and the exam does the same. Four reflexes.

1. **Read the title first.** It tells you what the figures count, and over which period. A figure without its title means nothing.
2. **A percentage is a share, not a number of people.** A region at 21 per cent has 21 of every 100 working children — not 21 children.
3. **Read a row across, and a column down.** Across the years, a row that grows shows an **increase**, a row that shrinks shows a **decrease**. Down a column, you find **the highest** and **the lowest** figure of that year.
4. **`na` means not available.** Where a cell says _na_, you cannot conclude anything at all about it — not even that the figure is small.

A conclusion is a sentence the **figures** support. _One region holds most of the working children, and another holds almost none_ is a conclusion: every word of it can be read off the table. A judgement on the families behind those figures is not a conclusion: no table has ever counted what a family feels, and blaming a whole population is not reading a table — it is accusing one. Say what the figures count, and stop there.

## 🥇 Comparing: the comparative and the superlative

The book's own definition: **the comparative shows a difference between two** things or groups, **the superlative shows a difference among three or more**. That is the first question to ask yourself — how many am I comparing?

| Adjective                                              | Comparative               | Superlative                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| one syllable: _young, tall, fast, few_                 | _younger_                 | _the youngest_                    |
| ending in _-y_ or _-er_: _pretty, easy, tender_        | _prettier_                | _the prettiest_                   |
| ending in _-ful_ or _-re_: _doubtful, mature_          | _more doubtful_           | _the most doubtful_               |
| three syllables or more: _important, expensive_        | _more important_          | _the most important_              |
| a past participle in _-ed_: _tired, worried_           | _more tired_              | _the most tired_                  |

Spelling, on the short ones: a single consonant after a single vowel doubles (_big → bigger → the biggest_), a final _-e_ takes only _-r_ and _-st_ (_safe → safer → the safest_), and a final _-y_ turns into _-i_ (_easy → easier → the easiest_).

Six pairs are **irregular**, and they are the ones the reading text uses.

| Adjective          | Comparative              | Superlative                          |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| **good**           | better                   | the best                             |
| **bad**            | worse                    | the worst                            |
| **little**         | less                     | the least                            |
| **much** (uncountable) | more                     | the most                             |
| **many** (countable)   | more                     | the most                             |
| **far**            | farther / further        | the farthest / the furthest          |

Three rules of use finish the point. The superlative always keeps **the** in front of it: _the fastest_, never "fastest" alone. It takes **in** before a place or a group seen as a place (_the largest town **in** the north_) and **of** before a number or a period (_the largest **of** the three_, _the best **of** all_). And it works on **adverbs** as well, where the word that carries the mark says how the action was done and never names a thing: _Of the three, Rania **worked the hardest**_ · _Nizar **explained the most clearly**_ — same forms, same _the_, and an adverb in _-ly_ always takes **the most**.

> ⚠️ Never mark the comparison twice: "more better" ✗, "the most largest" ✗. One mark is enough — either the ending or the word in front of it, never both.

## 🤝 Causative verbs: make, let, have

Lesson 11 ends on a table that names three intentions. Here it is, with the example sentence each line needs.

| Structure                       | What it expresses           | Example                                                 |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **make** somebody **do** something | **obligation** — no choice is left | _The coach **made** us **run** twice round the pitch._ |
| **let** somebody **do** something  | **permission** — the choice is granted | _Her parents **let** her **join** the drama club._ |
| **have** somebody **do** something | **request** or weak obligation — you arrange it with somebody whose job it is | _The head teacher **had** the caretaker **open** the hall._ |

The one form to remember: after _make_, _let_ and _have_, the second verb is a **bare infinitive** — the infinitive **without _to_**. _The nurse made him **sit** down._ ✓ · "The nurse made him to sit down." ✗

> 🗡️ **let** and **allow** mean the same thing and are built the opposite way: _They **let** him **leave** early._ ✓ · _They **allowed** him **to leave** early._ ✓ · "They allowed him leave early" ✗ · "They let him to leave early" ✗.

Two more details worth marks. _make_ + object + **adjective** exists too, and there the second word is not a verb at all: _The noise **made** him **angry**._ And _have_ in this frame is not the _have_ of possession: _I **have** a car_ says what you own, _I **had** a mechanic **check** the car_ says what you arranged.

## 🔊 One note on stress

The long nouns of this theme all end in **-tion** or **-sion**, and they all put the strong beat on the syllable **just before** that ending: _educ**a**tion_, _con**struc**tion_ — more precisely _edu-**ca**-tion_, _con-**struc**-tion_, _depri-**va**-tion_, _exploi-**ta**-tion_. Find the ending, step back one syllable, and you have the stress.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                    | Right                                          | Why                                                    |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| "She gave me two informations."          | _She gave me two **pieces of information**._   | _information_ is uncountable: no _-s_, no number       |
| "The news are good."                     | _The news **is** good._                        | an uncountable noun takes a singular verb              |
| "There were too much noise."             | _There **was** too much noise._                | _noise_ is uncountable, so the verb is singular        |
| "a large number of homework"             | _a large **amount of** homework_               | _number of_ counts, _amount of_ measures               |
| "He is the tallest of the two brothers." | _He is the **taller** of the two brothers._    | two → comparative; three or more → superlative         |
| "This is the more difficult exercise."   | _This is the **most** difficult exercise._     | _more_ compares two, _most_ ranks among several        |
| "It was the most bad result of the year."| _It was **the worst** result of the year._     | _bad_ is irregular: worse, the worst                   |
| "My father let me to drive his car."     | _My father **let** me **drive** his car._      | after _make_, _let_, _have_: no _to_                   |
| "She is the best pupil of the school."   | _She is the best pupil **in** the school._     | _in_ before a place, _of_ before a number or a period  |
