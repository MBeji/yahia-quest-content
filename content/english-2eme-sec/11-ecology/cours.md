## 📏 Adverbs of degree — how much, and whether it is enough

Four little words measure quantity or intensity, and each one takes a different position.

| Adverb | Degree | Position | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **quite** | moderate | **before** the adjective | *Your work is **quite** good.* |
| **very** | high | **before** the adjective | *The story is **very** amusing.* |
| **too** | excessive | **before** the adjective | *It's **too** cold to swim.* |
| **enough** | sufficient | **after** the adjective | *He's old **enough** to go to school.* |

> ⭐ **`enough` is the odd one out, and it is the one examiners test.** It follows the adjective — *old enough*, *warm enough*, *strong enough* — where all the others come first. "enough old" ✗.
> With a **noun**, however, `enough` goes **before**: *enough time*, *enough water*, *enough money*. Adjective → after; noun → before.

### `enough to` and `too … to` — the question of what is still possible

Two structures, and one decisive difference:

> *Sea levels will be **high enough to** cover coastal areas.* → covering them **is possible**.
> *The rate of burning is **too high to** be ignored.* → ignoring it **is not possible**.

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| adjective + **enough to** + verb | the quality is **sufficient**, so the action **happens** | *The case is light **enough to** carry.* |
| **too** + adjective + **to** + verb | the quality is **excessive**, so the action **cannot** happen | *The plate is **too** hot **to** touch.* |
| **not** + adjective + **enough to** + verb | the quality is **insufficient**, so the action **cannot** happen | *The ice isn't thick **enough to** walk on.* |

> ⭐ **How to transform a pair of sentences.** Take *He's very tall. He can see over the heads of other people.*
> Ask: does the action happen? **Yes** → use `enough to`: *He's tall **enough to** see over the heads of other people.*
> Take *The parcel is very heavy. We can't send it by post.* Does the action happen? **No** → use `too … to`: *The parcel is **too** heavy **to** send by post.*

> ⚠️ **The negative case is the one that catches everybody.** *The ice is not very thick. We can't walk on it.* The action does not happen, so you reach for `too` — but the sentence says the ice is **not thick**, not that it is too thick. The right answer keeps the negative: *not thick **enough to** walk on*. Ask which quality is **missing**, not only whether the action fails.

> ⚠️ After `too … to` and `enough to`, do **not** repeat the object: *too hot to touch* ✓, "too hot to touch **it**" ✗. The object is already the subject of the sentence.

## 💧 Water scarcity — and the paragraph that does not belong

Lesson 28 gives you four paragraphs in the wrong order and asks you to **cross out the one that does not belong**, then rearrange the rest. *(Source: 2003 Independent Digital (UK) Ltd.)*

| Paragraph | What it says | Its place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **B** | The gravest challenge of the century may be the **lack of fresh water**: only **2.5 %** of the earth's water is fresh, and what is available is under pressure **from several directions**. | **first** — it opens the subject and announces a list |
| **A** | **Population growth is the main cause**: more than six billion people by 2000 meant consumption almost doubled in half a century, and demand comes from industry and agriculture as well as from drinking and washing. | **second** — the first direction |
| **D** | "**All that's bad enough. But** what's increasing the stress still further" is **climate change**, likely to account for a fifth of the increase in scarcity. | **third** — it adds to what precedes |
| **C** | **Irrigation** has historically reduced poverty by providing food security and employment, and has been an engine of economic growth. | ⚠️ **crossed out** |

> ⭐ **How to see the order without being told it.** The connectors do the work. *Several directions* announces a list, so B opens. *The main cause* is the first item of that list, so A follows. *All that's bad enough. But… still further* can only come after something, so D closes.
>
> ⚠️ **And why C is the intruder is worth understanding.** C is not off-topic — it is about water. It is **off-argument**: the text explains a **shortage and its causes**, and C praises the **benefits of irrigation**. An intruder that shares the subject is far harder to spot than one that does not.

## ➕ `not only … but also`

> *Demand comes **not only** from the need to drink, to wash and to deal with waste, **but also** from industry and from agriculture.*

The relation is **addition**: two things are true, and the second is the more surprising of the pair.

**The rule of position**: put `not only` and `but also` in front of **the two elements that are being added**, and keep those two elements of the same kind.

| Original | With the structure |
| --- | --- |
| *He's deaf and unable to speak.* | *He's **not only** deaf **but also** unable to speak.* (two adjectives) |
| *He can speak English and German.* | *He can speak **not only** English **but also** German.* (two nouns) |
| *They smoke and drink.* | *They **not only** smoke **but also** drink.* (two verbs) |
| *He dislikes how we speak and disapproves of how we dress.* | *He **not only** dislikes how we speak **but also** disapproves of how we dress.* (two clauses) |

> ⚠️⚠️ **The agreement trap, and your book uses it twice without teaching it.** When the structure joins two **subjects**, the verb agrees with the **second** one:
> *Not only his father **but also his mother is** dead.* — not *are*.
> *Not only Peter **but also John is** a pop fan.* — not *are*.
> English looks at the noun **nearest** the verb, and that noun is the one after *but also*.

> ⚠️ **The structure joins two things, not three.** *They broke into his office, stole his books and tore up his manuscripts* has three actions; `not only … but also` has only two slots. Group two of them — *They **not only** broke into his office **but also** stole his books and tore up his manuscripts* — or use a different linker.

## 🎵 Comparing — the simile

Lesson 29 is built on a song, and the point of language it teaches is a **figure of speech**. Three of them are named in the exercise, and only one of them uses a word of comparison.

| Figure | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **a simile** | compares two things **explicitly**, using **like** or **as** | *Her voice is **like** a bell.* / *He is **as** quick **as** a cat.* |
| **a metaphor** | says one thing **is** another, with **no** word of comparison | *Her voice **is** a bell.* |
| **a personification** | gives human qualities to something that is not human | *The wind **whispered** through the trees.* |

> ⭐ **The test takes one second**: look for **like** or **as**. Present → simile. Absent, and one thing is called another → metaphor. Absent, and something not human is **acting** like a person → personification.

A structure repeated with *like* at the head of several lines is a chain of similes, and what it expresses is a **similarity** — not a difference, and not a contrast.

## ✅ Progress Check 3 — how a text is organised

The last checkpoint of the book revises the year, and four of its twelve exercises are about **organisation** rather than grammar. They are worth preparing, because they come back every year.

| Task | What it asks | How to do it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Write the topic sentence** | supply the missing first sentence of a paragraph | read what follows and find what it is all **about**; the paragraph usually refers back to it with *this* or *that* |
| **Insert the missing sentences** | put three sentences back where they belong | each one continues the idea just before it — read the sentence **before** the gap, not the one after |
| **Punctuate and capitalise** | restore full stops and capitals | find where one complete idea ends; every new sentence opens with a capital |
| **Develop hints into sentences** | turn telegraphic notes into a paragraph | add the articles, the auxiliaries and the linkers the notes left out, and keep the order given |

> ⭐ **The topic sentence is the one that pays.** In the exercise, the paragraph's second sentence is *Growth must be checked to avoid this catastrophe* — and the word **this** is the clue: the missing sentence has to name the catastrophe. A model answer runs: *The world's population will soon reach a level where there will not be enough resources to sustain life as we know it.*

Two more of its exercises revise the **sounds** and the **word families** of the year: sorting sixteen words by **stressed syllable**, and sorting twenty words under *education*, *employment*, *family* and *entertainment*.
