## 🌱 The words of a damaged planet

**Ecology** is the study of how living things and the place they live in depend on each other. The **environment** is that place itself: the land, the air and the water in which people, animals and plants live. Do not swap them: ecology is a science, the environment is a thing.

| Word                | Meaning                                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **global warming**  | the rise in the temperature of the earth's air, held in by certain gases        |
| **ozone depletion** | the slow using up of the ozone layer, which lets more of the sun's rays through |
| **thinning**        | becoming thinner — what the ozone layer does                                    |
| **a rain forest**   | a thick forest in a hot part of the world where a great deal of rain falls      |
| **deforestation**   | the cutting down of the trees of a whole forest                                 |
| **wildlife**        | the wild animals and birds of a place, taken together (never _a wildlife_)      |
| **fumes**           | the gases and smoke that come out of a factory or an engine (always plural)     |
| **to purify**       | to take out of water or air what should not be in it                            |
| **to ban**          | to forbid something by law                                                      |
| **to endanger**     | to put something at risk of disappearing                                        |
| **extinct**         | with **no** living member left anywhere                                         |
| **to trap**         | to hold something so that it cannot get out                                     |
| **damage**          | harm done to something (uncountable: _a lot of damage_, never _damages_)        |

> ⚠️ **Endangered is not extinct.** A species that still has sixty animals left is **endangered**; a species whose last animal died is **extinct**. The first can still be saved, the second cannot. One word, all the difference.

## 📏 Adverbs of degree — saying exactly how much

An **adverb of degree** says **how much** of a quality there is. It modifies an **adjective** or **another adverb**, and it stands **in front of** the word it modifies.

> The air is **quite** clean. → in front of the adjective _clean_.
> He drives **quite** carefully. → in front of the adverb _carefully_.

Your book gives you four of them, and each one names a different degree.

| Adverb     | Degree it names       | Example                                        |
| ---------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **quite**  | moderate degree       | _The result is **quite** good._                |
| **very**   | high degree           | _The river is **very** polluted._              |
| **too**    | excessive degree      | _The water is **too** hot._                    |
| **enough** | sufficient degree     | _The tank is deep **enough**._                 |

Three of them go **before**; **enough** is the exception. **Enough** stands **after** an adjective or an adverb, and **before** a noun.

> _The pipe is wide **enough**._ ✓ — "enough wide" ✗
> _We have **enough** water._ ✓ — the noun comes after.

Two more adverbs of degree appear in the grammar summary of your book — **fairly**, close to _quite_, and **extremely**, above _very_ — together with _almost_, _nearly_ and _just_.

> ⚠️ **too is not very.** _The report is **very** long_ states a fact. _The report is **too** long_ makes a complaint: it means **more than anybody wants**. Never write _too_ where you only mean a lot.

## 🚪 too + adjective + to, and adjective + enough + to

These two structures both end in an infinitive, and they are opposites.

> **too + adjective + to + verb** → the action **does not happen**.
> **adjective + enough + to + verb** → the action **does happen**.

| Sentence                                              | What really happened          |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| _The plastic was **too small to be picked up**._      | nobody picked it up           |
| _The pump was **light enough to be carried**._        | they carried it               |
| _The soil is **too dry to hold** the rain._           | it does not hold the rain     |
| _The lake is **shallow enough to walk** across._      | you can walk across it        |

When you name the person concerned, put **for** in front of that person, before the infinitive.

> _The article was **too technical for the pupils to follow**._ → the pupils did not follow it.
> _The path is **wide enough for a lorry to pass**._ → a lorry can pass.

**The same fact, with the opposite adjective.** Every _not ... enough_ sentence can be written with _too_ and the opposite adjective, and the two say **exactly** the same thing.

> _The ice is **not thick enough** to walk on._ = _The ice is **too thin** to walk on._
> _She is **not old enough** to vote._ = _She is **too young** to vote._

> ⚠️ **The negative is already inside too ... to.** _The tank is too deep to see the bottom_ ✓ — "too deep so that we cannot see the bottom" ✗ marks the negative twice. And the verb after _to_ is a **bare infinitive**: "too heavy to carrying it" ✗, "too heavy to be carried" ✓.

## 🔗 not only... but also — the rule your book leaves blank

This is the empty table, filled in.

| Structure                            | Meaning and function                                                          | Example                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **not only** ... **but also** ...    | **addition**, not opposition and not result. It adds a second fact and tells the reader that it counts as much as the first, often more. | _Demand comes **not only** from homes **but also** from industry._ |

They are a **pair of conjunctions**: neither half lives without the other. And they obey one law, which the grammar summary of your book states as a note:

> **The two members joined must be of the same grammatical kind.**

| Both members are...  | Example                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| two **verbs**        | _He **not only** returned the money **but also** paid the interest._     |
| two **nouns**        | _**Not only** the parents **but also** the children enjoyed it._         |
| two **adjectives**   | _The water was **not only** warm **but also** salty._                    |

> ⚠️ "He is not only clever but also he works hard" ✗ — an adjective on one side, a whole clause on the other. Write _He is **not only clever but also hard-working**._

**Two refinements the book uses but never states.**

1. **Inversion.** When the sentence **begins** with _Not only_, English puts the auxiliary **in front of** the subject, exactly as in a question. If there is no auxiliary, _do_, _does_ or _did_ appears.
   > _**Not only does** the plant clean the water, **but** it **also** produces electricity._
   > _**Not only did** they plant trees, **but** they **also** dug a well._
   > "Not only the plant cleans the water" ✗ — no inversion, no sentence.
2. **Agreement.** When the two members are two subjects, the verb agrees with the **nearer** one, the one after _but also_.
   > _Not only the pipes but also the **tank is** leaking._ ✓ (_the tank_ is nearer)
   > _Not only the tank but also the **pipes are** leaking._ ✓ (_the pipes_ is nearer)

**but also can split.** When a subject follows, _but_ and _also_ separate round it: _..., **but** the town **also** pays for it._

## 💧 The words of water, and the water of Tunisia

| Word              | Meaning                                                             |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **rainfall**      | the amount of rain that falls on a place in a given time            |
| **a drought**     | a long period during which little or no rain falls                  |
| **consumption**   | the amount of something that is used up                             |
| **a reduction**   | the fact of making an amount smaller                                |
| **to reverse**    | to make something go the opposite way                               |
| **a dam**         | a wall built across a river to hold the water back                  |
| **to drill**      | to make a deep hole in the ground, here to reach water              |
| **running water** | water that comes out of a tap, not water carried in a vessel        |
| **a hose**        | a long flexible tube that carries water                             |
| **to dump**       | to get rid of waste by leaving it somewhere                         |
| **a bill**        | the paper that says how much you must pay for what you have used    |
| **to account for**| to be a given part of a total — _farming **accounts for** 80 %_ = farming uses 80 % of it |

Tunisia is a country of low and irregular **rainfall**, and lesson 28 says so plainly: dams and mountain lakes hold the winter water back, and deep wells are **drilled** where the surface water is not enough. At home the arithmetic is just as simple: a **hose** left running on a car uses several times what a bucket uses, and a leaking tap that nobody reports raises the **bill** of a family that has done nothing else wrong. The lesson asks you two questions, and they are not the same question: what is being done, and what **you** can do.

## 🎵 Time for a song — simile and metaphor

Lesson 29 is a listening lesson built on a song, and it ends with a question about a figure of style. Your book offers you the three names — **a simile**, **a metaphor**, **a personification** — and defines none of them. Here they are.

| Figure              | What it does                                                  | Sign to look for      | Example                                          |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **a simile**        | says one thing is **like** another                            | **like** or **as ... as** | _The dry field was **like** a sheet of paper._ |
| **a metaphor**      | says one thing **is** the other                               | **no** _like_, **no** _as_ | _The river is the town's oldest road._        |
| **a personification** | gives a human action or feeling to a thing                  | a human verb          | _The wind **complained** all night._             |

The test is mechanical, and it takes two seconds. **Look for _like_ or _as ... as_.** If one of them is there, you are reading a **simile**: two things are held side by side and declared similar. If neither is there and the sentence still says that one thing **is** another, you are reading a **metaphor**: the comparison is not announced, it is stated as a fact.

> _Her voice was **as clear as** water._ → simile (the sign _as ... as_ is there).
> _Her voice was clear water._ → metaphor (no sign, and yet a comparison).

A metaphor is the stronger of the two, because it does not ask you to compare: it tells you the thing **is** what it is compared to, and leaves you to work out in what way.

**The words of music**, all from the list of your book: **lyrics** = the words that are sung (always plural) · **a melody** = the line of notes, the tune itself · **tunes** = melodies, in the plural · **a compact disc** = the round disc a recording is stored on · **to drown** = to die under water, and, of a sound, to cover another sound completely.

| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| "The pipe is enough wide."                  | _The pipe is wide **enough**._                    | _enough_ follows an adjective                        |
| "There is water enough for everybody."      | _There is **enough water** for everybody._        | _enough_ goes before a noun                          |

| "too cold so that we cannot swim"           | _**too cold to swim**_                            | _too ... to_ already carries the negative            |
| "The talk was too interesting."             | _The talk was **very** interesting._              | _too_ is a complaint, not praise                     |
| "Not only he came but also he sang."        | _**Not only did he come**, but he **also** sang._ | inversion after a sentence-initial _Not only_        |
| "not only a doctor but also he teaches"     | _**not only a doctor but also a teacher**_        | the two members must be the same kind of word        |
