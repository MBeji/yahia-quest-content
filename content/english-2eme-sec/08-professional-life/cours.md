## 🕰️ Looking back at a working life — `should have` and `would`

### `should have` + past participle: what ought to have happened, and did not

Patricia says of the childminder:

> *I felt she was having precious hours with him which **should have been** mine.*

The hours were not hers. The form says exactly that: **something ought to have been the case, and was not.** It is regret, and regret is what you feel about a past that cannot be changed.

> **should have / shouldn't have + past participle** = regret or reproach about the past.

You met this form in the previous chapter, and here it comes back **inside a text** instead of inside an exercise — which is where you will actually meet it.

### `would` + base verb: a habit that is over

Patricia says of her son:

> *He **would** make himself beans on toast and settle down to do his homework.*

This `would` is not a conditional, and it is not a future. It is the `would` of **repeated past habit**: something that happened again and again, over a period that is now closed.

| Form | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| *He **would** make himself beans on toast.* | a **habit**, repeated over years |
| *He **made** himself beans on toast.* | a **single** occasion, or simply a fact |
| *He **would** make himself beans on toast if he were hungry.* | a **conditional** — the *if* changes everything |

> ⚠️ The test is the presence of an *if*, or of a period of time. No *if*, and a stretch of past time in the background → habit. This is the same meaning `used to` carries in Lesson 22, and the two are close enough that your book teaches them four pages apart on purpose.

## 🎯 Purpose — the three ways to say *why*

Lesson 21 is built on one question: **why do you want that job?** English has three ways to answer, and your book prints them in a `GRAMMAR SUMMARY` box on page 117.

| Structure | Followed by | Example from the box |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **to** + verb | a **verb** in the base form | *They went into the fields **to pick up** mushrooms.* |
| **in order to** + verb | a **verb** in the base form | *She is having evening classes **in order to have** better qualifications.* |
| **so that** + clause | a **whole clause** with a modal | *She turned down the radio **so that she could** concentrate.* |

The modals the box allows after `so that`: **can · could · shall · should · may · might · will · would**.

> ⭐ **The one rule that decides between them.** `to` and `in order to` are followed by a **verb**; `so that` is followed by a **subject and a verb** — a complete clause. So you must ask: *is the person who acts the same in both halves of my sentence?*
> Same person → *I came to Japan **to learn** Japanese.*
> Different people → *The assistant does these little things **so that the doctor does not have** to do them.*
> The assistant acts, the doctor benefits: two subjects, so `so that` is the only one that works.

`in order to` is simply a more formal, more explicit `to`. Both are correct; `in order to` is what you use when the sentence is long and you want the reader to see the purpose coming.

> ⚠️ Two mistakes cost marks every year. **"for to learn"** ✗ — English never puts *for* before *to* + verb. And **"so that to learn"** ✗ — `so that` needs a subject after it, always.

## 🔊 The final **-s**, and the three sounds it makes

An English word ending in **-s** is not always pronounced the same way. There are exactly three possibilities, and the rule is mechanical.

| Sound | When | Words from your lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **/ɪz/** | after a hissing sound (s, z, sh, ch, ge, x) | *stitches*, *wishes* |
| **/s/** | after a voiceless consonant (p, t, k, f, th) | *check-ups*, *streets* |
| **/z/** | after a vowel or a voiced consonant | *affairs*, *gives*, *schools*, *goes* |

> ⭐ **How to find it without learning any list.** Say the word without the *-s* and stop. If your throat vibrates on the last sound, add **/z/**. If it does not vibrate, add **/s/**. If the last sound is already a hiss, you cannot add either without a vowel in between, so English adds **/ɪz/** — that is the whole reason the third sound exists.
>
> ⚠️ *stitches* and *streets* both end in a group with **t**, but it is the **ch** of *stitches* that decides: a hiss forces **/ɪz/**.

## 🎧 Two students, two dream jobs

Lesson 21 is a listening. Two Norwegian students are interviewed on an English-speaking radio station in Japan, in a daily fifteen-minute programme called *Meet the people*.

**Heidrun** wants to be a **physician's assistant**. She explains what one does: the small acts of a doctor's office — **check-ups**, taking out **stitches** — *so that the doctor does not have to do them*. The training is "a lot of schooling": a minimum of **four to six years** of college, **two** of them in a hospital as an **intern**. Afterwards she hopes to have her own small **clinic**, or to work alongside a doctor.

**Thorson** wants to be a **diplomat**, preferably in Japan. That is why he came: to learn Japanese and, he hopes, to obtain a master's degree in **International Relations**. After that, the diplomat school of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs — a **three-year** programme — and then a return to Japan *in order to work for* the Norwegian **Embassy** in Tokyo. It is **very competitive**, but knowing a language few people speak gives an edge: only three hundred Norwegians live in Japan. And as a child? He had no dream job of the fireman-or-policeman kind. He wanted to be a **baseball player**, *so that he could* be famous the world over.

> ⭐ Notice how much of that paragraph is **purpose**. Every ambition in the lesson is expressed with `to`, `in order to` or `so that` — which is exactly why the lesson chose these two speakers.

### The words of qualifications

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **a degree** | the qualification a university gives at the end of a course |
| **a physician** | a doctor of medicine — *not* a physicist |
| **surgery** | the treatment of illness by cutting; also the room where a doctor sees patients |
| **a check-up** | a general medical examination, done to make sure all is well |

> ⚠️ **a physician** is a *doctor*; a **physicist** is a scientist who studies physics. The two words look alike and mean nothing alike — your book uses that resemblance as a trap in the listening.

## 🎼 Emma Hutchinson — `used to` and the past that has closed

Lesson 22 is the life of **Emma Hutchinson**, 36, who teaches music to children and is **partially deaf**. Born with the condition, she wears a **hearing aid** in each ear. As a small girl she loved the piano and the triangle; her mother, worn out by the noise, booked her into piano lessons. She was **hooked** at once, even though getting the right **pitch** was sometimes hard — and she refused to use her deafness as an excuse. At **eleven** she won a **scholarship** to music school. She now plays the French horn, the violin, the piano and the trumpet, and passed the examinations *so that she could* teach as well. She worked with **drama** and dance companies; at **thirty**, helping a friend organise a children's concert, she found it so rewarding that she **set up** her own company, *The Music House for Children*. At **thirty-six** she employs **thirty** teachers and they teach nearly **three hundred** children across London — and she works with deaf children in a special school, which is what makes it all **worthwhile**.

*(Emma Hutchinson, in* Woman's Own*, adapted.)*

### `used to` + base verb

> *I **used to work** with drama and dance companies.*
> *…that little deaf girl who **used to bang** the piano so tunelessly.*

**`used to` + base verb** says two things at once: it was true **repeatedly or for a long time in the past**, and it is **not true now**. The second half is what makes the form worth having — you cannot get it from the simple past.

| Sentence | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| *She **used to play** the triangle.* | she did, over a period — and she does not any more |
| *She **played** the triangle at the concert.* | one occasion; nothing is said about now |
| *She **plays** the triangle.* | she still does |

**The forms**:

| | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| affirmative | **used to** + base verb | *Camels **used to be** the only form of transport.* |
| negative | **didn't use to** + base verb | *Modern trains **didn't use to** exist.* |
| question | **did … use to** + base verb | *Did people **use to** travel by camel?* |

> ⚠️⚠️ **The spelling trap.** In the affirmative it is **used** with a *d*; after **did**, the *d* disappears — **didn't use to**, **did you use to**. This is the same rule as any other verb after *did*, and it is the most common written mistake of the whole chapter.

> ⚠️⚠️ **`used to` and `be used to` are two different structures, and the difference is total.**
>
> | Structure | Followed by | Meaning |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **used to** + **base verb** | *used to **work*** | a past habit that has **stopped** |
> | **be used to** + **noun or -ing** | *am used to **working*** | being **accustomed** to something, now |
>
> *I **used to work** at night* = I did, and I no longer do.
> *I **am used to working** at night* = I do it, and it no longer troubles me.
> The first has no *be* and no *-ing*; the second always has both. Patricia's text uses only the first — but the second is one syllable away, and it is the commonest confusion of the whole year.

> ⚠️ **Do not use `used to` for everything past.** Your book's own exercise makes the point: *Traders sometimes **put** together camel trains of 15,000 animals* and *Each animal often **carried** 400 pounds* are ordinary past simples — the habit form is not needed once the sentence already says *sometimes* or *often*. And after a modal, no tense at all: *it could **travel** twenty miles a day*.

### The words of the story

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **partially** | in part, not completely |
| **deafness** | the condition of not being able to hear |
| **a hearing aid** | a small device worn in the ear to make sounds louder |
| **fed up** | tired and bored with something |
| **hooked** | very keen, unable to stop |
| **pitch** | how high or low a musical note is |
| **a scholarship** | money given to a student so that he can study |
| **drama** | theatre, as an art and as a subject |
| **to set up** | to found, to establish |
| **worthwhile** | important or rewarding enough to be worth the effort |
| **to light up** | to become bright and lively |

> ⚠️ Emma is **partially** deaf, not profoundly deaf — she hears with a hearing aid, and the whole point of her story is that she learnt to find the pitch anyway. A question about her that says "profoundly deaf" is testing whether you read the first paragraph.

## 🗂️ Career Research Project — Step 1: your ideal job

Your book starts here a project that will run to the end of the year. Step 1 asks for a **narrative report** describing the job you would want, and it lists what the report must contain:

- what you would do **all day**;
- the kind of **work environment** — indoors, outdoors, or both;
- working **with people** or **alone**;
- working with your **hands**, your **head**, or both;
- the **earnings potential**;
- the **qualities** needed to succeed in that career;
- how you **know** you could do this kind of work.

> ⭐ The last two are the ones that make the difference. Anybody can describe a job; describing the qualities it needs, and then arguing that you have them, is what turns a description into a report.
