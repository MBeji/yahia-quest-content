## 💼 The words of a working life

**The place and the people.** A **clerk** is an employee who does office work — filing, records, counters. The **management** is the group of people who run the company, and _to manage_ is what they do. A **physician** is a doctor of medicine; a **physician's assistant** is trained to carry out some of the doctor's routine tasks under his supervision. A **diplomat** represents his country abroad. A **clinic** is a small medical centre; **surgery** is the treatment in which a doctor opens the body to repair it; **stitches** are the thread that closes a wound afterwards; a **check-up** is the routine examination you have when nothing is wrong.

**What you need before you are hired.** A **degree** is the qualification a university gives you at the end of your studies. **Training** is the period in which you are taught to do the job itself. An **internship** is a period spent inside a company to learn the work, usually short and often unpaid. A **scholarship** is money given to a student so that studying costs the family nothing.

**Money.** To **earn** is to receive money for work you have done — a salary is earned, a prize is won. A **profit** is what is left when the money coming in is larger than the money going out; a **deficit** is the opposite, and a company that turns a deficit into a profit has done exactly what this theme calls a success story.

**What work does to people.** Being **self-sufficient** means managing without help from anybody. A job may be **competitive**, that is, one many people want and few obtain. And you may be **hooked** on it (caught by it, unable to leave it alone), **fed up with** it (tired and bored of it), or find it **worthwhile** — important enough to be worth the trouble.

> 🗡️ **The preposition, and what comes after it.** _fed up_ takes **with** and nothing else: "fed up of the same tasks" ✗ → _fed up **with** the same tasks_ ✓. And once a preposition is there, the verb behind it goes into the **-ing** form — this is true of every preposition in English, not only of this one: _fed up **with waiting**_, _good **at drawing**_, _tired **of asking**_, _instead **of leaving**_. An infinitive after a preposition is always wrong: "fed up with to wait" ✗.

**The feelings a working life leaves behind.** Lesson 20 asks you to name them, so learn the four words together. You feel **guilty** when you believe you have let somebody down; **relieved** when a worry lifts and the thing you feared does not happen; **envious** when somebody else has what you wanted for yourself; **proud** when the work is your own and it turned out well. And you are **fond of** a person, a place or a thing you have grown to like over time — _he is fond of the old workshop_ — which is not the same as being **hooked on** something you cannot leave alone.

**The verbs of a life story.** To **pick somebody up** = to collect them by car or on foot. To **look after somebody** = to take care of them. To **settle down** = to begin a stable, quiet life in one place. To **set up** a company = to found it. To **drop out** = to leave school or a course before the end. To **light up** = to become bright, said of a face.

> 🗡️ **Where the object goes.** _pick up_ and _set up_ come in two pieces, and the two pieces can be pulled apart. With a **noun** you may put it on either side: _pick up my sister_ ✓ · _pick my sister up_ ✓. With a **pronoun** there is only one place — **between** the two pieces: _pick **her** up_ ✓ · "pick up her" ✗ · _set **it** up_ ✓ · "set up it" ✗. That is why the lesson prints the verb as _pick **somebody** up_, with the person written inside it. _look after_ works the other way: _after_ is a preposition and not a particle, so nothing is ever pulled apart — _look after **him**_ ✓ · "look him after" ✗.

> ⚠️ **The word that betrays you.** In your own language, the two months you spend inside a company to learn the job are a _stage_. English does not use that word for it: the word is an **internship**. In English a **stage** is a step in a process, or the platform an actor stands on. Two other pairs cost marks every year: you **earn** money and **win** a match; you **do** an internship and **take** a course.

## ⏳ Used to — the habit that is over

The book underlines two forms in the reading text of lesson 22 and then leaves the rule to you. Here it is, as the appendix states it.

**Form: `used to` + the base verb.** Nothing agrees with anything: _I used to_, _she used to_, _they used to_ — the same three words for every subject, and a bare infinitive after them.

**Meaning.** The appendix gives **used to** two values.

1. **A habit that has stopped.** _My grandmother **used to weave** carpets in Kairouan._ She does not weave any more.
2. **A past routine that has not necessarily stopped.** _When we worked at the port, the drivers **used to eat** at eleven._ They may well still do.

The first value is the one the exam wants, and it is why the form is so useful: **used to** describes the past and, in the same breath, says something about the present — that this is over. _There **used to be** a cinema at the end of our street_ tells you there is none now.

> 🗡️ Set it against the tense of Theme I. The present perfect says _it began then and it is still true now_; **used to** says _it was true then and it is not true now_. Two forms, two opposite messages, and both of them are about the present.

**Negative and question.** Both are built with **did**, and after _did_ the **-d** disappears: _She **didn't use to** work at night._ · _**Did** he **use to** live in Gafsa?_ Writing "didn't used to" is the commonest mistake there is. A second negative exists without _did_: _He **never used to** complain._

**Against the past simple.** A single event that happened once, on a named day, takes the **past simple** and never _used to_: _She **signed** the contract in 2019._ ✓ · "She used to sign the contract in 2019." ✗ To use _used to_, you need a habit or a lasting state, not one dated fact.

**Against would.** For a **repeated action** of the past you may also use **would**: _Every August my uncle **would drive** us to Tabarka before dawn._ But _would_ works only for actions, and only once the past frame is already set. For a **state** — being, having, liking, living — only _used to_ is possible: _He **used to be** a fisherman._ ✓ · "He would be a fisherman." ✗

**Against the present.** _used to_ and _usually_ are two different words. _I **used to** walk to school_ is finished; _I **usually** walk to school_ is my habit today.

> ⚠️ **Be used to** is another structure altogether, and the appendix warns you against the confusion. **be used to + a noun or an -ing form** means _this is no longer strange to me_: _I **am used to** the noise of the workshop._ · _She **is not used to working** at weekends._ It has a subject that agrees (_am, is, are, was_), it exists in every tense, and the verb after it ends in **-ing**. Compare in one line: _I **used to work** in a hotel_ (I no longer do) · _I **am used to working** in a hotel_ (it does not tire me).

## 🎯 Saying what you are aiming at

Lesson 21 prints three structures side by side and asks whether they express a purpose, a request or a deduction. They express a **purpose** — an aim, the answer to _what for?_ Here are the three, with what decides between them.

| Structure | When you use it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **to** + base verb | the shortest form; the aim belongs to the subject of the sentence | _Wassim gets up at five **to finish** his revision._ |
| **in order to** + base verb | the same thing, more formal; usual in a written report or notice | _The centre printed the leaflet in three languages **in order to reach** every family._ |
| **so that** + a clause | when the aim belongs to **somebody else**, or when the aim needs a whole clause | _The teacher spoke slowly **so that** the new pupils **could** follow._ |

**The rule that decides.** The appendix says it plainly: a clause with **so that** is **necessary when the person the purpose refers to is named** instead of merely being understood. Read your sentence and ask _who is meant to do the second action?_ If it is the subject already there, _to_ or _in order to_ are enough. If it is somebody else, an infinitive cannot carry that person, and only **so that** can: _She turned the radio down **so that** her brother could revise._ ✓ · "She turned the radio down to her brother revise." ✗

**One exception worth knowing**: when the main verb already has a personal object, the infinitive attaches to that object and not to the subject. _The head teacher sent two pupils **to collect** the certificates_ — the pupils collect them, not the head teacher.

**The modal inside so that.** A purpose clause almost always carries **will / would** or **can / could**, and the choice follows the tense of the main verb: **can** and **will** after a present, a present perfect or a future; **could** and **would** after a past. Inside a pair both are open to you, and they do not say quite the same thing: **could** means _would be able to_, and **would** is simply the future seen from the past. After a past main verb, therefore, _so that he **could** hear_ and _so that he **would** hear_ are both correct English; what is never correct is a present modal after a past.

- _I am writing the address down **so that** you **can** find the office._ (present → can)
- _They wrote the notice in large letters **so that** everybody **could** read it._ (past → could)

> ⚠️ **A purpose is not a consequence.** They look alike and they are opposites in time. A **purpose** is what you wanted **before** you acted, and nothing says it worked. A **consequence** is what actually happened **after**. _He left at six **so that** he would not miss the train_ = his aim. _He left at six, **so** he did not miss the train_ = the result. One word of difference, and the pupil who writes _so that_ for a result has told the reader that the train was an intention.
>
> 🗡️ Two forms your own language pushes you towards, and English refuses: "**for** to finish", "**for** finishing" as an aim ✗ — the aim of an action is **to** + base verb. And _so that_ is followed by a **subject and a verb**, never by an infinitive: "so that to help him" ✗ → _**so that** I **could** help him_ ✓.

## 😔 Should have — looking back at what was not done

Last theme's modals gave advice about what is still possible. This one turns them round and points them at what is finished.

**Form: `should have` + the past participle**, for every subject, and never anything else after _have_: _I should have **written**_ ✓ · "I should have wrote" ✗ · "I should had written" ✗ · "I should have go" ✗.

**Meaning** — the appendix calls it a **duty of the past that was not carried out**, a sensible action that was not performed, and it gives the structure two tones.

- **Regret**, when you are speaking about yourself: _I **should have applied** in March._ (I did not, and I am sorry.)
- **Reproach**, when you are speaking about somebody else: _You **should have told** the manager._ (You did not, and I blame you for it.)

**The negative reverses the facts.** **should have** says the thing was **not** done and ought to have been; **shouldn't have** says it **was** done and ought not to have been. _I **should have kept** the receipt_ = I threw it away. _I **shouldn't have kept** the receipt_ = I kept it, and it was a mistake. Read the situation first, then choose the polarity; a wrong polarity says the opposite of what happened.

**Against the present form.** _You **should send** your application_ is advice, and there is still time. _You **should have sent** your application_ is a verdict, and the deadline has passed. The past participle is what closes the door.

> 🗡️ The appendix gives **should have** a second value, far from regret: an **assumption** about what is probably true by now. _The van left Sfax at six; it **should have arrived** by now._ Nobody is being blamed here — the speaker is calculating. What tells the two apart is the context: a regret looks at a choice that was made, an assumption looks at a timetable.

## 🗣️ Asking somebody what they intend to do

Lesson 21 is a listening lesson, and what it teaches you to do is **enquire about an intention** — to ask, not to state. Four questions do the whole job: _What do you **want to become**?_ · _What are you **going to do** after the bac?_ · _What do you **intend to** study?_ · _Are you **planning to** take the entrance exam?_ And two follow-ups, because a good interviewer never stops at the first answer: _Why that job in particular?_ · _What makes you think you are fit for it?_

## 📄 Reading a job profile

The listening of lesson 21 is a job profile, and it answers four questions in order. Ask them of any advertisement you are given.

1. **What does the person actually do all day?** Not the title — the tasks.
2. **What training does it take, and how long?** A number of years is a fact; check it before you conclude anything.
3. **Where is the work done?** A hospital, a clinic, an office, a home, outdoors.
4. **What does it ask of the candidate?** Read the conditions one by one. An advertisement that lists two conditions and joins them with **and** rejects the candidate who meets only one; joined with **or**, one is enough. Read that little word before you read anything else.

## 🔊 The final -s, and the three sounds it makes

Lesson 21 asks you to sort words by the sound of their ending and then says: work out the rule with your teacher. The rule is short and it covers plurals and verbs alike.

- After a **hissing** ending (_-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -ge_), the ending is a whole extra syllable, /ɪz/: _stitches_, _wishes_, _stages_.
- After any other **voiceless** sound (_p, t, k, f_), it is /s/: _check-ups_, _streets_.
- After a **voiced** sound — every vowel, and _b, d, g, v, m, n, l, r_ — it is /z/: _schools_, _gives_, _goes_, _affairs_.

Say the last sound of the word before the _-s_, and the _-s_ will follow it: silent after silent, voiced after voiced, and a new syllable only after a hiss. Your book prints these same three sounds in ordinary capitals, as [ S ], [ Z ] and [ IZ ].

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "She didn't used to work here." | _She **didn't use to** work here._ | after _did_, the _-d_ goes |
| "I used to going to the club." | _I **used to go** to the club._ | _used to_ takes the base verb |
| "I used to work here since 2019." | _I **have worked** here since 2019._ | _since_ + still true = present perfect, not a finished habit |
| "He would be very shy as a child." | _He **used to be** very shy as a child._ | _would_ never carries a past state |
| "I am used to work at night." | _I **am used to working** at night._ | _be used to_ + _-ing_; another structure entirely |
| "I am fed up of doing the same tasks." | _I am **fed up with doing** the same tasks._ | _fed up_ keeps **with**; and after any preposition the verb takes **-ing** |
| "She picked up him at the station." | _She **picked him up** at the station._ | a pronoun sits **between** the two pieces of the verb |
| "He came for to see the manager." | _He came **to see** the manager._ | an aim is _to_ + base verb, never _for to_ |
| "They lowered the price so that to sell more." | _They lowered the price **so that** they **could** sell more._ | _so that_ needs a subject and a modal |
| "She saved money so that she can buy the van." (last year) | _…**so that** she **could** buy the van._ | the modal follows the tense of the main verb |
| "I should have wrote to them." | _I **should have written** to them._ | after _have_, always the past participle |
| "You should have not signed it." | _You **shouldn't have** signed it._ | the negative sits on _should_, not on _have_ |
