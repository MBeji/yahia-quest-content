## 🎯 Saying what you want — _I want to_ and _I'd like to_

A wish, in English, is built with **a verb of wanting + to + a base verb**. That little **to** is not decoration: it is the joint that holds the two verbs together.

| Structure                  | Example                                     | What it sounds like           |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **I want to** + base verb  | _I **want to** study medicine._             | direct, plain, a real wish    |
| **I'd like to** + base verb| _I **'d like to** work with children._      | softer, more polite           |
| **I want** + noun          | _I **want** a quiet room to revise in._     | you want a thing, not an act  |
| **I'd like** + noun        | _I **'d like** a word with you, please._    | a polite request              |

**I'd like** is the short form of **I would like**. Use it whenever you are asking somebody — a parent, a teacher, an employer — for something. _I want a scholarship_ is not rude, but _I'd like to apply for a scholarship_ opens far more doors.

> 🗡️ Other ways to say the same thing: _I **hope to** become an engineer._ · _I **am willing to** work hard for it._ · _My dream **is to** open my own workshop._ Notice that all of them keep **to** + base verb.

> ⚠️ Three errors this structure collects every year. "I want become a nurse." ✗ — the **to** is missing. "I'd like becoming a nurse." ✗ — after _would like_ the verb never takes _-ing_. And _I **like** to read_ ✓ is not a wish at all: it describes a **habit**, something you enjoy doing regularly. _I **would like** to read_ is the wish.

## 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Talking about your family — the words you need

Families come in many shapes, and English has a precise word for each link.

| Word                     | Who it names                                                    |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **siblings**             | your brothers and sisters, taken together                        |
| **stepmother / stepmum** | the wife of your father, when she is not your own mother         |
| **stepfather / stepdad** | the husband of your mother, when he is not your own father       |
| **stepson / stepdaughter** | the child your husband or wife already had                     |
| **mother-in-law**        | the mother of your husband or of your wife                       |
| **grandchild**           | the child of your child                                          |
| **Nan**                  | the affectionate name many children give their grandmother       |

> ⚠️ **stepmother** and **mother-in-law** are two different people. Many languages cover both with one single word; English does not, and mixing them is the classic mistake of this lesson. Your father's new wife is your **stepmother**. Your husband's mother is your **mother-in-law**.

And these are the words the lesson uses for **what a family gives you** — you will need them to speak about your own:

**security** (the feeling that nothing bad can reach you) · **protection** · **support** (help that keeps you going) · **guidance** (advice that shows you the way) · **care** (attention given with love) · **appreciation** (letting somebody know you value them) · **trouble** (difficulty, a bad situation) · **a path** (the way you choose for your life) · **to be willing to** (to be ready and glad to) · **to accomplish** (to reach a goal) · **to comment on** (to give your opinion about).

_My parents give me **security** and **guidance**; my friends give me **support** when I am in **trouble**. I am **willing to** work hard to **accomplish** what I want, and I have already chosen my **path**._

## ⏪ Narrating past events — the past simple and the irregular verbs

To tell what happened, English uses the **past simple**. With regular verbs you add **-ed** (_walk → walked_). With irregular verbs you have no choice: you learn the three forms by heart, because you need the **third** one — the **past participle** — for the whole rest of this chapter.

| Infinitive | Past form | Past participle |
| ---------- | --------- | --------------- |
| become     | became    | become          |
| feel       | felt      | felt            |
| grow       | grew      | grown           |
| hold       | held      | held            |
| keep       | kept      | kept            |
| know       | knew      | known           |
| make       | made      | made            |
| say        | said      | said            |
| stride     | strode    | stridden        |

Read the table in **columns**, not in lines: some verbs keep the same form twice (_keep – kept – kept_), some change every time (_grow – grew – grown_), and a few come back to the start (_become – became – become_).

> ⚠️ Never invent a regular form for an irregular verb: "growed" ✗, "sayed" ✗, "knowed" ✗. And never swap the last two columns: it is _know – **knew** – **known**_, not "know – known – knew".

## 🔗 The present perfect — the tense that reaches the present

Start with a sentence, the way the lesson does.

> Salma left Testour in 2018. **She has lived in Sfax since then.**

Ask yourself the three questions of the lesson. **Which tense is used?** A form you have not named yet. **What does it express?** Something that began in the past — 2018 — and is **still true now**: she is in Sfax at this very moment. **What is the special indicator?** The word **since**.

That tense is the **present perfect**, and here is its rule.

**Form: have / has + past participle.**

| Subject             | Affirmative            | Negative                    | Question                  |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| I / you / we / they | _I **have** worked_    | _I **have not** worked_     | _**Have** you worked?_    |
| he / she / it       | _she **has** worked_   | _she **has not** worked_    | _**Has** she worked?_     |

In speech everything shrinks: _I**'ve** worked_, _she**'s** worked_, _I **haven't** worked_, _she **hasn't** worked_. Short answers keep the auxiliary alone: _Have you told your Nan? — **Yes, I have.** / **No, I haven't.**_

> ⚠️ After **have** or **has**, the verb is **always** a past participle — never the base form, never the past form: _She has **written**_ ✓ · "She has write" ✗ · "She has wrote" ✗.

## 🧭 What the present perfect expresses, and how to spot it

The present perfect is a bridge: one foot in the past, one foot in **now**. It is used for three things.

1. **A situation that started in the past and is still going on.** _My uncle **has kept** the same shop since he was twenty._
2. **An experience in your life up to now**, without saying when. _We **have never** been to Djerba._ · _**Have** you **ever** met your cousins from Nabeul?_
3. **A past event whose result matters now.** _I **have lost** my keys._ (So I cannot get in — now.)

Its markers are the fastest way to recognise it.

| Marker                 | What it introduces                  | Example                                            |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **since**              | the **starting point**              | _since 2018_, _since Monday_, _since she left school_ |
| **for**                | the **length of time**              | _for six years_, _for two weeks_                     |
| **yet**                | not up to now — negatives, questions | _The bus **hasn't** arrived **yet**._                |
| **already**            | sooner than expected — affirmative  | _Mehdi **has already** finished._                    |
| **ever / never**       | experience, once or not once        | _I **have never** driven a car._                     |
| **just**               | a moment ago                        | _My sister **has just** phoned._                     |
| **so far / up to now** | the total counted until today       | _She **has written** three articles **so far**._     |

Two more places where the present perfect is compulsory, and where nobody expects it:

- **It's the first time / the second time…** _It's the first time I **have travelled** alone._
- **This is the best… I have ever…** _This is the best film I **have ever** seen._

> ⚠️ **since** takes a **point**, **for** takes a **duration**. _since 2020_ ✓ · "since four years" ✗ · _for four years_ ✓. The test is simple: if you can answer _when did it start?_, use **since**; if you can answer _how long?_, use **for**.

## ⚖️ Present perfect or past simple? — the one decision that matters

This is the heart of the chapter, and it comes down to a single question: **is the moment named and over?**

- **Yes → past simple.** _yesterday_, _last winter_, _in 2015_, _two months ago_, _when he was a child_, _on Sunday_ — all of them close the door on the present.
- **No, or it still reaches today → present perfect.**

| Past simple — the moment is named and finished | Present perfect — the moment is not named, or it reaches now |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _Aymen **bought** that bike in 2019._           | _Aymen **has had** that bike since 2019._                     |
| _I **saw** her at the wedding last month._      | _I **haven't seen** her since the wedding._                   |
| _They **moved** to Mahdia three years ago._     | _They **have lived** in Mahdia for three years._              |
| _My grandfather **worked** here for forty years._ (he has stopped) | _My grandfather **has worked** here for forty years._ (he is still there) |

Look hard at the last line. **for** goes with **both** tenses — so **for** decides nothing. Only the **tense** tells you whether the story is over or still running. That is why you cannot answer these questions by hunting for a key word alone: you have to ask what the sentence means.

> 🗡️ A very common pattern puts the two tenses side by side, one sentence each: _Ines **opened** her workshop in 2016. Since then she **has trained** eleven apprentices._ The first sentence dates the start; the second counts what has happened up to today.

## 🎭 Describing a person — the adjectives of the lesson

When you speak about the people in your family, you need to judge them fairly. Here are the six adjectives of the lesson, three warm and three cold.

| Adjective        | It describes somebody who…                                     |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **kind**         | is good to others and does them a favour without being asked     |
| **caring**       | pays attention to what others need and looks after them          |
| **affectionate** | shows love openly — a hug, a word, a smile                       |
| **proud**        | is very pleased with what they, or somebody close, have achieved |
| **arrogant**     | thinks they are better than everybody around them                |
| **selfish**      | thinks only of themselves and shares nothing                     |

**proud** is the double-edged one. _My mother is **proud of** my results_ is warm. _He is too **proud** to ask for help_ is cold. The preposition and the situation decide.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                              | Right                                   | Why                                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "I have seen her yesterday."       | _I **saw** her yesterday._              | _yesterday_ is a named, finished moment → past simple        |
| "She lives here since 2018."       | _She **has lived** here since 2018._    | a situation that started then and still lasts → present perfect |
| "I know him since three years."    | _I **have known** him for three years._ | **for** takes a duration, and the tense must be present perfect |
| "They have finish the work."       | _They **have finished** the work._      | after _have_, always a past participle                        |
| "Have you already eat?"            | _**Have** you **already eaten**?_       | same rule inside a question                                   |
| "It's the first time I see snow."  | _It's the first time I **have seen** snow._ | the frame _It's the first time…_ takes the present perfect |
| "I want be a teacher."             | _I **want to be** a teacher._           | a wish keeps **to** + base verb                               |

_Yesterday I have finished my project_ is the single most frequent sentence in second-year copies — and it is wrong twice over: wrong tense, and a marker that forbids it.
