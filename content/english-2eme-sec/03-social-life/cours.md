## 🧳 The kinds of journey — four words English refuses to mix

French covers a lot of ground with one or two words. English cuts the same ground into pieces, and each piece has its own name.

| Word             | What it names                                                             | Example                                              |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **a flight**     | a journey **by plane**                                                     | _Our **flight** to Rome takes two hours._            |
| **a voyage**     | a **long** journey made **by boat or ship**                                | _The **voyage** to Marseille lasted a whole night._  |
| **an excursion** | a **short** journey made **for pleasure**, usually by several people together | _The school organised an **excursion** to Dougga._ |
| **a trip**       | any short journey there and back, for pleasure or for work                 | _a business **trip**_, _a day **trip**_               |
| **travel**       | the **general idea** of going from one place to another, especially far    | _**Travel** broadens the mind._                       |
| **a journey**    | the movement itself, from the moment you leave to the moment you arrive    | _a three-hour **journey** by coach_                   |

> ⚠️ **travel** is almost always **uncountable**: _I love **travel**_ ✓ / "I made a travel to Sousse" ✗ → _I made a **trip** to Sousse._ And you do not "make" a journey with _do_: you **make** a journey, you **take** a trip, you **go on** an excursion, you **catch** a flight.

And the words you meet on the paper the agency hands you:

| Word              | Meaning                                                              |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a brochure**    | a small booklet, with photos and prices, that advertises a place      |
| **a leaflet**     | a single printed sheet, often folded, handed out free                 |
| **galleries**     | the long rooms of a museum where the collections are displayed        |
| **jewellery**     | rings, bracelets, necklaces — the ornaments, taken all together       |
| **to broaden**    | to make wider; _to broaden your mind_ = to open it to new things      |
| **to amaze**      | to surprise somebody so much that they cannot react                   |

_At the agency Sana took a **leaflet**, then a whole **brochure**; the photos of the **galleries** and of the Berber silver **jewellery** **amazed** her._

## 🗺️ Saying what is possible — and giving advice

A leaflet never orders you about. It tells you what you **can** do, and it **suggests**.

**Possibility** — the door is open, nobody forces you through it.

| Structure                  | Example                                                     |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **can / can't** + base verb| _You **can** sail, windsurf and canoe at the same centre._   |
| **could** + base verb      | _You **could** spend the morning in the medina._             |
| **may / might** + base verb| _The museum **may** be closed on Mondays._                    |
| **It is possible to** …    | _**It is possible to** hire a bicycle on the spot._           |

**Advice** — you tell somebody what would be good for them.

| Structure                      | Example                                                   |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You should** + base verb     | _You **should** book your flight early._                   |
| **Why not** + base verb **?**  | _**Why not** drop in at the water sports centre?_          |
| **How about** + verb**-ing ?** | _**How about** taking the night train?_                    |
| **If I were you, I would** …   | _**If I were you, I'd** start with the old town._          |
| **It is worth** + verb**-ing** | _The climb **is worth** doing at sunrise._                  |

> ⚠️ Three shapes that collect mistakes every year. "Why not to take the bus?" ✗ → _Why not **take** the bus?_ (no _to_ after _why not_). "How about to visit the souk?" ✗ → _How about **visiting** the souk?_ (_-ing_ after _about_). "You should to book" ✗ → _You **should book**._ (never _to_ after a modal).

## ➖ *either* — the word that carries a second negative

Start with a sentence, the way the lesson does.

> Getting close to the players has never been easy. It has never been cheap **either**.

Ask the three questions. **Where is *either*?** At the very **end** of the sentence. **Is the sentence affirmative or negative?** **Negative** — _has never been_. **What is it doing there?** It adds a **second negative fact** to the first one.

That is the whole rule, and it has a twin.

| The sentence is…  | you add a second fact with… | Example                                                            |
| ----------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **affirmative**   | **too** (at the end)        | _Hedi enjoys long journeys. Emna enjoys them **too**._               |
| **negative**      | **either** (at the end)     | _Hedi doesn't enjoy flying. Emna doesn't enjoy it **either**._       |

**too** and **either** are the same word doing the same job on the two sides of the mirror. If the sentence has _not_, _never_, _no_ or _nobody_ in it, you need **either**.

In a **short reply**, English changes tools again — and this time the subject and the auxiliary swap places:

| The other person says…            | you agree with…                       |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| _I love the sea._ (affirmative)   | _**So do I.**_ · _I do **too**._       |
| _I have been to Rome._            | _**So have I.**_ · _I have **too**._   |
| _I don't like crowds._ (negative) | _**Neither do I.**_ · _I don't **either**._ |
| _I have never flown._             | _**Neither have I.**_ · _I haven't **either**._ |

> 🗡️ **Rewriting with *either*** is a classic exam task. Three shapes turn up again and again:
>
> - _Both Sana **and** Mehdi were absent._ → _Sana was absent, and Mehdi was absent **too**._ (affirmative → **too**)
> - _**Neither** Sana **nor** Mehdi came._ → _Sana didn't come, and Mehdi didn't come **either**._
> - _The old man has **no** home and **no** family._ → _The old man hasn't got a home, and he hasn't got a family **either**._

> ⚠️ **Four traps.** "I don't like it **too**." ✗ — _too_ never lives in a negative sentence. "I don't like it **neither**." ✗ — one negative per clause is enough; _neither_ already carries its own. "**Either** I don't like it." ✗ — in this use _either_ stands at the **end**, never at the front. "**Neither I do.**" ✗ — after _neither_, the auxiliary jumps in front of the subject: _**Neither do I**._

## 🎤 The interview — asking about satisfaction, and answering

An interviewer's job is to make somebody say how they feel. There is a small set of questions for that, and a smaller set of answers.

| Asking about satisfaction                | Expressing satisfaction              | Expressing dissatisfaction               |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| _How do you feel **about** your season?_ | _I'm **delighted with** it._          | _I'm **not satisfied with** it._          |
| _Are you **happy with** the result?_     | _I'm very **pleased with** it._       | _I'm **disappointed with** the result._   |
| _Are you **satisfied with** the club?_   | _I'm **grateful for** their welcome._ | _I'm **fed up with** the travelling._     |
| _What do you **think of** the coach?_    | _I couldn't be happier._              | _It could have been better._              |

> ⚠️ The prepositions are not free, and they are the first thing a corrector looks at: **satisfied with**, **happy with**, **pleased with**, **disappointed with**, **fed up with**, **think of**, but **grateful for** a thing and **grateful to** a person. And the trap of the year: **to look forward to** is followed by a **noun** or a **verb in -ing**, never by an infinitive — _I'm looking forward **to meeting** my teammates_ ✓ · "I'm looking forward to meet them" ✗.

The words the interview is made of:

| Word                | Meaning                                                            |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a league**        | the competition in which the clubs of one country play each other   |
| **teammates**       | the players of your own side                                        |
| **a coach**         | the person who trains the team and picks it                          |
| **a career**        | the working life of a person, taken as a whole                       |
| **to cheer**        | to shout to encourage a player or a team                             |
| **to miss (somebody)** | to feel sad because that person is far away                       |
| **to look forward to** | to wait for something pleasant with impatience                    |
| **grateful**        | feeling that you want to thank somebody                              |
| **eager**           | impatient and full of desire to do something                         |

## ⏳ The present perfect progressive — the tense that measures a duration

Two sentences, side by side. Read them slowly.

> **I have signed** the contract.
> **I have been playing** here **for** twelve years.

The first one gives you a **result**: the signature exists, the deal is done. The second one does something else entirely: it takes an activity, stretches it over twelve years, and tells you it is **still going on**. That second tense is the **present perfect progressive**.

**Form: have / has + been + verb-ing.**

| Subject             | Affirmative                | Negative                          | Question                        |
| ------------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| I / you / we / they | _I **have been** training_ | _I **have not been** training_    | _**Have** you **been** training?_ |
| he / she / it       | _he **has been** training_ | _he **has not been** training_    | _**Has** he **been** training?_   |

In speech it shrinks: _I**'ve been** training_, _he**'s been** training_, _I **haven't been** training_. Note that **been** never changes and never disappears: it is the hinge of the whole structure.

> ⚠️ Three broken forms, all three heard every week. "I have playing since 2019." ✗ — _been_ is missing. "I have been play since 2019." ✗ — the _-ing_ is missing. "I am playing here since 2019." ✗ — a present continuous cannot reach back to 2019; only a **perfect** tense can.

## 🧭 What it expresses — and *since*, *for*, *How long…?*

The present perfect progressive says three things at once: the activity **started** in the past, it has gone on **without stopping**, and it is **still going on now** (or it has only just stopped, and you can see the result).

_Wassim **has been revising** since seven o'clock._ → he started at seven, he has not left his desk, he is still there.

Its two markers are the ones you already know, and they divide the work between them:

| Marker    | What follows it                      | Examples                                                        |
| --------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **since** | the **starting point**               | _since 2019_ · _since Monday_ · _since he signed the contract_   |
| **for**   | the **length of time**               | _for twelve years_ · _for three months_ · _for a long time_      |

**since** also accepts a whole clause behind it, and that clause goes into the **past simple**: _I have been waiting **since the bus left**._

And the question that goes with all this is one single question:

> **How long have you been** learning English? — _I have been learning it **for** seven years._
> **How long has** your brother **been working** in Sfax? — _He has been working there **since** last October._

> ⚠️ **since** takes a **point**, **for** takes a **length**. "since seven years" ✗ → _for seven years_ ✓ · "for last October" ✗ → _since last October_ ✓. And in a question, _How long_ is enough on its own: "How long time have you been waiting?" ✗ → _**How long** have you been waiting?_ ✓

## ⚖️ Progressive or simple? — how long against how many

Both tenses are present perfects, both reach the present. The difference is what you are pointing at.

| Present perfect **progressive** — the **activity** and its **length** | Present perfect **simple** — the **result** and its **number** |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _She **has been writing** letters all afternoon._                       | _She **has written** four letters._                              |
| _They **have been building** that stadium for two years._               | _They **have built** three stadiums in the region._              |
| _I **have been reading** your brochure._ (I am still on it)             | _I **have read** your brochure._ (I have finished it)            |
| answers **How long…?**                                                  | answers **How many…?** / **What have you done?**                 |

The test takes one second: if the sentence carries a **number of finished things**, use the **simple**; if it carries a **duration**, use the **progressive**.

> 🗡️ The two very often sit in the same sentence, one clause each: _Sami **has been driving** the Sfax to Gabes coach for nine years, and he **has carried** thousands of passengers._ The duration takes the progressive, the count takes the simple.

**One family of verbs refuses the progressive**: the verbs that describe a **state**, not an activity — _be, have_ (= possess), _know, like, love, want, believe, belong, understand_. With them, the simple does the job of both.

_I **have known** the coach since 2018._ ✓ · "I have been knowing the coach since 2018." ✗
_They **have had** that shop for ten years._ ✓ · "They have been having that shop" ✗

> ⚠️ And the mistake that survives from last year: a present perfect, simple or progressive, **never takes a date**. "I have been living here in 2020" ✗ → _I **moved** here in 2020_ ✓ (past simple) or _I **have been living** here **since** 2020_ ✓.

## 🔊 Which syllable do you hit? — word stress

Every English word of more than one syllable has **one** strong syllable. Hit the wrong one and the word disappears, even if every sound is right. Cut the word into syllables first, then find the beat.

| Word            | Syllables       | The beat falls on |
| --------------- | --------------- | ------------------ |
| **career**      | ca-reer         | the **second**: ca-**REER** |
| **excursion**   | ex-cur-sion     | the **second**: ex-**CUR**-sion |
| **museum**      | mu-se-um        | the **second**: mu-**SE**-um |
| **teammates**   | team-mates      | the **first**: **TEAM**-mates |
| **grateful**    | grate-ful       | the **first**: **GRATE**-ful |
| **eager**       | ea-ger          | the **first**: **EA**-ger |
| **opportunity** | op-por-tu-ni-ty | the **third**: op-por-**TU**-ni-ty |
| **expectations**| ex-pec-ta-tions | the **third**: ex-pec-**TA**-tions |

Four rules cover most of the work:

1. Most **two-syllable nouns and adjectives** take the beat on the **first** syllable: _**GRATE**-ful_, _**EA**-ger_, _**JOUR**-ney_. _ca-**REER**_ is a famous exception.
2. Words ending in **-tion / -sion / -cial** take the beat on the syllable **just before** the ending: _ex-**CUR**-sion_, _in-for-**MA**-tion_.
3. Words ending in **-ity** take the beat **two syllables before** the ending: _op-por-**TU**-ni-ty_, _pos-si-**BI**-li-ty_.
4. **Compound nouns** are stressed on their **first** part: _**TEAM**-mates_, _**FOOT**-ball_, _**TIME**-table_.

> ⚠️ Endings like **-ful**, **-ly**, **-ness**, **-er** and **-s** never move the beat: _**GRATE**-ful_ → _**GRATE**-ful-ly_. And do not cut English words the French way: _excursion_ is **three** beats in English (ex-cur-sion), not four.

## 🧩 Progress Check 1 — three reflexes to bring back

The checkpoint at the end of the theme does not test travel or football: it looks back over the whole first half of the year. Three reflexes carry most of the marks.

**Word formation.** You are given a word in brackets and you must fit it to its place in the sentence. Ask what **job** the word has to do there.

| Base word | as a verb    | as a noun       | as an adjective | as an adverb   |
| --------- | ------------ | --------------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| broad     | to broaden   | breadth         | broad           | broadly         |
| secure    | to secure    | security        | secure          | securely        |
| person    | —            | person          | personal        | personally      |
| know      | to know      | knowledge       | known           | —               |
| meet      | to meet      | a meeting       | —               | —               |

After a **modal** (_can, should, will_) you need a **base verb**; after _the_, _my_, _your_ you need a **noun**; before a noun you need an **adjective**.

**Punctuation and capitals.** A capital opens every sentence; proper nouns (Tabarka, Emna, English, March) keep theirs wherever they stand; the pronoun **I** is always a capital; a comma closes an opening clause; and a question mark that belongs to quoted words stays **inside** the quotation marks.

**Linkers.** Choose them by the **relation**, not by the sound: **and, moreover, in addition** join two ideas that go the same way; **but, however, although, yet** turn the sentence round; **so, therefore, that is why** draw a consequence; **because, since, due to** give the reason.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                                     | Right                                          | Why                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "I don't like flying too."                | _I don't like flying **either**._              | a negative sentence takes _either_, never _too_             |
| "Neither I do."                           | _**Neither do I**._                            | after _neither_ the auxiliary comes before the subject      |
| "I have been playing since twelve years." | _…**for** twelve years._                       | _since_ takes a point, _for_ takes a length                 |
| "I have playing since June."              | _I **have been** playing since June._          | the progressive needs _been_ between _have_ and the _-ing_  |
| "I am waiting since ten o'clock."         | _I **have been waiting** since ten o'clock._   | a present continuous cannot reach back into the past        |
| "I have been knowing him for years."      | _I **have known** him for years._              | state verbs refuse the progressive                          |
| "I'm looking forward to meet you."        | _I'm looking forward **to meeting** you._      | _look forward to_ + noun or _-ing_                           |
| "How long time have you been here?"       | _**How long** have you been here?_             | _how long_ is complete on its own                            |
| "I made a travel to Sfax."                | _I made a **trip** to Sfax._                   | _travel_ is uncountable and general                          |

_I am waiting for the results since Monday_ is the single most frequent sentence in second-year copies — wrong tense, right marker, and the marker is what makes the tense visible.
