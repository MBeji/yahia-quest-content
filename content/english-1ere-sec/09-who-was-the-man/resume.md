# 📜 Summary: Who Was the Man?

- **The many jobs of "what"** — the frame decides the function: **What about** + noun or -ing = a **suggestion**; **What if** + sentence = **fear, apprehension**; **What a/an** + noun **!** = **surprise, appreciation**; **What do you mean…?** = asking for **clarification**; **What's the … for …?** = asking for **information**; **What** + subject + verb + **is** = **insisting** on one point; **Is this what you…?** = inquiring about **satisfaction**.
- **Three grammatical roles behind the seven frames** — **interrogative word** (a question mark, a fact expected), **exclamation word** (an exclamation mark, nothing asked), and **"the thing that"** inside a sentence, recognised by the test: replace it with _the thing that_ and the sense holds (_What we need now is a good map._). The frames _What about…?_ and _What if…?_ carry a question mark without asking for a fact.
- **The story, in order** — at half past ten the narrator kisses her husband **Donald** goodnight at his rehabilitation centre; on the empty road home a car **overtakes** her, **brakes** and stays behind her; she slows, it slows, she locks the doors and watches its **headlights** in her **rear-view mirror**; she telephones her son **Kingston** and her daughter-in-law **Samantha** answers "He isn't home"; then a voice on her own telephone says "Hurry up, I'm right behind you and if you keep driving this slowly we'll never get home" — the two of them are driving to the **same address**.
- **The genitive** — the owner comes first: one owner → noun + **'s** (_the driver's window_); a plural already in -s → noun + **'** (_the drivers' windows_); a plural without -s → noun + **'s** (_the children's books_); a name ending in -s → noun + **'** (_Mr Jones' car_). The apostrophe is not the plural: _my sisters arrived_ ≠ _my sister's phone_ ≠ _my sisters' phone_. Family links: your son's wife is your **daughter-in-law**, your daughter's husband your **son-in-law**, and for them you are the **father-in-law** or the **mother-in-law**.
- **The tense that carries a story** — the **simple past**, because a narration lines up finished events; the present simple would describe a routine and will would predict. Give the infinitive back for the irregular ones: said → to say, drove → to drive, kept → to keep, rang → to ring, left → to leave.
- **The words of the road** — **a journey**, **a breakdown**, **the headlights**, **the rear-view mirror**, **the steering wheel**, **to overtake** (to pass a car and go in front), **to brake** (to slow down or stop), **to grip** (to hold very tightly), **thumping** (a heart beating hard and fast), **relieved** (the feeling when a fear turns out to be for nothing).
- **Naming the feeling** — the narrator writes what her body does and you name it: **nervous · uncomfortable · worried · very surprised · frightened** or **terrified**, and **relieved** at the end. _The hairs on my neck stood up._ → frightened. _I shifted in my seat._ → uncomfortable.
- **Reading a story with your pen** — true or false always **with a quotation** from the text; events numbered as they **happen**, not as they are mentioned; and inference kept to what the words actually **prove**.
- **The trap wall** — _my **sister's** bag_, never "the bag of my sister" nor "my sisters bag"; _the **children's** books_, since children is a plural without -s; the exclamation frame takes nothing extra (_**What a** nice view!_); and you overtake a car but you press a brake.
