# ⚔️ Who Was the Man? — One Road, One Apostrophe, One Long Fear

> 💡 "A story is only frightening while you still do not know who is behind you."

Half past ten at night. A woman leaves a rehabilitation centre, kisses her husband goodnight and drives home alone. A car catches her up, overtakes her, brakes — and stays behind her, however slowly she drives. This lesson uses that story to teach you three things at once: the many jobs of the word **what**, the apostrophe that says **who owns what**, and the tense that carries a story from beginning to end.

## 🏰 The many jobs of "what"

The same three letters open a question, an exclamation and a whole clause. What decides is the **frame** the word sits in.

| The frame                        | Its function                    | Example                                    |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **What about** + noun or -ing    | making a suggestion             | _What about a walk after the lesson?_      |
| **What if** + sentence           | expressing fear or apprehension | _What if it rains before the match?_       |
| **What a/an** + noun **!**       | expressing surprise, appreciation | _What a long journey!_                    |
| **What do you mean…?**           | asking for clarification        | _What do you mean by that word?_           |
| **What's the … for …?**          | asking for information          | _What's the word for that part of a car?_  |
| **What** + subject + verb + **is** | insisting on one point        | _What we need now is a good map._          |
| **Is this what you…?**           | inquiring about satisfaction    | _Is this what you were looking for?_       |

## ⚡ Three grammatical roles for the same word

Behind the seven frames there are only **three** roles. Test each sentence with the trick in the last column.

| Role                     | What it does                       | The test                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **interrogative word**   | opens a question, answer expected  | the sentence ends with a question mark and asks for a fact  |
| **exclamation word**     | shows surprise or admiration       | the sentence ends with an exclamation mark, nothing is asked |
| **"the thing that"**     | replaces a noun inside a sentence  | you can say _the thing that_ in its place and keep the sense |

_What we need now is a good map._ → _**The thing that** we need now is a good map._ ✓ — so here **what** is neither a question word nor an exclamation.

> 🗡️ Careful with the two fixed frames: **What about…?** and **What if…?** both end with a question mark, but neither asks for a fact. One offers an idea, the other fears one.

## 🌙 The story, in the order it happens

1. At half past ten the narrator gathers her belongings and kisses her husband **Donald** goodnight; he is in a rehabilitation centre after a serious illness.
2. She drives home along an empty road. A car comes up behind her, **overtakes** her, then **brakes** and settles behind her again.
3. She slows down; the other car slows down too and will not pass. She locks the doors and watches its **headlights** in her **rear-view mirror**.
4. She telephones her son **Kingston**. It is her daughter-in-law **Samantha** who answers: "He isn't home."
5. Her telephone rings. A voice says: "Hurry up, I'm right behind you and if you keep driving this slowly we'll never get home."

That last line changes everything: the two of them are driving to the **same address**. The fear was real; the danger was not.

## 🔮 The genitive — the apostrophe that says who owns

English does not say "the car of my brother". It puts the **owner first** and marks him with an apostrophe.

| Owner                            | Form              | Example                     |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------- | --------------------------- |
| one person or thing              | noun + **'s**     | _the driver**'s** window_   |
| several, plural already in **-s** | noun + **'**      | _the driver**s'** windows_  |
| several, plural **without** -s   | noun + **'s**     | _the child**ren's** books_  |
| a name ending in -s              | noun + **'**      | _Mr Jones**'** car_         |

> ⚠️ The apostrophe is not the plural. _My sisters arrived._ (they are several) · _My sister**'s** phone is broken._ (she owns the phone) · _My sister**s'** phone is broken._ (several sisters share one phone). Move the apostrophe and you change the number of people in the story.

The genitive also names family. Learn the four links the story uses: your son's wife is your **daughter-in-law**, your daughter's husband is your **son-in-law**, and for them you are the **father-in-law** or the **mother-in-law**.

## 🗡️ The tense that carries a story

Read the verbs of the text and one tense wins by a long way: the **simple past**. That is not a taste, it is a function — a narration lines up finished events, one after the other, and the simple past is the tense of the finished event. A text in the present simple would be describing a routine; a text with will would be predicting.

Many of those past forms are **irregular**, so you must be able to give the infinitive back:

| In the text | Infinitive |
| ----------- | ---------- |
| said        | to say     |
| drove       | to drive   |
| kept        | to keep    |
| rang        | to ring    |
| left        | to leave   |

## 📖 The words of the road

| Word                     | Meaning                                                   |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **a journey**            | the trip from one place to another                        |
| **a breakdown**          | the moment a car stops working                            |
| **the headlights**       | the two big lamps at the front of a car                   |
| **the rear-view mirror** | the mirror that shows the driver what is behind her       |
| **the steering wheel**   | the wheel the driver holds to choose the direction        |
| **to overtake**          | to pass another car and go in front of it                 |
| **to brake**             | to slow the car down or stop it                           |
| **to grip**              | to hold something very tightly                            |
| **thumping**             | beating hard and fast, said of a heart                    |
| **relieved**             | the feeling you have when a fear turns out to be for nothing |

## 😰 Naming the feeling

A narrator rarely writes "I was afraid". She writes what her body did, and you name the feeling: **nervous · uncomfortable · worried · very surprised · frightened** or **terrified**, and at the end **relieved**.

- _The hairs on my neck stood up._ → she is **frightened**.
- _I shifted in my seat._ → she is **uncomfortable**, not yet afraid.

## 📋 Reading a story with your pen

Three tasks come back in every text of this kind.

- **True or false, with a justification.** Never answer with the bare word: copy the words of the text that prove you right. A statement about the time of day is settled by the sentence that gives the time, never by the impression the scene leaves on you.
- **Putting the events in order.** Number them as they happen in the story, not as the text mentions them.
- **Guessing what is not written.** The text does not say who is driving behind her; it lets one sentence say it for you. Read what the words **prove**, and stop there.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                          | Right                          | Why                                            |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| "the bag of my sister"         | _my **sister's** bag_          | English puts the owner first, with **'s**     |
| "my sisters bag"               | _my **sister's** bag_          | no apostrophe, no owner — only a plural       |
| "the childrens' books"         | _the **children's** books_     | children is already plural, so it takes **'s** |
| "The what a nice view!"        | _**What a** nice view!_        | the exclamation frame takes no other word      |
| "He overtook the brake."       | _He pressed the **brake**._    | you overtake a car, you press a brake         |

> 🏆 The road is clear behind you, hero. You can sort the seven faces of what, place an apostrophe where the owner stands, and follow a story through its simple pasts. Keep the mirror clean: the next texts are longer, and they hide more.
