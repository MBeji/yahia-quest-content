# ⚔️ Teenagers and Money — Eighty Pounds a Month, Twenty Million in the Bank

> 💡 "Ask people what they do with their money, and they have already told you who they are."

A seventeen-year-old star sits in front of a journalist. The newspapers say she has already made £20 million. She still gets pocket money. This lesson is built on that gap: a questionnaire about your own money, then an interview to read, and above all the language you need to **say what you think of somebody** — and to say it politely enough to be printed.

## 🏰 Five questions about pocket money

Before you read a word about Carol Chapel, you answer a short questionnaire about yourself. It asks five things, always in the same order.

| The questionnaire asks    | What it wants to know                                          |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **How often?**            | whether you get pocket money every day, every week or never    |
| **Would you like more?**  | whether the sum feels enough to you                            |
| **What do you spend it on?** | which one thing takes most of your money                    |
| **Do you share it?**      | whether your brothers and sisters ever see any of it           |
| **Is it a right?**        | whether parents **must** give it, or give it as a favour       |

Two ideas hide behind those five questions, and the book asks you to name them.

- **Selfish or not selfish.** A person is **selfish** when he thinks only of himself. In this questionnaire the selfish answer is always the one that keeps everything: _I keep it all and never lend anybody a dinar._ The generous answer shares, lends or buys something for somebody else.
- **A right or a favour.** If pocket money is a **right**, parents owe it to you and you do not have to thank them for it. If it is a **favour**, they give it when they can and you thank them. Both answers can be defended — but you must be able to say **which one you chose and why**.

> 🗡️ Answer for yourself first. Every opinion question later in the lesson is easier once you know your own answer.

## ⚡ The interview: what Carol Chapel says

Carol Chapel is **17**. She is a singer, she is still at school, and some newspapers write that she has already made **£20 million**. A journalist asks her ten questions. Here is what she answers.

| Topic          | Carol's answer                                                                 |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| herself        | "I'm a mixture of both" — a girl, and a naïve but ambitious businesswoman       |
| her trade      | show business is a **ruthless** world                                          |
| Max            | they grew up together; they are friends, not a couple                          |
| her image      | "I'm not a skinny girl but I don't mind that"                                  |
| money today    | **£80 a month**, and she would like a rise                                     |
| money later    | she can touch her fortune at **21**; she is "not as rich as the press say"     |
| school         | an all-girls school: good for the studies, poor for learning to mix with boys  |
| a choice       | making the album instead of taking a year off for her GCSEs                    |
| family         | her mother is the person she is closest to                                     |
| spending       | her house — it belongs to **her**, and at 18 she could ask her parents to leave |

> ⚠️ Two facts in that table look as if they contradict each other: £20 million in the newspapers, £80 a month in her purse. They do not. The fortune is **locked until she is 21**; the pocket money is what she actually has today.

## 🔮 Which question goes with which answer?

The journalist's sheet holds **eleven** questions but only **ten** answers, so **one question is always left over**. That is the whole exercise, and it is a reading skill, not a memory game. Work like this:

1. Read the answer and name its **topic** in one word: money, family, school, looks, work.
2. Look for the question about **that** topic — not about a neighbouring one.
3. Check that the **shape** of the answer fits the shape of the question: a sum of money answers _How much…?_, a name answers _Who…?_, an age or a date answers _When…?_

| What she answers                              | Its topic | The question it fits                               |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| she names her own house, which belongs to her  | spending  | What's your biggest extravagance?                   |
| they grew up together and are only friends     | Max       | What's the truth about your relationship with Max?  |

> 🗡️ Two questions can share a topic and still be different: _How much pocket money do you get?_ and _When will you have access to your fortune?_ are both about money, but one asks for a **sum** and the other for a **date**. The shape of the answer separates them.

## 📖 The words of the lesson

Five of them come with their definition in the interview activity itself; the four others are on the book's own vocabulary list for this lesson.

| Word                | Meaning                                                       | In a sentence                                            |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **naïve**           | inexperienced, simple minded                                  | _She is naïve about contracts: she signs anything._      |
| **ruthless**        | cruel, pitiless                                               | _A ruthless manager drops a singer after one bad record._ |
| **close**           | near, intimate                                                | _My grandmother is the person I feel closest to._        |
| **looks**           | physical appearance                                           | _The press writes about her looks, never about her work._ |
| **to matter**       | to be of importance                                           | _What other people say does not matter to me._           |
| **self-confident**  | sure of yourself and of what you can do                       | _He is self-confident: he answers without hesitating._   |
| **obsessed (with)** | thinking about one single thing all the time                  | _My little brother is obsessed with trains._             |
| **an extravagance** | an expensive thing you allow yourself                         | _That watch was my one extravagance this year._          |
| **publicity**       | what the press and the public say and write about somebody    | _Publicity followed her from her first record._          |

> ⚠️ Three of these words trap a French speaker. **Extravagance** in English is a costly purchase, not a strange way of behaving. **Publicity** is public attention, not the advertisements on television. And **obsessed** keeps the preposition **with**: _obsessed with trains_, never "obsessed to" or "obsessed of".

## 🗣️ Asking for an opinion, and giving one

You already know _What do you think of…?_ from the Queen of Soul lesson. This lesson adds a second way of asking, and a third verb for answering.

| Move                | The frame                                     | Example                                          |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **ask**             | What do you think of…? · **How do you like…?** | _How do you like the interview?_                 |
| **answer**          | I **think** / I **feel** / I **believe** that… | _I feel that she works too hard for her age._    |

Both questions need the auxiliary **do**, and _How do you like…?_ takes its object **straight after it**: _How do you like the interview?_ ✓ — "How you like the interview?" ✗, "How do you like about the interview?" ✗.

After _think_, _feel_ and _believe_ comes **that + a whole sentence**: _I feel **that** she is honest._ These three verbs describe a state of mind, so they stay in the **present simple**: "I am feeling that…" ✗.

## 📝 The group report — and your turn to ask the questions

In groups of five or six you must agree on **three things you like and three things you do not like** about Carol, then write a short report. A report of opinions carries **opinions with their reasons**, never a list of facts anybody could copy from the text.

- ✓ _We think that she has kept her feet on the ground, because she still counts her pocket money._
- ✗ _Carol Chapel is seventeen years old and lives in Britain._ (true, but it is a fact, not an opinion)

Say **we** and **our** once the group has agreed: the report speaks for the whole group, not for you alone.

The lesson closes on a new box, **IT'S YOUR TURN TO ASK THE QUESTIONS**: you write the oral test of the next lesson yourself, on the topic, on your classmates' ideas and feelings, on the vocabulary and on the grammar. Writing a good question is already half of understanding a lesson.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                             | Right                                      | Why                                             |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| "How you like the album?"         | _**How do you** like the album?_           | the question needs the auxiliary **do**        |
| "I am feeling that she is right." | _I **feel** that she is right._            | _feel_ = a state of mind → present simple      |
| "He is obsessed of money."        | _He is obsessed **with** money._           | _obsessed_ keeps **with**                      |
| "Her extravagance made us laugh." | _That coat was her one **extravagance**._  | an extravagance is a costly purchase           |
| "The publicity on TV is too long."| _**Publicity** followed her everywhere._   | publicity = public attention, not advertisements |

> 🏆 You have read an interview end to end, matched ten answers to eleven questions and said what you think of a person without being rude. Keep those frames close, hero — the next gate asks a bigger question: what does being intelligent even mean?
