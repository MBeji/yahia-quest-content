# ⚔️ Are We All Intelligent? — Six Doors into the Same Room

> 💡 "The question is not how intelligent you are. It is in which way you are."

Ask a class what intelligence is and you get one answer: good marks. This lesson takes that answer apart. You will read about a boy who passed a national maths exam at eight, meet three words that are always confused, and answer a questionnaire that tells you **which kind** of clever you are. Then you will write a description of a gifted person you know — and let a classmate improve it.

## 🏰 What do you call intelligence?

The lesson opens with six statements. You choose the ones that match **your own** definition.

- getting good results at school · passing all your exams
- becoming famous · becoming rich
- getting on well with other people · winning medals in sport

Three of them are about school and money, three are about people and the body. Choosing between them is already an opinion, and the text you read next disagrees with the narrow ones: **gifts are not rare**. Nearly everybody carries one, and the difficulty is finding out **which**.

> 🗡️ Keep the statements you chose. At the end of the lesson you look at them again and decide whether you want to change any of them. Changing your mind after reading is a sign of a working brain, not of a weak one.

## ⚡ A gift · gifted · a prodigy

Three words of the same family. They are never interchangeable, because they do not even belong to the same class.

| Word            | Class     | Meaning                                          | Example                                        |
| --------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **a gift**      | noun      | a natural ability nobody taught you              | _She has a gift for numbers._                  |
| **gifted**      | adjective | having such an ability                           | _He is a gifted swimmer._                      |
| **a prodigy**   | noun      | a **young person** whose ability is astonishing  | _At ten he was already a prodigy._             |

- A gift is a **thing you have**: _to have a gift for…_ ✓ — "to be a gift for…" ✗
- Gifted is a **quality you are**: _to be gifted_ ✓ — "to have gifted" ✗
- A prodigy is a **person you are**: _to be a prodigy_ ✓ — "to have a prodigy for maths" ✗

The text of the lesson tells the story of **John Adams**, who read fluently at the age of two, solved complex maths problems when he was three, and at eight became the youngest pupil in Britain to pass O level maths. Its last line is the one that matters, word for word: "Being gifted isn't a rare quality. It's the natural birth right of almost every youngster."

## 🔮 Six types of intelligence

The questionnaire sorts its questions into sets and gives you six type names to put on them. Learn what each name looks like in ordinary life.

| Type           | You show it when you…                                                       |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **emotional**  | get along with the people round you, influence their choices, are respected  |
| **creative**   | invent and imagine something that did not exist before                       |
| **mechanical** | like building things, repairing what is broken, solving a practical problem  |
| **physical**   | are full of energy, live for sport, and say what you feel with your body     |
| **artistic**   | put things into words or into pictures — stories, poems, drawings, paintings |
| **social**     | look for what others feel, are warm-hearted, and listen well                 |

> 🎯 **artistic** and **creative** stand very close, so be careful. In the questionnaire it is the **artistic** side that the sets illustrate: describing a person, imagining a story, writing a poem, drawing, painting — all of that is artistic. Keep **creative** for inventing something that did not exist before. No exercise of this chapter ever asks you to choose between these two.

> ⚠️ The six are **not** a ladder from worst to best, and nobody is only one of them. The questionnaire is not a competition between classmates: it tells **each** pupil which type or types are his own.

## 🗡️ Answering the questionnaire

The questions come in two shapes, and the short answer must repeat the **helper** of the question — never another one.

| Question shape                          | Yes                | No                    |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| **Do you** enjoy…? / **Do you** like…?  | _Yes, I **do**._   | _No, I **don't**._    |
| **Can you** describe…? / **Can you** …? | _Yes, I **can**._  | _No, I **can't**._    |

A question with **do** is answered with do; a question with **can** is answered with can. "Do you enjoy sport? — Yes, I can." ✗ is the mistake to watch, and it comes from answering the meaning instead of the words.

## 📝 Writing the description of a gifted person

Think of somebody you know who is gifted, then work in four moves.

1. **Choose the statements that apply to that person** from the list in your book — for example _(S)he thinks quickly._ or _(S)he is interested in many activities._
2. **Find an example for each one.** A statement without an example is an empty word; the example is what makes the reader believe you.
3. **Add three kinds of detail**: his or her **age**, **physical appearance** and **social background** (the family and the world he or she comes from).
4. **Organise, then write a first draft.**

The draft is not the end. You **hand it to a partner** and take his in return, you read each other's work, and you suggest improvements on **three points only**:

| The point      | What a remark on it sounds like                                       |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **ideas**      | "You say he is gifted, but you never say what he can actually do."    |
| **grammar**    | "Here it should be _he has_, not _he have_."                          |
| **vocabulary** | "You have written the same word four times; look for another one."    |

Then you discuss the suggestions and use them to improve your own draft. Nobody writes a good description in one go.

## ✍️ The acrostic poem

An **acrostic** spells a word **downwards**: the first letter of each line, read from top to bottom, gives the word. The lines are not sentences — each one opens with a **verb in the -ing form** — and the word itself is not written inside any line.

> **S**eeking information · **T**elling stories · **U**ndertaking projects · **D**elivering presentations · **E**xploring new knowledge · **N**avigating the Web · **T**hinking about the future

Seven letters, seven lines, seven things a pupil does. Once you have studied it, you write your own on the word **PUPILS**: six letters, six lines, six verbs in -ing.

## 📖 The words of the lesson

- **the brain** — the organ inside your head that you think with · **curiosity** — the wish to know and to find out about things you do not know yet.
- **remarkable** — so good or so unusual that people notice it: _Her memory for faces is remarkable._ It is an **adjective**; the verb _to remark_ means to notice or to say.
- **to undertake** — to take a piece of work on yourself and see it through: _She undertook the whole project alone._
- **to navigate** — to find your way: on the sea for a sailor, from page to page for someone using the internet.

## ⚠️ The trap wall

| Wrong                             | Right                                   | Why                                           |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| "She is a gift for languages."    | _She **has a gift** for languages._     | a gift is something you **have**              |
| "He has gifted for drawing."      | _He **is gifted** at drawing._          | gifted is an adjective                        |
| "Her prodigy appeared at four."   | _She was a **prodigy** at four._        | a prodigy is a **person**, not an ability     |
| "Can you draw? — Yes, I do."      | _Can you draw? — **Yes, I can.**_       | the short answer repeats the question's helper |
| "He remarkable the mistake."      | _The mistake was **remarkable**._       | remarkable is an adjective, not a verb        |

> 🏆 Six doors, one room: you can now name the kind of intelligence a person shows, use the gift family without slipping, and build a description that a classmate can improve. Next gate, the light goes out — a lonely road at night, a car that will not overtake, and one question: who was the man?
