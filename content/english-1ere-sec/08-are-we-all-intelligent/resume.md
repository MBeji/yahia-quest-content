# 📜 Summary: Are We All Intelligent?

- **Six ideas of intelligence to choose from** — getting good results at school, passing all your exams, becoming famous, becoming rich, getting on well with other people, winning medals in sport. The lesson refuses the narrow ones: **gifts are not rare** — the text calls being gifted the natural birth right of nearly every youngster, and the work is to find out **which** gift you carry.
- **A gift · gifted · a prodigy** — **a gift** is a noun, a natural ability nobody taught you (_to have a gift for…_); **gifted** is an adjective, the quality of having one (_to be gifted_); **a prodigy** is a noun naming a **young person** whose ability is astonishing (_to be a prodigy_). The text's example is John Adams, who read fluently at two, solved complex maths problems at three, and passed O level maths at eight, the youngest pupil in Britain to do so.
- **Six types of intelligence** — **emotional** (getting along with people, weighing on their choices, being respected), **creative** (inventing and imagining), **mechanical** (building, repairing, solving practical problems), **physical** (energy, sport, feelings shown with the body), **artistic** (words or pictures — stories, poems, drawings, paintings), **social** (looking for what others feel, warm-hearted, a good listener). They are not a ladder and nobody has only one: the questionnaire tells each pupil which type or types are his own.
- **The short answers of the questionnaire** — the answer repeats the **helper** of the question: _Do you enjoy…?_ → _Yes, I **do**. / No, I **don't**._ and _Can you…?_ → _Yes, I **can**. / No, I **can't**._ Answering "Yes, I can." to a do-question is the classic slip.
- **Describing a gifted person, in four moves** — choose the statements that apply to him (_(S)he thinks quickly._, _(S)he is interested in many activities._), find an **example** for each, add the three details (**age**, **physical appearance**, **social background**), then organise and write a first draft.
- **The draft is exchanged, not finished** — a partner suggests improvements on **three points only**: the **ideas** (a quality with no example behind it), the **grammar** (a wrong form), the **vocabulary** (the same word four times); you discuss the suggestions, then improve your own draft.
- **The acrostic poem** — the first letters of the lines, read downwards, spell the word; each line opens with a **verb in the -ing form**; the word itself is never written inside a line. Model: **S**eeking information · **T**elling stories · **U**ndertaking projects · **D**elivering presentations · **E**xploring new knowledge · **N**avigating the Web · **T**hinking about the future — then your own, on **PUPILS**.
- **The words of the lesson** — **the brain** (the organ you think with), **curiosity** (the wish to find out), **remarkable** (an adjective: so good or unusual that people notice it), **to undertake** (to take a piece of work on yourself and see it through), **to navigate** (to find your way, on the sea or from page to page on the internet).
- **The trap wall** — _She **has a gift** for languages._ (not "is a gift"), _He **is gifted** at drawing._ (not "has gifted"), _She was a **prodigy** at four._ (a prodigy is a person, not an ability), _Can you draw? — **Yes, I can.**_ (the helper of the question comes back in the answer), and **remarkable** is an adjective, never a verb.
