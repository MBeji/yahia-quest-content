# ⚔️ Going Shopping — The Gate of the Market

> 💡 "A shop is the easiest place in the world to speak English: you want something, someone has it, and four sentences stand between you and it."

## 🏪 The shops in my town — and what each one sells

Saturday morning. Your mother gives you a list and some money: bread, meat, and a present for your little cousin. Three things — and three different doors in the street.

You know the street by heart. What you do not know yet is the **English name of each door**, and the shopkeeper behind it speaks no other language.

::: definition
A **shop** is a place where you **buy** things. The person who **sells** is the **shopkeeper**; the person who buys is the **customer**. What the shop sells are its **goods**.

The shops of this unit: the **bakery** (bread, croissants), the **butcher's** (meat), the **toy shop** (toys), the **clothes shop** (clothes), the **jewellery shop** (a **ring**, a **necklace**, in **gold** or in **silver**), the **music shop** (an **instrument**), the **market** (many small shops in one street) and the **mall** (many shops in one building).

:::

Look at the four names above the goods: each shop is named after **what it sells**. Hold that idea and you never need to learn the list twice — a shop that sells toys is a **toy shop**, a shop that sells clothes is a **clothes shop**.

::: exemple
Take the three things on your mother's list, one by one:

1. **Bread** is baked, and the shop that bakes and sells it is the **bakery**.
2. **Meat** is cut and sold by the butcher, so you go to the **butcher's**.
3. A **present** for a little cousin: a ball, a doll, a small car — those are toys, so the **toy shop**.

Three lines, three doors: _I go to the bakery, to the butcher's and to the toy shop._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _The shopkeeper **buys** me a necklace._ ✗

The two verbs look like one action seen from two sides, and beginners take the wrong side. Keep them apart by the **money**: the one who **gives** the money **buys**, the one who **takes** the money **sells**.

The right sentence: _The shopkeeper **sells** me a necklace, and I **buy** it._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Your uncle plays music and his old guitar is broken. Which shop does he go to, and why?

---

He goes to the **music shop** — a guitar is an **instrument**, and each shop is named after the goods it sells. He is the **customer** there, and the shopkeeper is the one who **sells** him the guitar.

:::

::: retenir
Each shop is named after its goods: the **bakery** (bread), the **butcher's** (meat), the **toy shop**, the **clothes shop**, the **jewellery shop**, the **music shop** — and the **customer buys** what the **shopkeeper sells**.

:::

## 🛍️ "I would like…" — asking politely

You are at the counter of the market. The meat is in front of you and one sentence comes to your mouth: _Give me meat._

Every word of it is correct English. And no customer in the world says it, because it sounds like an order given to a servant. So what does a customer say?

::: propriete
**Would like** means _want_, but polite. It is the sentence of every shop:

| What you want           | Structure                        | Example                            |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| a thing                 | **I would like** + noun          | I would like a kilo of meat.       |
| to do something         | **I would like to** + verb       | I would like to see the rings.     |
| you ask the other       | **Would you like** + noun?       | Would you like a croissant?        |
| you ask the other       | **Would you like to** + verb?    | Would you like to try it on?       |

**I would like** is often shortened to **I'd like**. And `would` never changes: I would, you would, he would, she would, we would, they would.

The answers are short: **Yes, please.** / **No, thank you.**

:::

::: exemple
Turn _Give me meat_ into the sentence of a customer, in three steps:

1. Open with **I would like** — the polite form replaces the order.
2. After _would like_ comes the **thing you want**, with the quantity → _a kilo of meat_.
3. Close with **please**, the word that costs nothing and changes everything.

The whole sentence: _**I would like** a kilo of meat, **please**._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _I **like** a croissant, please._ ✗ when you are at the counter.

The two sentences exist and they do not say the same thing. _I **like** croissants_ tells your taste — it is true every day of the year, in the shop or in your bed. _I **would like** a croissant_ is a request — it is true **now**, at this counter, and the shopkeeper must answer it.

The right sentence in the shop: _I **would like** a croissant, please._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
You are at the clothes shop and you want to see the blue sweater. Write your sentence.

---

_I **would like to** see the blue sweater, please._ — **would like** makes it a polite request and not an order, and because a **verb** follows (_see_), it takes **to** in front of it.

:::

::: retenir
**I would like** + a thing · **I would like to** + a verb · **Would you like…?** to ask — answer _Yes, please._ or _No, thank you._

:::

## 💰 "How much is it?" — asking and giving the price

The silver ring in the window is beautiful. Your birthday money is in your pocket. And there is no little paper on the ring.

There is exactly one question between you and the answer, and it is four words long.

::: propriete
To ask a **price**:

| You ask about          | Question                | Answer                        |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| one thing              | **How much is it?**     | **It's** 12 dinars.           |
| more than one thing    | **How much are they?**  | **They're** 30 dinars.        |

_It's_ is the short form of _It is_, and _They're_ of _They are_ — both are correct, and the short one is what you hear in a shop.

The words of the price: something **cheap** costs little money (the opposite is **expensive**); a shop on **sale** puts money **off** its prices; a **special offer** is a price for a few days only.

:::

::: exemple
Ask for the price of the ring, then read the shopkeeper's answer, step by step:

1. One ring, so the question is **How much is it?** — the little word _it_ names one thing.
2. The shopkeeper answers with **It's** and then the number and the money → _It's 45 dinars._
3. The ring is on **sale**: 10 dinars **off**. So the price you pay is 35 dinars — that is **cheap** for silver.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _How much **it is**?_ ✗

It tempts you because the answer says _it is_ in that order, and your ear copies the answer. But an English question turns the two words round: the question puts **is** first, the answer puts **it** first.

The right question: _How much **is it**?_ ✓ — and the right answer: _**It is** 45 dinars._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
You are holding two tickets for the museum and you want their price. Write your question.

---

_**How much are they?**_ — two tickets are **more than one** thing, so _it_ becomes **they** and _is_ becomes **are**. The shopkeeper answers _They're 6 dinars._

:::

::: retenir
**How much is it?** → **It's** … · **How much are they?** → **They're** … · **cheap** ≠ expensive, **on sale**, money **off**.

:::

## 🔢 The numbers up to the hundreds

The blazer in the window carries a small white card, and on the card: 468.

You can read those three digits one by one — four, six, eight — and the shopkeeper will look at you strangely. He says them in **one breath**, and this section is that breath.

::: propriete
From one hundred to nine hundred:

| Number | You say                     | Number | You say         |
| ------ | --------------------------- | ------ | --------------- |
| 100    | a hundred / one hundred     | 600    | six hundred     |
| 200    | two hundred                 | 700    | seven hundred   |
| 300    | three hundred               | 800    | eight hundred   |
| 400    | four hundred                | 900    | nine hundred    |
| 500    | five hundred                |        |                 |

Two rules hold the whole table together:

- **hundred never takes an -s** after a number: _two hundred_ ✓, _two hundreds_ ✗.
- between the hundreds and the rest of the number, English puts **and**: 468 = four hundred **and** sixty-eight.

:::

::: exemple
Read the price card 468, step by step:

1. Cut the number in two: **400** and **68**.
2. The left part is **four hundred** — _hundred_ stays alone, with no -s behind it.
3. The right part is **sixty-eight**, and the two parts are joined by **and**.

The price card, read aloud: _four hundred **and** sixty-eight dinars._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _four **hundreds** and sixty-eight._ ✗

It tempts you because there are four of them, and in English things you count usually take an -s — four ring**s**, four shop**s**. The word **hundred** is the exception: after a number it never moves.

The right reading: _four **hundred** and sixty-eight._ ✓ (You do say _hundred**s** of people_ — but only when no number stands in front of it.)

:::

::: verifie
The sweater costs 350 dinars. Say the price.

---

_Three hundred **and** fifty dinars._ — 350 cuts into **300** and **50**: _three hundred_ keeps its **-s**-free _hundred_, and **and** joins the two parts of the number.

:::

::: retenir
100 = a hundred … 900 = nine hundred; **no -s** after a number, and **and** before the rest: 468 = four hundred and sixty-eight.

:::

## 🕰️ "Last Saturday I bought…" — the simple past

Monday morning, in the schoolyard. Your friend asks what you did on Saturday.

The shopping is finished. The bread is eaten, the present is given, the money is spent — everything you want to tell happened **before now**. English has one tense for exactly that, and it changes the verb.

::: propriete
The **simple past** tells what happened **before now**. Its time words: **yesterday**, **last week**, **last month**, **five weeks ago**.

**Regular** verbs take **-ed**: visit → visit**ed**, want → want**ed**, help → help**ed**, thank → thank**ed**. Three small spelling rules:

| The verb ends in…                  | You add…            | Example                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **-e**                             | only **-d**         | close → close**d**          |
| consonant + **-y**                 | **-ied**            | study → stud**ied**         |
| one short consonant-vowel-consonant| double + **-ed**    | shop → sho**pped**          |

**Irregular** verbs do not take -ed: they change shape, and you learn them one by one. The ones you need in a shop:

| Now    | Before now |
| ------ | ---------- |
| buy    | bought     |
| sell   | sold       |
| find   | found      |
| choose | chose      |
| win    | won        |
| spend  | spent      |
| pay    | paid       |
| go     | went       |

And the good news, true for every verb: the past is **the same for everybody** — I bought, you bought, she bought, we bought.

:::

::: exemple
Tell your friend about Saturday, step by step:

1. Start with the time word, so he knows at once you are before now → _**Last Saturday**…_
2. _Buy_ is **irregular**: it does not take -ed, it becomes **bought** → _Last Saturday I **bought** a present._
3. _Visit_ is **regular** and ends in a consonant, so it simply takes -ed → _and I **visited** the mall._

The whole answer: _Last Saturday I **bought** a present and I **visited** the mall._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Last Saturday I **buyed** a present._ ✗

It tempts you because -ed works for hundreds of verbs, so the hand writes it everywhere. But the most common verbs of English are exactly the ones that refuse it — and _buy_ is the verb of this whole unit.

The right sentence: _Last Saturday I **bought** a present._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Put the sentence before now: "My sister **chooses** a necklace and she **pays** 60 dinars."

---

_My sister **chose** a necklace and she **paid** 60 dinars._ — _choose_ and _pay_ are both **irregular**, so they change shape instead of taking -ed, and the -s of _chooses_ and _pays_ disappears: in the past every subject uses the same form.

:::

::: retenir
Before now = **simple past**: regular verbs take **-ed** (visited, shopped, studied), irregular verbs change shape (**bought**, sold, found, chose, won, spent, paid, went) — the same form for everybody.

:::

## 👕 At the clothes shop — trying it on

On the shelf, the blazer is beautiful. On you? Nobody knows yet, and the shopkeeper is waiting for a sentence.

This is the one shop where you do not only ask a price: you take the clothes **off the shelf and onto your body** before you decide.

::: definition
The clothes of this unit: a **blazer**, a **vest**, a **sweater**, a **tie**, a shirt, trousers, a jacket, jeans.

To **try on** a piece of clothing = to put it on in the shop, to see it on you. The little room where you do it is the **fitting room**.

Two verbs then tell you the answer: it **fits** you = it is your **size**; it **suits** you = it **looks good** on you.

:::

The four names under the drawings are the four words you will hear in the fitting room. Notice that each one takes **a** in front of it: _a tie_, _a sweater_, _a blazer_, _a vest_ — one piece of clothing, one little word.

::: exemple
Buy the blazer, step by step:

1. Ask for it politely, with the structure of the shop → _I would like to **try on** this blazer, please._
2. The shopkeeper sends you to the **fitting room** — the only place where you put the clothes on.
3. Come out and say what you found. It is your size, so it **fits**; and it looks good on you, so it **suits** you → _It fits me and it suits me. I'll take it._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _This blazer **suits** me, but it is too small._ ✗

The two verbs are said at the same moment, about the same blazer, so beginners use one for both. They answer two different questions: **fit** answers _is it my **size**?_, **suit** answers _does it **look good** on me?_

The right sentence: _This blazer **suits** me, but it does not **fit** me — it is too small._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
The green sweater is your size, but green is a terrible colour on you. Say it in one sentence.

---

_The sweater **fits** me, but it does not **suit** me._ — _fits_ answers the question of the **size** (it is the right one), and _suit_ answers the question of the **look** (green is not good on you), so the second verb is the one you refuse.

:::

::: retenir
**Try on** the clothes in the **fitting room**: a blazer, a vest, a sweater, a tie — it **fits** you if it is your size, it **suits** you if it looks good on you.

:::

## 📍 in the corner, on the corner

Two shops, two sentences, and one little word between them. The fitting room is **in** the corner. The bakery is **on** the corner.

Change the word and the shop moves — literally. So which corner is which?

::: propriete
Both words point at a corner, but not at the same one:

| Word                | Where it puts you           | Example                                        |
| ------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **in the corner**   | **inside**, where two walls meet | The fitting room is **in the corner** of the shop. |
| **on the corner**   | **outside**, where two streets meet | The bakery is **on the corner** of Market Street.  |

The rule in one line: walls are **in**, streets are **on**.

:::

3. Same word _corner_ in both sentences: it is the little word in front of it that tells you whether to look for a street or for a wall.

:::

> ⚠️ _The bakery is **in** the corner of the street._ ✗ — a street has no walls. Say _**on** the corner of the street._

::: verifie
Complete: "The perfume shop is ___ the corner of the mall, and the market is ___ the corner of the big street."

---

_The perfume shop is **in** the corner of the mall, and the market is **on** the corner of the big street._ — the mall is a **building** with walls, so its corner takes **in**; the big street is outside, so its corner takes **on**.

:::

::: retenir
**in the corner** = inside, where two walls meet · **on the corner** = outside, where two streets meet.

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the sound of jeans and the sound of garage

Say **jeans**. Now say **garage**, and hold the sound in the middle of it.

The two sounds are cousins: your tongue is in the same place and the air buzzes in both. But one of them starts with a tiny hit, and the other does not. Can you hear it?

::: propriete
Two close sounds live in the words of this unit:

| Sound                | How it starts                     | Words of the shop                                      |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| the sound of **jeans** | a tiny **d** before the buzz     | jacket · jewellery · fridge · jump · enjoy             |
| the sound of **garage**| no **d**, the buzz alone, soft   | usually · garage                                        |

The test: say the word very slowly. If your tongue **hits** the roof of your mouth before the buzz, it is the sound of **jeans**. If the buzz simply flows, it is the sound of **garage**.

:::

::: exemple
Sort two words of the clothes shop, step by step:

1. Say **jacket** slowly: the tongue hits first, then the buzz → the sound of **jeans**.
2. Say **usually** slowly and hold its middle: no hit at all, the buzz flows → the sound of **garage**.
3. The letters do not decide: _jacket_ and _jewellery_ both begin with the letter **j**, and so does _jump_ — but _usually_ has no **j** at all and still buzzes.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: saying **usually** as if it began the way _jump_ does, with the little hit in the middle. ✗

It tempts you because the two sounds are so close that your mouth takes the easier one — and the hit is easier. Say the word twice, very slowly, and let the middle **flow**: that is the sound of _garage_. ✓

:::

::: verifie
In one of these three words you hear the soft sound of _garage_: **fridge**, **garage**, **jewellery**. Which one — and what do the other two have?

---

**garage** — its sound flows, with no little hit before the buzz. In **fridge** and in **jewellery** the tongue hits first, so both carry the sound of **jeans**, the one with the tiny **d** in front of the buzz.

:::

::: retenir
Two cousin sounds: the one of **jeans** (jacket, jewellery, fridge, jump, enjoy) starts with a tiny **d**; the one of **garage** (usually) is the buzz alone.

:::

> 🏆 Sixth gate cleared. You can walk into any shop, ask for what you want politely, ask its price, read a number up to nine hundred, try the clothes on and tell the whole story afterwards in the past. Keep your coins: the last gate, **Around the World**, takes you much further than Market Street.
