# ⚔️ Celebrations — The Gate of the Days We Remember

> 💡 "A year has three hundred and sixty five days. A handful of them you will remember all your life — and to tell them, English opens a tense you have never used."

## 🎉 The days we celebrate

It is the eleventh of January. Your school looks different: there are colours on the walls, the classroom is full of paper flowers, and nobody is doing an exercise.

A day like this one has a name, and so does every object on the table. This unit gives you both.

::: definition
To **celebrate** a day is to make it special, with other people.

The days of this unit: **Children's Day** (the eleventh of January), **Mother's Day**, **Teacher's Day**, **New Year**, and the **end of school year party**.

What you find at a party: a **gift** (the thing you give), a **wish** (the good thing you say), **snacks** (the small things you eat), **crackers**, a **clown**, a **show**, **magic**.

What people do: they **decorate** the room, they **blow up** the balloons, they **put on** their best clothes, they **hug** each other, they **wait for** midnight, and somebody is **surprised**.

:::

::: exemple
Name what each person does at the New Year party, step by step:

1. Sarra puts paper flowers and lights on the walls → she **decorates** the room.
2. Karim takes a balloon and fills it with air → he **blows up** the balloon.
3. Everybody looks at the clock and nobody goes to bed → they **wait for midnight**.

:::

::: verifie
Your mother opens a box and finds a perfume she did not expect. Name her feeling and name the object in English.

---

The object is a **gift** (a **perfume**), and she is **surprised** — she did not know it was coming, and that is exactly what the word _surprised_ says.

:::

::: retenir
**celebrate · gift · wish · snacks · crackers · decorate · blow up · put on · hug · wait for · surprised** — the words of a celebration.

:::

## ⚖️ Everybody has the right to…

Children's Day is not only balloons. On that day your teacher writes four words on the board — **education**, **life**, **clothing**, **health** — and asks what they have in common.

They are not things you own. They are things that are **owed** to every child, and English says that with one fixed structure.

::: propriete
A **right** is something every child must have, everywhere in the world.

**have the right to + something** — and the verb follows the subject:

| Subject                 | Form                  | Example                              |
| ----------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| I / you / we / they     | **have the right to** | Children **have the right to** education. |
| he / she / it           | **has the right to**  | She **has the right to** health.     |

The rights this unit names: the right to **education**, to **life**, to **clothing**, to **health**.

:::

::: exemple
Say that your little sister must go to school, step by step:

1. Your sister is one girl → she is **she**, so the verb is **has**, not _have_.
2. The structure never changes: **has the right to** — the little word _to_ is part of it.
3. Add what is owed → _She **has the right to** education._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _She **have** the right to education._ ✗

It tempts you because the words _the right to_ feel like one long block, and you stop watching the verb in front of it. But that verb is an ordinary verb: with **he**, **she** or **it** it becomes **has**.

The right sentence: _She **has the right to** education._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Children ___ the right to health, and every child ___ the right to a home."

---

_Children **have** the right to health, and every child **has** the right to a home._ — _children_ are _they_, so the verb is **have**; _every child_ is one child, that is _he_ or _she_, so the verb is **has**.

:::

::: retenir
**I / you / we / they have the right to** · **he / she / it has the right to** — education, life, clothing, health.

:::

## 📅 Yesterday — the simple past

Mother's Day was yesterday. You want to tell your friend what happened at your house — and every sentence you know says _I give_, _I buy_, _I prepare_.

Those sentences are about today. Yesterday is finished, and a finished day needs its own tense.

::: propriete
The **simple past** tells what happened at a moment that is **over**. For a **regular** verb, you add **-ed** — and the ending is the same for **every** subject:

_I play**ed**._ · _She play**ed**._ · _They play**ed**._ — never an extra -s.

Three little groups of words call the past and can never sit in a present sentence:

| The words           | Examples                                        |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **yesterday**       | Yesterday, we decorated the house.              |
| **last** + a moment | last week · last night · last year              |
| a number + **ago**  | four days ago · two weeks ago                   |

:::

The two red points of the line are behind you and cannot move: any sentence that lands on them takes the past.

::: exemple
Tell what your family did for Mother's Day, step by step:

1. The moment is over, and _yesterday_ says so → the verb goes to the past.
2. _prepare_ is a regular verb, so it takes **-ed** → _prepared_.
3. The ending never changes with the subject → _Yesterday, we **prepared** a big cake._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Yesterday, she **playeds** with her cousins._ ✗

It tempts you because you have spent a whole year adding an **-s** for _he_, _she_ and _it_. That **-s** lives in the present only: in the past, every subject shares the same form.

The right sentence: _Yesterday, she **played** with her cousins._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Put the sentence in the past: "We wait for midnight." Begin with _Last year_.

---

_Last year, we **waited** for midnight._ — _last year_ is a finished moment, and _wait_ is a regular verb, so it takes **-ed** and keeps the same form for every subject.

:::

::: retenir
**Regular verb + -ed** for every subject · the past is called by **yesterday**, **last** + a moment, and a number + **ago**.

:::

## ✍️ Writing the -ed — three spellings

You know the ending. Now look at three verbs of the New Year party as they are written in the past: _waited_, _loved_, _hugged_.

Three verbs, three different pictures — and yet the ending you hear is the same one. What happened to the letters?

::: propriete
The ending is always **-ed**; what changes is what the verb does to make room for it:

| The verb…                                     | You write…          | Examples                                       |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| ends in a consonant                           | + **ed**            | wait → wait**ed** · ask → ask**ed** · talk → talk**ed** |
| already ends in **-e**                        | + **d** only        | love → love**d** · prepare → prepare**d** · decorate → decorate**d** |
| is short: one vowel + one consonant           | double it + **ed**  | hug → hug**ged** · stop → stop**ped**          |

:::

::: exemple
Write the past of _decorate_ and of _hug_, step by step:

1. _decorate_ already ends in **e**, so it does not need a second one → add only **d** → _decorated_.
2. _hug_ is short and ends in one vowel plus one consonant → double the g → _hugged_.
3. Both endings are pronounced with the same little ending, but the spelling of each verb follows its own line.

:::

> ⚠️ _loveed_ ✗ and _huged_ ✗ — the first adds an **e** the verb already has, the second forgets to double. Write _loved_ and _hugged_.

::: verifie
Write the past of the three verbs: _wish_, _live_, _stop_.

---

_wish → **wished**_ (an ordinary consonant, so simply **-ed**), _live → **lived**_ (it already ends in **e**, so only **-d**), _stop → **stopped**_ (short, one vowel and one consonant, so the p doubles).

:::

::: retenir
**+ ed** after a consonant · **+ d** when the verb ends in **e** · **double + ed** for a short verb in one vowel + one consonant.

:::

## 🔀 The verbs that go their own way

Your mother opened her gift and you want to say it: _I buyed a perfume._ Your teacher shakes her head.

The rule you have just learnt is true for most verbs. A small group refuses it — and those are the ones you will use every single day.

::: propriete
An **irregular verb** does not take **-ed**: it changes shape, and you learn the pair by heart.

| Infinitive | Simple past |
| ---------- | ----------- |
| buy        | **bought**  |
| write      | **wrote**   |
| do         | **did**     |
| have       | **had**     |
| eat        | **ate**     |
| drink      | **drank**   |
| make       | **made**    |
| say        | **said**    |
| blow up    | **blew up** |
| put        | **put**     |

The last one is the strangest: **put** does not move at all — _Yesterday I **put** on my best clothes._

:::

::: exemple
Tell what happened at the Mother's Day breakfast, step by step:

1. _buy_ is in the list, so no **-ed** is possible → the past is **bought**.
2. The form is the same for every subject, exactly like a regular verb → _I bought_, _she bought_.
3. Build the sentence → _Yesterday, my father **bought** a necklace and we **ate** a big cake._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _I **buyed** a perfume._ ✗ and _She **maked** a cake._ ✗

It tempts you because the **-ed** rule works for so many verbs that your hand adds it without asking. These verbs were learnt before the rule existed, and they kept their old shapes.

The right sentences: _I **bought** a perfume._ ✓ and _She **made** a cake._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Put in the past: "We drink lemonade and we write a card for our teacher."

---

_We **drank** lemonade and we **wrote** a card for our teacher._ — both verbs are irregular: _drink_ becomes **drank** and _write_ becomes **wrote**, and neither of them ever takes **-ed**.

:::

::: retenir
**buy → bought · write → wrote · do → did · have → had · eat → ate · drink → drank · make → made · say → said · put → put** — learnt by heart, never with -ed.

:::

## 👤 I was, we were

One verb is so irregular that it takes two different shapes in the past, and you cannot avoid it: it is the verb you use to say how somebody **is**.

_The party is beautiful_ — that is today. Yesterday, what do you say?

::: propriete
The past of **be** chooses its shape from the subject:

| Subject               | Past     | Example                              |
| --------------------- | -------- | ------------------------------------ |
| I / he / she / it     | **was**  | I **was** surprised. · She **was** happy. |
| you / we / they       | **were** | We **were** at the party. · They **were** tired. |

:::

::: exemple
Say how you felt when you opened your gift, step by step:

1. The moment is over → the verb goes to the past.
2. The subject is **I**, and I belongs to the first line of the table → **was**.
3. Add what you felt → _I **was** surprised._ And for your parents, who are _they_ → _They **were** happy._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _We **was** at the party._ ✗

It tempts you because **was** is the form you meet first and you keep it for everything. But _we_ sits on the second line of the table, with _you_ and _they_.

The right sentence: _We **were** at the party._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "The clown ___ very funny, and the children ___ excited."

---

_The clown **was** very funny, and the children **were** excited._ — one clown is _he_, so the form is **was**; several children are _they_, so the form is **were**.

:::

::: retenir
**I / he / she / it → was** · **you / we / they → were**.

:::

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the three sounds of the final -ed

Say _talked_, then _played_, then _waited_. All three end with the same two letters, and your mouth makes **three** different endings.

The spelling gives you no clue at all. What decides is the sound that comes just **before** the ending.

::: propriete
The ending **-ed** has three pronunciations:

| Sound    | You use it when the verb ends in…                          | Words of this unit                      |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **/t/**  | a quiet sound: k, p, f, s, sh, ch (your throat stays still) | baked · asked · talked · danced         |
| **/d/**  | a sound that shakes your throat, or a vowel                 | hugged · played · prepared · lived      |
| **/ɪd/** | the sound **t** or the sound **d** — nothing else            | waited · counted · wanted · decorated   |

Start with the third line: if the verb already ends in **t** or **d**, you cannot glue anything to it, so a whole little syllable appears. Your teacher's book writes that third sound **/id/**; it is the same small ending.

:::

::: exemple
Find the ending of _asked_ and of _wanted_, step by step:

1. _ask_ ends in the quiet sound **k**: your throat does not shake → the ending is **/t/**.
2. _want_ ends in **t**: nothing can be glued to a t → a syllable appears, and the ending is **/ɪd/**.
3. The two words are written with the same **-ed**, so only your ear decides, never your eye.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: reading **-ed** as a syllable everywhere — _talk-ed_, _play-ed_. ✗

It tempts you because the letters are there and you want to say them. But the extra syllable belongs to the **/ɪd/** line only, which needs a verb ending in **t** or **d**.

The right readings: _talked_ in one piece with **/t/**, _played_ in one piece with **/d/**, and _waited_ in two pieces with **/ɪd/**. ✓

:::

::: verifie
One of these three verbs ends with the sound **/ɪd/**: _laughed_, _counted_, _hugged_. Which one?

---

**counted** — the verb _count_ ends in the sound **t**, so a whole little syllable appears and the ending is **/ɪd/**. _Laughed_ ends in a quiet sound and takes **/t/**, and _hugged_ ends in a sound that shakes the throat and takes **/d/**.

:::

::: retenir
**/t/** after a quiet sound (talked, asked) · **/d/** after a sound that shakes the throat (played, hugged) · **/ɪd/** only after **t** or **d** (waited, counted).

:::
