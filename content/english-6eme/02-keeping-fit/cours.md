# ⚔️ Keeping Fit — The Gate of the Strong Body

> 💡 "You already know that an apple is better than a doughnut. This chapter gives you the words to say it — and to tell a friend before he eats the doughnut."

## 🥗 On your plate — healthy food and unhealthy food

Break time. Your friend opens his bag: a doughnut and a bottle of soda. You open yours: an apple and a sandwich with cheese.

Both bags carry food, and both of you will eat. The question of this whole unit is the one your body asks at four o'clock: **which food keeps you strong until the evening?**

::: definition
**Healthy** food is good for your body. You should eat **lots of** it: **fruits**, **vegetables**, **meat**, **fish**, **eggs**, **pasta**, **bread**, **milk**, **yoghurt**.

**Unhealthy** food is **bad for** your body. You should eat very little of it: **sweets**, **doughnuts**, **crisps**, **cakes**, too much **oil**, soda.

**Lots of** means a big quantity: _I eat lots of vegetables._

:::

The two baskets never mix: a food goes into one of them, and it is your body, not your taste, that decides which one.

::: exemple
Sort three foods from your kitchen, one step at a time:

1. **Carrots** grow in the ground and they are **vegetables** → the healthy basket.
2. **Fish** gives your body what it needs to grow → the healthy basket.
3. **Crisps** are full of salt and **oil**, and oil is **bad for** your body → the unhealthy basket.

:::

::: verifie
Your plate today: rice, a big cake, an egg and green beans. Which one goes into the unhealthy basket?

---

The **cake** — it is full of sugar and it is **bad for** your body. Rice, the egg and the green beans are **healthy** food, and you should eat **lots of** them.

:::

::: retenir
**Healthy** food (fruits, vegetables, fish, eggs, milk) is good for your body; **unhealthy** food (sweets, doughnuts, crisps) is **bad for** it.

:::

## 💚 Giving advice — should and shouldn't

Your friend is tired every single morning. He sleeps at midnight, he eats sweets before school, and he asks you: _what can I do?_

You know the answer. You are not his father and you are not giving him an order — you are giving him **advice**. English has one word for exactly that.

::: propriete
To give **advice**, use **should** and **shouldn't**:

| You want to say…      | Structure            | Example                               |
| --------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| it is a good idea     | **should** + verb    | You **should eat** vegetables.        |
| it is not a good idea | **shouldn't** + verb | You **shouldn't eat** lots of sweets. |

**shouldn't** is the short form of **should not**. The verb after it stays **bare**: no _to_, and no **-s**, even with he, she or it — _He **should sleep** early._

:::

::: exemple
Give your friend his advice in three steps:

1. Find the good habit: sleeping early. It is a **good idea**, so the word is **should** → _You **should sleep** early._
2. Find the bad habit: sweets before school. It is a **bad idea**, so the word is **shouldn't** → _You **shouldn't eat** sweets before school._
3. Keep the verb bare after both words: _sleep_ and _eat_, never _to sleep_ and never _eats_.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _You should **to drink** water._ ✗ and _He **shoulds** drink water._ ✗

The first one tempts you because your own language puts an infinitive after a verb. The second one tempts you because you have just learned that **he** and **she** add an **-s**.

**should** refuses both: the verb after it is bare, and **should** itself never changes.

The right sentences: _You **should drink** water._ ✓ — _He **should drink** water._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Your little sister watches television until midnight. Give her two pieces of advice, one with should and one with shouldn't.

---

_You **should go** to bed early._ and _You **shouldn't watch** television until midnight._ — **should** opens the good idea, **shouldn't** opens the bad one, and the verb stays bare after both (_go_, _watch_).

:::

::: retenir
**should** + bare verb = good advice · **shouldn't** + bare verb = advice not to do it. Never _to_, never an **-s**.

:::

## 🛡️ The strong rule — must and mustn't

Advice can be refused. Your friend can listen to you, say _yes_, and eat his sweets anyway. Nothing happens.

Now the dentist speaks: _brush your teeth twice a day._ That is not an idea any more, it is a **rule**. So which word carries the strong one?

::: propriete
**must** and **mustn't** say that something is **necessary** or **forbidden**:

| You want to say… | Structure          | Example                                |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| it is necessary  | **must** + verb    | You **must brush** your teeth.         |
| it is forbidden  | **mustn't** + verb | You **mustn't eat** sweets before bed. |

**mustn't** is the short form of **must not**. Like **should**, it takes a **bare verb** and it never adds an **-s**: _She **must wash** her hands._

:::

Put the two pairs side by side and the difference is the **force**, never the shape:

| Word          | Force       | Example                                |
| ------------- | ----------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **should**    | a good idea | You should eat fruits.                 |
| **must**      | a real rule | You must wash your hands before meals. |
| **shouldn't** | a bad idea  | You shouldn't eat lots of crisps.      |
| **mustn't**   | forbidden   | You mustn't play with the medicines.   |

::: exemple
Choose the word for two sentences at the doctor's, step by step:

1. _Wash your hands before you eat._ The doctor makes it a **rule**, not a suggestion → **must** : _You **must wash** your hands before you eat._
2. _Eat one fruit every day._ It is a **good idea**, and nothing bad happens if you forget one day → **should** : _You **should eat** one fruit every day._
3. Both verbs stay bare after the word: _wash_, _eat_ — exactly like after **should**.

:::

::: verifie
Complete with must or should: "You ___ keep fit, and in the car you ___ wear your seat belt."

---

_You **should** keep fit, and in the car you **must** wear your seat belt._ — keeping fit is a **good idea**, so it takes **should**; the seat belt is a **rule** of the road, so it takes **must**.

:::

::: retenir
**must** + bare verb = it is necessary · **mustn't** + bare verb = it is forbidden. Stronger than should / shouldn't, same bare verb after it.

:::

## 💪 What you can do — can and can't

Two boys at the school gate. Amine runs up to the third floor and arrives without stopping. Karim stops after ten steps, and he is **tired**.

Amine is **fit**, Karim is not. You want to say it in one sentence about what each body **is able to do** — and that needs a new word.

::: propriete
**can** expresses **ability**, **can't** expresses **inability**:

| You want to say…       | Structure        | Example           |
| ---------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------- |
| I am able to do it     | **can** + verb   | I **can swim**.   |
| I am not able to do it | **can't** + verb | I **can't swim**. |

**can't** is the short form of **cannot**. The verb stays **bare** and **can** never adds an **-s**: _She **can run** fast._

To ask: **Can you swim?** — the answers are short: **Yes, I can.** / **No, I can't.**

:::

The words for the body come with it: a **fit** person plays and runs without difficulty; an unfit person is quickly **tired**. And the words for how you feel: **excited**, **happy**, **sad**, **unhappy**.

::: exemple
Say what each body can do, step by step:

1. Amine climbs three floors and he is not tired → he is able to do it → _Amine **can climb** the stairs._
2. Karim stops after ten steps → he is not able to do it → _Karim **can't climb** the stairs._
3. _Amine_ is **he**, and **can** still does not change: never _cans_, and the verb after it stays bare, never _to climb_.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _She **cans** swim._ ✗ and _He can **to run** fast._ ✗

They are the same two temptations as with **should**: the **-s** of he / she / it, and the _to_ of your own language.

The right sentences: _She **can swim**._ ✓ — _He **can run** fast._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Nadia goes to the swimming pool every week. Her little brother is afraid of the water. Write one sentence about each of them.

---

_Nadia **can swim**._ and _Her brother **can't swim**._ — **can** says that the body is able to do it, **can't** says that it is not, and the verb stays bare after both (_swim_).

:::

::: retenir
**can** + bare verb = ability · **can't** + bare verb = inability. Answer a question with _Yes, I can._ / _No, I can't._

:::

## 🤒 How are you feeling? — have a and to be

Monday morning. Meriem's head hurts and she cannot look at the blackboard. The teacher asks her: _How are you feeling?_

Meriem has two things to say — what hurts, and how she feels. And English gives her two different structures for them.

::: propriete
An **ailment** is the name of the thing that hurts. It is a **noun**, so it goes with **have a**:

**have a + ailment** — _I **have a headache**._ · _I **have a toothache**._ · _I **have a stomachache**._

With **he**, **she** or **it**, _have_ becomes **has**: _She **has a** toothache._

How you feel is an **adjective**, so it goes with the verb **to be**:

**to be + adjective** — _I **am sick**._ · _I **am tired**._ · _He **is sad**._ · _They **are excited**._

:::

2. How she feels is **tired** — an adjective → the structure is **to be** : _I **am tired**._
3. Now give her advice with the word of the good idea: _You **should go** home and rest._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _I **am a headache**._ ✗ and _I **have sick**._ ✗

The two structures get exchanged, because in your own language one single verb carries both.

Sort them by the word that follows: a **noun** in **-ache** takes **have a**, an **adjective** takes **to be**.

The right sentences: _I **have a headache**._ ✓ — _I **am sick**._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Karim ate too many sweets yesterday. Complete: "He ___ a stomachache and he ___ unhappy."

---

_He **has** a stomachache and he **is** unhappy._ — _a stomachache_ is a noun, so it takes **have**, which becomes **has** with _he_; _unhappy_ is an adjective, so it takes the verb **to be**, which is **is** with _he_.

:::

::: retenir
**have a + ailment** for what hurts (a headache, a toothache, a stomachache) · **to be + adjective** for how you feel (sick, tired, sad, excited).

:::

## 🔢 What quantity? — How many and How much

You are writing the menu for Saturday, and you go to the shop with two questions. One is about **eggs**, the other is about **cheese**.

Both questions ask the same thing — _what quantity?_ — and English refuses to give them the same word. Why?

::: propriete
It depends on the noun:

| The noun is…                                                                  | Question word | Examples                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **countable** — you count it 1, 2, 3, and it takes an **-s**                  | **How many**  | How many **eggs**? · How many **biscuits**? |
| **uncountable** — you cannot count it one by one, and it has no plural **-s** | **How much**  | How much **cheese**? · How much **oil**?    |

Countable: eggs, apples, bananas, biscuits, tomatoes. Uncountable: cheese, milk, oil, rice, bread, water, meat, pasta, yoghurt.

:::

::: exemple
Ask the shopkeeper your two questions, step by step:

1. **Eggs** — you can count them, and the word carries an **-s** → the noun is **countable** → _**How many** eggs do you want?_
2. **Cheese** — you cannot say _one cheese, two cheeses_ for the block on the table, and it has no **-s** → the noun is **uncountable** → _**How much** cheese do you want?_
3. The test that never fails: put the number **two** in front of the noun. _Two eggs_ works, so it is **How many**. _Two cheese_ does not work, so it is **How much**.

:::

> ⚠️ _How much eggs?_ ✗ — the noun carries an **-s**, so it is countable, and the question is _**How many** eggs?_

::: verifie
Complete the two questions of the menu: "___ tomatoes are there? ___ milk is there?"

---

_**How many** tomatoes are there? **How much** milk is there?_ — _tomatoes_ carries an **-s** and you can count them, so the word is **How many**; _milk_ has no plural and you cannot count it, so the word is **How much**.

:::

::: retenir
**How many** + a countable noun in **-s** (eggs, biscuits) · **How much** + an uncountable noun (cheese, milk, oil).

:::

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the short i and the long ee

Say **fit** out loud. Now say **feet**. The first sound and the last sound are the same in both words, and yet nobody confuses them.

What changed sits in the middle, and it is the only thing that tells your friend which word you said. So what is it?

::: propriete
The same letters can carry a **short** vowel or a **long** one:

| Sound     | What your mouth does               | Words of this unit                        |
| --------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **short** | it goes fast; you cannot hold it   | fit · hit · drink · milk · rabbit · sit   |
| **long**  | it stays; you can hold it a second | cheese · meat · teeth · eat · feet · read |

The test is the one your mouth already knows: **try to hold the vowel**. Only the long one lets you.

:::

::: exemple
Sort two words with the holding test, step by step:

1. Say _milk_ and try to hold the middle sound: it stops at once → the vowel is **short**.
2. Say _teeth_ and hold it: the sound goes on as long as you have air → the vowel is **long**.
3. Now say them one after the other — _milk, teeth, milk, teeth_ — and your ear hears the pair, not the spelling.

:::

::: verifie
One of these three words has the long sound: _drink_, _sit_, _eat_. Which one?

---

**eat** — you can hold its vowel, so it is the **long** one, like _cheese_ and _feet_. In _drink_ and in _sit_ the vowel stops at once: it is the **short** one, like _fit_ and _milk_.

:::

::: retenir
Two vowels behind the same letters: the **short** one (fit, milk, sit) stops at once, the **long** one (feet, cheese, eat) can be held.

:::
