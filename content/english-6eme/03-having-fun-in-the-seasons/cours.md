# ⚔️ Having Fun in the Seasons — The Gate of the Four Skies

> 💡 "The sky above your head changes four times a year, and every time it changes it takes your clothes, your games and your holidays with it. This chapter gives you the words for all four."

## 🍂 Four seasons, twelve months

Open the window in January: the air is cold and you look for your **sweater**. Open the same window in July: the sun burns and you look for your **swimsuit**.

Nothing moved but the date. The **year** turns, and it turns through four doors. What are their names in English?

::: definition
A **season** is one of the four parts of the **year**. Each season lasts three **months**:

| Season     | Its months                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------ |
| **spring** | March · April · May            |
| **summer** | June · July · August           |
| **autumn** | September · October · November |
| **winter** | December · January · February  |

The twelve months, in order: **January**, **February**, **March**, **April**, **May**, **June**, **July**, **August**, **September**, **October**, **November**, **December**.

:::

Read the wheel from the green quarter and turn: spring, summer, autumn, winter — then spring again. The wheel never stops, and after December comes January.

::: exemple
Find the season of three dates, step by step:

1. Your birthday is in **April**. April sits between March and May → the season is **spring**.
2. The school holidays start in **July**. July sits between June and August → the season is **summer**.
3. You go back to school in **September**. September opens the third quarter → the season is **autumn**.

:::

::: verifie
Your cousin was born in **February**. Which season is it, and which season comes after it?

---

February is the last month of **winter** (December, January, February). The season that comes after it is **spring**, which opens in March.

:::

::: retenir
Four **seasons** in one **year**: **spring**, **summer**, **autumn**, **winter** — three **months** each, and the wheel starts again after December.

:::

## ☁️ Noun + y = adjective

You look at the sky and you see a **cloud**. That word names the thing. But you want to describe the **day**, not the cloud — and _a cloud day_ is not English.

There is one small letter between the name of the thing and the word that describes the day. Which one?

::: propriete
Add a **y** to the noun and you get the **adjective**:

$$ Noun + y = Adjective $$

| Noun      | + y | Adjective  |
| --------- | --- | ---------- |
| **cloud** | y   | **cloudy** |
| **rain**  | y   | **rainy**  |
| **wind**  | y   | **windy**  |
| **snow**  | y   | **snowy**  |
| **sun**   | y   | **sunny**  |
| **fog**   | y   | **foggy**  |

Watch the last two: when the noun is **consonant + vowel + consonant** (s-u-n, f-o-g), you **double the last consonant** before the **y** — _sunny_, _foggy_, never _suny_.

:::

::: exemple
Build two adjectives, step by step:

1. **wind** — count the letters at the end: w-i-**n-d**, two consonants, so there is nothing to double → _wind + y_ = **windy**.
2. **sun** — count again: s-**u**-**n**, consonant + vowel + consonant → double the **n** → _sun + n + y_ = **sunny**.
3. Now use them: _It is a **windy** day in autumn._ · _It is a **sunny** day in summer._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _It is a **cloud** day._ ✗

It tempts you because the word _cloud_ is the one you know, and it is already in your head when you look up.

But _cloud_ names the **thing**; the **day** needs the adjective. The right sentence: _It is a **cloudy** day._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Make the adjective of **fog** and the adjective of **snow**, and say which one doubles its last letter.

---

**foggy** and **snowy**. Only **fog** doubles: f-o-g is consonant + vowel + consonant, so the **g** is written twice. _snow_ ends in the letter **w**, which is never doubled, so it simply takes the **y**.

:::

::: retenir
**Noun + y = adjective** (cloud → cloudy, rain → rainy, wind → windy) — and a consonant + vowel + consonant noun doubles its last letter: sun → **sunny**, fog → **foggy**.

:::

## 🌤️ What's the weather like?

Your cousin telephones you from another town. He cannot see your sky, so he asks you one question — always the same one, word for word.

And the answer is not _yes_ or _no_: he wants the **weather**. So how do you ask, and how do you answer?

::: propriete
The question is fixed: **What's the weather like?**

The answer is fixed too: **It's** + the **adjective**.

| Question                 | Answer                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| What's the weather like? | **It's sunny.**          |
| What's the weather like? | **It's cold and windy.** |
| What's the weather like? | **It's rainy today.**    |

The adjectives of the weather: **hot**, **warm**, **cool**, **cold**, **sunny**, **cloudy**, **rainy**, **windy**, **snowy**, **foggy**. The **sky** can be **clear**, and the **air** can be **fresh**.

:::

::: exemple
Answer your cousin, step by step:

1. Look outside: there is no cloud, the **sky** is **clear** and the sun is strong.
2. Turn each thing into its adjective: sun → **sunny**, and the air is **hot**.
3. Open the answer with **It's**, then join the two adjectives with _and_ → _**It's** hot and sunny._

:::

> 🗡️ One question, two useful answers: _It's cold._ speaks about the **weather**, _I'm cold._ speaks about **you**. The weather takes **it**, never _I_.

::: verifie
It is December, the wind is strong and the rain is falling. Ask the question and give the answer.

---

_**What's the weather like?** — **It's** rainy and windy._ — the question never changes, and the answer opens with **It's** and puts the adjectives after it (rain → **rainy**, wind → **windy**).

:::

::: retenir
**What's the weather like?** is the fixed question · **It's + adjective** is the fixed answer (It's cold. · It's sunny and windy.).

:::

## 🔮 Next spring — will and won't

Today it is raining and you are inside. Tomorrow the sky will open, and you already know what you are going to do: catch butterflies in the field.

Everything in that plan is **after** today. It has not happened yet, so the verb you know is not enough. What do you put in front of it?

::: propriete
To speak about the **future**, put **will** in front of the bare verb:

**will + verb** — _I **will go** to the beach._ · _She **will play** outside._

The verb after **will** can be the verb **to be** itself, and it stays bare too: _Tomorrow it **will be** sunny._

**will** never changes: I, you, he, she, it, we, they — all of them take **will**, and the verb after it stays bare, with no **-s** and no _to_. The short form is **'ll**: _I\**'ll\** go._

For the negative, use **won't** (the short form of **will not**): _It **won't rain** tomorrow._

The words that announce the future: **tomorrow**, **next week**, **next month**, **next year**, **next spring**.

:::

::: exemple
Write your plan for tomorrow, step by step:

1. The moment is **tomorrow**, so it is after today → the sentence needs **will**.
2. Put **will** in front of the bare verb: _I **will catch** butterflies._ — never _I will to catch_, never _I will catches_.
3. Add what will not happen, with **won't** : _It **won't rain** tomorrow._

The whole plan: _Tomorrow I **will catch** butterflies in the field, and it **won't rain**._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Next spring she **will goes** to the beach._ ✗

It tempts you because you have learned that **she** adds an **-s** to the verb. That rule belongs to what happens **every day**, not to what **will** happen.

After **will**, the verb is always bare. The right sentence: _Next spring she **will go** to the beach._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete with will or won't: "Next summer we ___ swim in the sea, and we ___ go to school."

---

_Next summer we **will** swim in the sea, and we **won't** go to school._ — swimming in the sea is what will happen, so it takes **will**; going to school is what will not happen in the summer holidays, so it takes **won't**. Both verbs stay bare.

:::

::: retenir
**will + bare verb** for the future (I will go · She will play) · **won't + bare verb** for the negative · future words: tomorrow, next week, next month, next year.

:::

## 🌡️ Colder than — comparing two things

January and April are both cold in the morning. You have the word **cold** for each of them separately, and it is true twice.

But you want to say something about the **two together**: one of them is more cold than the other. English does not say _more cold_ here. So what does it say?

::: propriete
To compare two things with a **short adjective**, add **-er** to it and follow with **than**:

$$ adjective + er + than $$

| Adjective | + er        | Sentence                                      |
| --------- | ----------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **cold**  | **colder**  | January is **colder than** April.             |
| **warm**  | **warmer**  | Spring is **warmer than** winter.             |
| **cool**  | **cooler**  | Autumn is **cooler than** summer.             |
| **long**  | **longer**  | June days are **longer than** May days.       |
| **short** | **shorter** | December days are **shorter than** June days. |
| **hot**   | **hotter**  | July is **hotter than** May.                  |

The last line doubles its last letter for the same reason as _sunny_: **hot** is consonant + vowel + consonant (h-o-t), so the **t** is written twice.

:::

::: exemple
Compare two seasons, step by step:

1. Choose the adjective that describes both: summer and spring are both warm → the adjective is **warm**.
2. Add **-er** to it → **warmer**.
3. Name the second thing after **than** → _Summer is **warmer than** spring._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Summer is **more warm** than spring._ ✗ and _Summer is warmer **that** spring._ ✗

The first one copies the way your own language compares; the second one hears _than_ and writes the word it knows, _that_.

A short adjective takes **-er**, and the second thing always comes after **than**. The right sentence: _Summer is **warmer than** spring._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Compare the nights of December and the nights of June with the adjective **long**.

---

_The nights of December are **longer than** the nights of June._ — the short adjective **long** takes **-er** (longer), and the thing you compare it with comes after **than**.

:::

::: retenir
**short adjective + er + than** compares two things: colder than, warmer than, longer than — and a consonant + vowel + consonant adjective doubles its last letter (hot → **hotter**).

:::

## ⛷️ Verb + ing = the activity

In summer you **are on holidays**: you swim, you play on the **beach**, you make a **sandcastle**. In winter you put on your **boots**, your **gloves** and your **socks**, and you walk in the **fresh air**.

Those are actions. Now name them, as you would on a list of the things you love. Which form do you write?

::: propriete
Add **-ing** to the verb and the action becomes the **name of the activity**:

$$ Verb + ing = Activity $$

| Verb        | Activity       | In the sentence                             |
| ----------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **play**    | **playing**    | I like **playing** on the beach.            |
| **swim**    | **swimming**   | **Swimming** is my summer activity.         |
| **catch**   | **catching**   | I enjoy **catching** butterflies.           |
| **collect** | **collecting** | **Collecting** flowers is a spring game.    |
| **walk**    | **walking**    | **Walking** in the fresh air keeps you fit. |

**swim** doubles its last letter — s-w-**i**-**m** is consonant + vowel + consonant, exactly like _sun_ and _hot_.

:::

::: exemple
Name two winter activities, step by step:

1. The action is _walk_. Add **-ing**, and check the end: w-a-l-**k**, with an **l** before it, so nothing doubles → **walking**.
2. The action is _run_. Add **-ing**, and check the end: r-**u**-**n**, consonant + vowel + consonant, so the **n** doubles → **running**.
3. Put them in a sentence with the verbs of Unit I: _I enjoy **walking** in the fresh air, but I hate **running** in the snow._

:::

::: verifie
Nadia is on the beach with her bucket. Name her activity, and name the activity of her brother, who is in the water.

---

Nadia is **making a sandcastle**, and her brother is **swimming**. Both names are built the same way: the verb plus **-ing** — and _swim_ doubles its **m**, because it is consonant + vowel + consonant.

:::

::: retenir
**Verb + ing = the activity** (play → playing, collect → collecting) — and a consonant + vowel + consonant verb doubles its last letter: swim → **swimming**, run → **running**.

:::

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the sound of "the"

Say the word **the** out loud and stop on its first sound. Your tongue is not behind your teeth: it comes **between** them, and something in your throat shakes.

That sound has a name in the books: **/ð/**. Which other words of this chapter carry it?

::: propriete
The sound **/ð/** is the first sound of **the**. You make it with the tongue **between** the teeth, and the throat shakes.

Words of this unit that carry it: **weather** · **with** · **them** · **this** · **mother**.

Two letters write it, always the same two: **th**. But careful — the same two letters can also make a sound where the throat stays **quiet**, as in _teeth_ and _three_. Only your ear can sort them.

:::

::: exemple
Test two words with your hand flat on your throat, step by step:

1. Say **weather** slowly and hold the middle sound: the throat **shakes** → the sound is **/ð/**, like in _the_.
2. Say **teeth** and hold the last sound: the air passes, but the throat stays **quiet** → this is not **/ð/**.
3. The spelling gave you no help at all: both words are written with **th**. It is the shaking, never the letters, that decides.

:::

::: verifie
In one of these three words the throat stays quiet: _mother_, _three_, _this_. Which one?

---

**three** — its **th** lets the air pass and the throat stays quiet. In _mother_ and in _this_ the throat shakes: those two carry the **/ð/** of _the_, like _weather_ and _them_.

:::

::: retenir
**/ð/** is the sound of **the**: tongue between the teeth, throat shaking — weather, with, them, this, mother.

:::
