# ⚔️ Entertaining Myself — The Gate of Free Time

> 💡 "Your first English words are not for the exam. They are for the afternoon when school is over and a friend asks: _what shall we do?_"

## 🎣 Free time — the words for what you do

School is over. It is four o'clock, your bag is on the floor and the afternoon belongs to you. You already know what you do: you take your bike, or your fishing rod, or you help your mother with the flowers.

The question is new, and it is the one this whole unit answers: **how do you say it in English?**

::: definition
A **leisure activity** (also called a **free time activity**) is something you do for fun, not for school and not for work.

The activities of this unit: **fishing**, **camping** (we say _go camping_), **riding a bike**, **gardening**, **drawing**, **shopping**, going to the **museum**, playing **hide and seek**, **rope skipping**, **flying a kite**.

:::

Read the four names under the drawings: three of them end in **-ing** (_riding_, _fishing_, _gardening_). That ending is not a decoration — it turns the action into the **name of the activity**, and you will need it in every section of this chapter.

::: exemple
Yassine tells you about his Saturday. Name each activity in English:

1. He takes his bike and goes to the park → the action is _ride_, so the activity is **riding a bike** (the action becomes a name with **-ing**).
2. He takes his rod and sits by the river → the activity is **fishing**.
3. He waters the flowers with his mother → the activity is **gardening**.

:::

::: verifie
Nadia takes her kite to the park. Name her activity in English.

---

She is **flying a kite**. The action is _fly_, and an activity is named with the **-ing** form: _fly_ becomes _flying_.

:::

::: retenir
A free time activity is named with the verb + **-ing**: fishing, camping, gardening, riding a bike, flying a kite.

:::

## 💚 Saying what you like — and why

Your friend asks: _What is your favourite free time activity?_ You can answer with one word — _Fishing._ It is correct, and it is very short.

You want to say more: that you love it, why you love it, and what you do **not** love. So how do you build the full sentence?

::: propriete
Four verbs say how you feel about an activity. After each one, the activity keeps its **-ing** form:

| You feel                | Structure                 | Example                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| I love it               | **like** + verb + **ing** | I **like fishing**.     |
| I love it a lot         | **enjoy** + verb + ing    | I **enjoy gardening**.  |
| I do not love it        | **dislike** + verb + ing  | I **dislike shopping**. |
| I really do not love it | **hate** + verb + ing     | I **hate waiting**.     |

Three small words finish the sentence: **because** gives the reason, **and** joins two ideas that go together, **but** joins two ideas that are different.

:::

::: exemple
Build Salma's sentence in three steps:

1. The activity after _enjoy_ takes **-ing** → _I enjoy gardening._
2. **because** always introduces the **reason**, never the activity → _because I like flowers._
3. The second idea is the opposite, so **but** joins it, not _and_ → _but I dislike shopping._

The whole sentence: _I enjoy gardening **because** I like flowers, **but** I dislike shopping._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _I enjoy **to swim**._ ✗

It tempts you because your own language puts the infinitive there, and your ear asks for _to_. English does not: after **like**, **enjoy**, **hate** and **dislike**, the verb takes **-ing**.

The right sentence: _I enjoy **swimming**._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete with the right forms: "I ___ (hate) ___ (wait) for the bus ___ it is boring."

---

_I **hate waiting** for the bus **because** it is boring._ — after _hate_ the verb takes **-ing** (_waiting_), and **because** opens the reason.

:::

::: retenir
like / enjoy / hate / dislike + verb + **ing**; **because** gives the reason, **and** and **but** join two ideas.

:::

## ⏰ How often? — the adverbs of frequency

Meriem goes fishing every Sunday of the year. Karim goes fishing one Sunday a year. Both of them can say the same sentence: _I go fishing._

Something important is missing, and everybody feels it: **how often** do they go?

::: propriete
An **adverb of frequency** answers the question _how often?_ Four of them belong to this unit:

| Adverb        | How often           |
| ------------- | ------------------- |
| **always**    | every time          |
| **usually**   | most times          |
| **sometimes** | not often           |
| **never**     | not one single time |

It goes **before the verb**: _I **always** play._ — _She **never** plays._

And with **he**, **she** or **it**, the verb itself takes an **-s**: _I play_ but _she play**s**_.

:::

::: exemple
Write Meriem's sentence, step by step:

1. _Meriem_ is one girl, so she is **she**, and the verb takes its **-s** → _plays_.
2. The adverb goes **before** the verb, never after it → _Meriem **usually** plays_.
3. Add the activity and the moment → _Meriem usually plays volleyball on Saturday._

:::

::: verifie
Put these words in the right order: _never / my brother / goes / camping_

---

_My brother **never goes** camping._ — the adverb comes **before** the verb (_never goes_), and _my brother_ is _he_, so the verb keeps its **-s**.

:::

::: retenir
always, usually, sometimes, never go **before** the verb — and with he / she / it the verb takes an **-s**.

:::

## 📅 When? — the prepositions on and in

Meriem plays volleyball. Your next question is normal: _when?_ She answers with two very small words — _on Saturday_, _in the afternoon_.

They are both correct, and you cannot exchange them. So which one goes where?

::: propriete

| Preposition | Use it with                    | Examples                                                     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **on**      | a day, or a day + a part of it | **on** Monday, **on** the weekend, **on** Monday morning     |
| **in**      | a part of the day, with no day | **in** the morning, **in** the afternoon, **in** the evening |

The rule in one line: name a **day** and you need **on**; name only a **part of the day** and you need **in**.

:::

::: exemple
Choose the preposition for _Sunday_ and for _the evening_, step by step:

1. _Sunday_ is a **day** → **on** Sunday.
2. _the evening_ is a **part of the day** with no day → **in** the evening.
3. Now put the day first: the day commands the whole group, so **on** stays → _on Sunday evening._

:::

> ⚠️ _in Monday_ ✗ — a day never takes _in_. Say _on Monday._

::: verifie
Complete: "We go to the museum ___ the weekend, and we come back ___ the evening."

---

_We go to the museum **on** the weekend, and we come back **in** the evening._ — _the weekend_ names days, so it takes **on**; _the evening_ is only a part of the day, so it takes **in**.

:::

::: retenir
**on** + a day (on Friday, on the weekend, on Friday morning) · **in** + a part of the day (in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening).

:::

## 💡 Making suggestions — Let's, How about, What about

Saturday morning. Your cousin is at your house and says: _I'm bored._ You have an idea — the zoo is not far.

An idea for **two people together** is a **suggestion**. And a suggestion has its own three structures.

::: propriete

| Structure                           | Example                               |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **Let's** + verb (no _to_, no -ing) | **Let's go** to the zoo.              |
| **How about** + verb + **ing**?     | **How about going** to the zoo?       |
| **What about** + verb + **ing**?    | **What about playing** hide and seek? |

Your friend answers _Good idea!_ or _Great!_ — or refuses politely: _Sorry, I'm tired._

:::

::: exemple
Take one idea — _play football_ — and say it in the three ways:

1. After **Let's**, the verb stays bare: no _to_, no _-ing_ → _Let's play football._
2. **How about** and **What about** end with _about_, and after that little word a verb always takes **-ing** → _How about playing football?_
3. Watch the end of the sentence: _Let's…_ takes a full stop, _How about…?_ takes a question mark.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Let's **to go** to the zoo._ ✗ and _How about **go** to the zoo?_ ✗

The two structures get mixed, because they mean exactly the same thing. Keep them apart: **Let's** takes the **bare verb** (_Let's go_), **How about** and **What about** take **-ing** (_How about going_).

:::

::: verifie
Your friend is bored. Suggest the cinema in two different ways.

---

_Let's go to the cinema._ and _How about going to the cinema?_ — after **Let's** the verb is bare (_go_), after **How about** it takes **-ing** (_going_), and that second form asks a question, so it ends with a question mark.

:::

::: retenir
**Let's** + bare verb · **How about / What about** + verb + **ing** — three ways to suggest the same thing.

:::

## 🎟️ Inviting — Would you like to…?

A suggestion is for us two, together. Now something else: your team plays on Saturday and you want Amine to come with you.

You are not deciding for him — you are **inviting** him, and he must be free to say no. English has one polite structure for exactly that.

::: propriete
To **invite** someone, or to **offer** something, use:

**Would you like + to + verb?** — _Would you like to join our team?_

The answers are short and fixed: **Yes, please.** / **Yes, I'd love to.** — or **No, thanks.**

:::

::: exemple
Invite Amine to your picnic, step by step:

1. Open with **Would you like** — it is polite, and it leaves him free to refuse.
2. _Would like_ is always followed by **to** + the bare verb → _to come_.
3. Finish with the place and the moment → _Would you like to come to our picnic on Sunday?_

:::

::: verifie
Nadia has two tickets for the match and wants Sarra with her. Write her invitation.

---

_Would you like to come to the match?_ — **Would you like** opens the polite invitation, and it is followed by **to + verb** (_to come_), never by the -ing form.

:::

::: retenir
**Would you like + to + verb?** invites or offers — answer _Yes, please._ / _Yes, I'd love to._ / _No, thanks._

:::

## 👪 Whose is it? — the possessive adjectives

Two bikes are waiting at the picnic: one belongs to Amine, one belongs to Nadia. You can say _Amine's bike_ and _Nadia's bike_ — but you will repeat those names ten times in one afternoon.

English has seven small words that replace the name of the owner. Which one do you choose?

::: propriete
A **possessive adjective** says **whose** something is. It comes **before** the noun, and it never changes with the noun:

| Subject | Possessive | Example     |
| ------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| I       | **my**     | my kite     |
| you     | **your**   | your ball   |
| he      | **his**    | his bike    |
| she     | **her**    | her bike    |
| it      | **its**    | its name    |
| we      | **our**    | our team    |
| they    | **their**  | their kites |

:::

::: exemple
Amine has a red bike. Write the sentence, step by step:

1. _Amine_ is one boy → he is **he**, so the possessive is **his**.
2. The possessive stands **before** the noun → _his bike_.
3. The noun does not change the word: _his bike_, _his bikes_ — one bike or ten, it stays **his**.

The sentence: _This is **his** bike._ And for Nadia, who is **she**: _This is **her** bike._ Same bike, different owner, different word.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Nadia and **his** kite._ ✗

It tempts you because your own language chooses the word from the **object**. English chooses it from the **owner** — and Nadia is a girl.

The right sentence: _Nadia and **her** kite._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "Karim is with ___ sister. They are flying ___ kite together."

---

_Karim is with **his** sister. They are flying **their** kite together._ — Karim alone is _he_, so the word is **his**; Karim and his sister are _they_, so the word is **their**. The owner chooses, never the object.

:::

::: retenir
my, your, his, her, its, our, their stand **before** the noun — and it is the **owner** who chooses the word.

:::

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the s that hisses and the s that buzzes

Say _let's_ out loud. Now say _plays_. Both words end with the same letter, and your mouth does two different things.

Put your hand flat on your throat and say them again. One of them makes your throat shake. Which one, and why does it matter?

::: propriete
The letter **s** hides two sounds:

| Sound   | It does…            | Words of this unit                          |
| ------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **/s/** | hisses like a snake | let's · cinema · restaurant · hide and seek |
| **/z/** | buzzes like a bee   | zoo · museum · plays · present              |

The test is your throat: with **/z/** it shakes, with **/s/** it stays quiet.

:::

::: exemple
Sort two words with the throat test, step by step:

1. Say _plays_ slowly and hold the last sound: the throat shakes → the sound is **/z/**.
2. Say _let's_ and hold the last sound: the air hisses, the throat is quiet → the sound is **/s/**.
3. The spelling is the same letter in both words, so it is your **ear** that decides, never the letter.

:::

::: verifie
In one of these three words the letter s buzzes: _museum_, _restaurant_, _seek_. Which one?

---

**museum** — its s buzzes and the throat shakes on it, so the sound is **/z/**. In _restaurant_ and in _seek_ the letter s only hisses, and the throat stays quiet: the sound there is **/s/**.

:::

::: retenir
One letter, two sounds: the hissing **/s/** (let's, cinema) and the buzzing **/z/** (zoo, plays).

:::
