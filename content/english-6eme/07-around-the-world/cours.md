# ⚔️ Around the World — The Last Gate

> 💡 "Six gates taught you to speak about your street. This one hands you a map of the whole world — and the whole world speaks English to a traveller."

## 🧭 North, south, east, west — reading the world

A big map of the world is open on the table, and your finger is on Tunisia. Your teacher asks a simple question: _Where is Italy?_

You can answer _up there, above the sea_. Every child in the class knows what you mean — and not one traveller, one pilot or one sailor in the world would accept it. They use four words instead, and those four words never change.

::: definition
The four **cardinal directions** say where a place is on the **world** map:

**North** is at the top of the map, **south** at the bottom, **east** on the right, **west** on the left.

A **country** is a land with its own name, its own flag and its own people — Tunisia, Italy, Japan, Spain. To **travel** is to go from one place to another, often far away.

:::

The four arrows of the drawing carry the four letters you will see on every map in the world: **N** for north, **S** for south, **E** for east, **W** for west. Turn the paper and the words stay true, because they do not describe the paper — they describe the Earth.

::: exemple
Answer your teacher's question, step by step:

1. Find Tunisia on the map, then find Italy: Italy is **above** Tunisia, near the top.
2. The top of a map is always the **north**.
3. Say it the way a traveller says it → _Italy is **north** of Tunisia._

And the other way round, with the same map: _Tunisia is **south** of Italy._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _Japan is on the **right** of the map._ ✗

It tempts you because your eyes really do see it on the right — but the right of a map is only the right of that sheet of paper. Turn the paper and your sentence becomes false, while a direction never changes.

The right sentence: _Japan is in the **east**._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
On the map, Spain is on the left of Tunisia, near the top. Give the two directions a traveller would use.

---

Spain is **north** and **west** of Tunisia — the top of the map is the **north** and its left side is the **west**. A traveller would say: _Spain is in the north-west._

:::

::: retenir
**North** at the top, **south** at the bottom, **east** on the right, **west** on the left — four words that describe the Earth, never the sheet of paper.

:::

## 💡 "Let's travel to…" — suggesting a journey

The map is still open and the summer holidays start in three weeks. Your whole family is around the table, and nobody has said a word yet.

You have an idea. In Unit I you learned to suggest a game in the street; the very same three structures now carry your whole family to another country.

::: propriete
Three ways to say the same idea to somebody:

| Structure                           | Example                                |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **Let's** + verb (no _to_, no -ing) | **Let's travel** to Italy this summer. |
| **How about** + verb + **ing**?     | **How about visiting** Rome?           |
| **What about** + verb + **ing**?    | **What about going** to Spain?         |

The answers are short: _Good idea!_ / _Great!_ — or a polite refusal: _Sorry, it is too far._

:::

::: exemple
Turn your idea — _travel to Italy_ — into the three structures, step by step:

1. After **Let's** the verb stays bare, with nothing in front of it → _Let's travel to Italy._
2. **How about** and **What about** both end with the little word _about_, and after it a verb always takes **-ing** → _How about travelling to Italy?_
3. Watch the end of the line: _Let's…_ takes a full stop, the two questions take a question mark.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _How about **travel** to Italy?_ ✗

The three structures say exactly the same thing, so beginners mix their shapes. Keep the little word **about** in your ear: whatever comes after it takes **-ing**.

The right question: _How about **travelling** to Italy?_ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Your cousin wants to see the sea. Suggest a journey to Sousse in two different ways.

---

_Let's **go** to Sousse._ and _What about **going** to Sousse?_ — after **Let's** the verb is bare, and after **What about** it takes **-ing**. The second one is a question, so it ends with a question mark.

:::

::: retenir
**Let's** + bare verb · **How about / What about** + verb + **ing** — three ways to suggest one journey.

:::

## 🤝 Greetings around the world

The plane lands. The door opens. And before you buy anything, before you ask anything, you must do the very first thing two human beings do: **greet** each other.

You already know how it is done at home. But your hand, your cheek and your head do not mean the same thing in every country.

::: definition
To **greet** somebody is to say hello when you meet. Four ways of greeting travel around the world:

| Country                | People greet each other by…                      |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| England, the USA       | **shaking hands**                                 |
| Tunisia, France, Spain | giving a **kiss** on each **cheek**               |
| Japan                  | **bowing** — they bend the head and the back      |
| New Zealand (Maori)    | **touching** noses and foreheads gently           |

The words to build the sentences: people **greet each other**, they **use** their hands or their heads, and what they **think** is polite at home may surprise a traveller.

:::

::: exemple
Describe two greetings, step by step:

1. Name the people first → _In Japan, people…_
2. Choose the verb of the greeting → _…**bow** to each other._
3. Say what the body does, so a reader who has never seen it can picture it → _They bend the head and the back, and they do not touch._

The second one: _In Tunisia, people **greet each other** with a **kiss** on each **cheek**._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: thinking that one country's greeting is the polite one and the others are strange. ✗

It tempts every traveller, because the greeting of your own childhood feels natural, like walking. It is not: it is a **custom**, learned exactly like the others.

The right idea: every country has its own greeting, and a good traveller **uses the greeting of the country they are in**. ✓

:::

::: verifie
A Japanese friend arrives at your school in Tunisia. What does he do, and what do your classmates do?

---

He **bows** — he bends his head and his back, without touching, because that is the greeting of **Japan**. Your classmates **shake hands** with him or give him a **kiss on each cheek**, because that is the greeting of **Tunisia**: the two of them **greet each other**, each with his own custom.

:::

::: retenir
To **greet** = to say hello when you meet: people **shake hands**, give a **kiss** on the **cheek**, **bow**, or **touch** noses — one world, many customs.

:::

## 🅰️ a, an, the — the three little words

You want to tell your family about your journey: _I saw amphitheatre in Rome. Then I visited amphitheatre of El Jem._

Both sentences are missing something small, and an English ear hears the hole at once. English almost never leaves a noun alone.

::: propriete
Three little words stand in front of a noun. Two of them say **one, any one**:

- **a** before a **consonant**: **a** cape · **a** country · **a** dancer · **a** festival
- **an** before a **vowel** (a, e, i, o, u): **an** amphitheatre · **an** island · **an** Indian · **an** egg

And one of them says **that one, the one we both know**:

- **the** before anything already named, or unique in the world: **the** world · **the** north · **the** Eiffel Tower · **the** pyramids of Egypt

:::

::: exemple
Repair the two sentences of the journey, step by step:

1. The first amphitheatre is new for your listener — one among many — so it takes **a** or **an**. The word _amphitheatre_ begins with the vowel **a**, so the little word is **an** → _I saw **an** amphitheatre in Rome._
2. The second one is not any amphitheatre: it is the famous one, the one everybody in Tunisia knows → **the** → _Then I visited **the** amphitheatre of El Jem._
3. Read the pair aloud: **an** opens the story, **the** points at something already known. That is the whole difference.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _**a** amphitheatre_ ✗ and _**an** cape_ ✗

It tempts you because both little words mean the same thing, so the hand takes the first one. What separates them is not their meaning: it is the **first letter of the next word**. A vowel is hard to say after _a_, so English slides an **n** in.

The right forms: _**an** amphitheatre_ ✓ and _**a** cape_ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "In Spain I watched ___ dancer in ___ street, and ___ festival began at midnight."

---

_In Spain I watched **a** dancer in **a** street, and **the** festival began at midnight._ — _dancer_ and _street_ both begin with a consonant and are new for the listener, so both take **a**; the **festival** is the one the whole sentence is about, already known, so it takes **the**.

:::

::: retenir
**a** + consonant (a cape) · **an** + vowel (an Indian) · **the** for what is already known or unique (the world, the pyramids).

:::

## 🚆 Travel by, travel to — and what you prefer

The family has chosen the country. Now the second question arrives, and it splits the table in two: the plane is fast, the boat is slow but you can see the sea for a whole day.

Two little words carry the answer, and one verb says which side you are on.

::: propriete
Two prepositions never change:

- **travel by** + a means of transport: travel **by** plane, **by** train, **by** car, **by** boat, **by** bus.
- **travel to** + a place: travel **to** Italy, **to** Rome, **to** the mountains.

And to say which one you choose, the verb **prefer** takes two shapes, both correct:

| Structure                    | Example                            |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **prefer to** + verb         | I **prefer to travel** by plane.   |
| **prefer** + verb + **ing**  | I **prefer travelling** by plane.  |

A plane is **fast**, a boat is **slow**, and on a long journey you **spend** many hours inside.

:::

Under each drawing the same little word comes back: **by**. It is the word of the **vehicle**, and it never takes _a_ or _the_ after it — _by plane_, never _by a plane_.

::: exemple
Say which side of the table you are on, step by step:

1. Name the one you choose with **prefer**, then take one of its two shapes → _I prefer **to travel**…_ or _I prefer **travelling**…_
2. The vehicle takes **by**, with no little word after it → _…**by** boat…_
3. The place takes **to** → _…**to** Italy._

The whole sentence: _I prefer to travel **by** boat **to** Italy, because I like the sea._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _I travel **by** Italy._ ✗

It tempts you because one journey holds both words at the same moment, and they sit side by side in the sentence. Sort them by what follows: a **vehicle** takes **by**, a **place** takes **to**.

The right sentence: _I travel **to** Italy **by** plane._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Your family chooses the train, and the city is Sousse. Write the sentence with the verb prefer.

---

_We prefer **to travel by** train **to** Sousse._ (or _We prefer **travelling by** train to Sousse._) — _prefer_ accepts both shapes, the **train** is a vehicle so it takes **by**, and **Sousse** is a place so it takes **to**.

:::

::: retenir
**travel by** + a vehicle (by plane, by train) · **travel to** + a place (to Italy) · **prefer to** + verb = **prefer** + verb + **ing**.

:::

## 📏 Bigger, older, faster — the comparative

Your cousin says the plane wins. You say the boat wins. Neither of you will ever win, because _it wins_ is only an opinion.

Put the two of them side by side with a number in your hand, and English gives you one ending — three letters that end the argument.

::: propriete
To compare **two** things, a short adjective takes **-er**, and the second thing comes after **than**:

_A plane is **faster than** a boat._ — _Italy is **bigger than** Tunisia._

Three spellings to watch:

| The adjective ends in…              | You add…         | Example                    |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------- |
| a normal consonant                  | **-er**          | old → old**er**            |
| **-e**                              | only **-r**      | nice → nice**r**           |
| consonant + **-y**                  | **-ier**         | easy → eas**ier**          |
| one short consonant-vowel-consonant | double + **-er** | big → bi**gger**           |

:::

::: exemple
Compare the plane and the boat, step by step:

1. Choose the adjective that says what you mean: the plane takes three hours, the boat takes a whole day → **fast**.
2. _Fast_ ends in a normal consonant, so it simply takes **-er** → **faster**.
3. The thing you compare it with comes after **than** → _A plane is **faster than** a boat._

And your cousin can answer with the same tool: _A boat is **slower than** a plane, but it is **nicer**._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _A plane is **more fast** than a boat._ ✗

It tempts you because your own language puts a little word like _more_ in front of the adjective. Short English adjectives refuse it: they carry the comparison **inside** themselves, in their ending.

The right sentence: _A plane is **faster** than a boat._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Big Ben was finished in 1859 and the Eiffel Tower in 1889. Compare them with the adjective old.

---

_Big Ben is **older than** the Eiffel Tower._ — 1859 comes before 1889, so Big Ben is the older of the two; _old_ ends in a normal consonant, so it takes **-er**, and the second monument follows **than**.

:::

::: retenir
Two things compared: short adjective + **-er** + **than** (faster than, bigger than, older than) — never _more_ in front of a short adjective.

:::

## 🏛️ The tallest, the oldest — the superlative and the monuments of the world

Two monuments, and _-er than_ was enough. Now put **five** of them in one line — the pyramids, El Jem, the Eiffel Tower, Big Ben, the Great Wall — and ask which one stands above them all.

No _than_ can answer that. For the top of a whole group, English changes both ends of the adjective.

::: propriete
To put one thing **above a whole group**, a short adjective takes **the** in front and **-est** behind:

_The Eiffel Tower is **the tallest** monument of the five._ — _The pyramids are **the oldest**._

The spellings of the comparative come back, unchanged: old → **the oldest** · nice → **the nicest** · easy → **the easiest** · big → **the biggest**.

The five monuments of this unit:

| Monument               | Country | Town    | Age                     |
| ---------------------- | ------- | ------- | ----------------------- |
| the pyramids           | Egypt   | Giza    | about 4500 years old    |
| the amphitheatre       | Tunisia | El Jem  | built about 1800 years ago |
| Big Ben                | England | London  | finished in 1859        |
| the Eiffel Tower       | France  | Paris   | finished in 1889        |
| the Great Wall         | China   | —       | the longest wall on Earth |

:::

The three shapes are drawn to the same scale, so the drawing tells you the numbers: the Eiffel Tower is 330 metres high, the Great Pyramid of Giza is 139 metres, and the tower of Big Ben is 96 metres. The tallest one wins by a long way.

Answer the question _which one is the tallest?_, step by step:

2. Take the adjective **tall**, put **the** in front and **-est** behind → **the tallest**.
3. Read the three heights: 330 is bigger than 139 and than 96 → _The Eiffel Tower is **the tallest** of the three._

And with the ages: the pyramids are about 4500 years old, so _the pyramids are **the oldest** monument of the unit._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _The Eiffel Tower is **tallest** than Big Ben._ ✗

It tempts you because the two endings live in the same family and the sentence mentions two monuments. Sort them by how many things you look at: **two** things → **-er … than**; a **whole group** → **the … -est**.

The right sentences: _The Eiffel Tower is **taller than** Big Ben._ ✓ and _The Eiffel Tower is **the tallest** of the three._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Among the pyramids, Big Ben and the Eiffel Tower, which one is the newest — and how do you say it?

---

_The Eiffel Tower is **the newest**._ — it was finished in 1889, after Big Ben in 1859 and long after the pyramids; three monuments make a **group**, so the adjective takes **the** in front and **-est** behind.

:::

::: retenir
One thing above a whole group: **the** + short adjective + **-est** (the tallest, the oldest, the longest) — and the Eiffel Tower, at 330 metres, is the tallest monument of this unit.

:::

## 🎉 Customs and habits — from Spain to Tunisia

A **monument** is easy to photograph and it never moves. What a traveller really brings home is something else: the way people eat, dance and celebrate.

Spain is the country this unit visits. Look at what its people do — then turn the question round and look at your own country as a traveller would.

::: definition
A **custom** is something the people of a country have done for a very long time; a **habit** is something a person does very often.

Four customs of **Spain**:

| Custom            | What happens                                                                 |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| paella            | a dish of rice with **seafood**, vegetables and red **pepper**                |
| flamenco          | a **dancer** claps, stamps and turns to the sound of a guitar                 |
| La Tomatina       | a **festival** where people **throw** tomatoes at each other for one morning  |
| the corrida       | a man with a red **cape** **fights** a **bull** in a round arena              |

The **flag** of Spain is red and yellow.

:::

::: exemple
Write about one custom of your own country the way a traveller would read it, step by step:

1. Name the country and the custom → _In Tunisia, families eat couscous…_
2. Say **when** it happens, because a custom always has its moment → _…on Friday._
3. Add the detail a foreigner cannot guess → _Everybody eats from one big dish, together._

The whole sentence: _In Tunisia, families eat couscous on Friday, and everybody eats from one big dish, together._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: writing _In Spain, people **are eating** paella._ ✗

It tempts you because you can see the scene in your head, happening now. But a custom is not what people are doing at this minute — it is what they do **again and again**, year after year.

The right sentence: _In Spain, people **eat** paella._ ✓ — the simple present is the tense of customs and habits.

:::

::: verifie
Your Spanish friend asks what Tunisians do at the end of Ramadan. Write one sentence about that custom.

---

_In Tunisia, families **visit** each other and **eat** cakes for the Eid._ — it is a **custom**, something the people do every year, so the verb stays in the **simple present** and not in a tense that says _now_.

:::

::: retenir
A **custom** belongs to a country and comes back every year (paella, flamenco, La Tomatina, couscous on Friday); a **habit** belongs to a person — and both are told in the **simple present**.

:::

> 🏆 The seventh and last gate is cleared. You started the year naming what you do on a free afternoon; you end it reading a world map, suggesting a journey, greeting people who do not greet the way you do, comparing the monuments of five countries and describing your own customs to a stranger. That is a whole year of English — and it is only the first one.
