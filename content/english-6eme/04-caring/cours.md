# ⚔️ Caring — The Gate of the Helping Hand

> 💡 "A hero is not the one who is strongest alone. It is the one the village calls when something must be done."

## 🤝 Caring — the words for helping

Dinner is over. The plates are dirty, the table is full, and your mother has worked all day. Nobody says anything, and everybody sees the same thing.

You stand up and you start. That small moment has a name in English, and this whole unit is built on it.

::: definition
To **care** means to think about somebody and to do something for them. To **take care of** somebody is to look after them.

The actions of this unit: to **help**, to **share**, to **wash the dishes**, to **tidy** the room, to **clean up** the street, to **come together**.

The words that describe what you find: **dirty** (it needs cleaning), **tidy** (everything is in its place), a **neighbour** (the person who lives next to you), **alone** (with nobody).

And two words for both directions at once: **each other** — _we help each other._

:::

::: exemple
Name what each person does, step by step:

1. Amine takes the dirty plates to the sink and washes them → he **washes the dishes**.
2. Sarra has one sandwich and gives half of it to her friend → she **shares** her sandwich.
3. The neighbours meet on Saturday and clean their street → they **come together** to **clean up** the street.

:::

::: verifie
Nadia helps Karim with his homework, and Karim helps Nadia with hers. Say it in English with two words.

---

_They help **each other**._ — the help goes in both directions at the same time, and English says that with **each other**, not with two separate sentences.

:::

::: retenir
**care · take care of · help · share · tidy · clean up · come together · each other** — the words of this unit.

:::

## 👪 Whose is it? — the possessive adjectives

Your neighbour carries two heavy bags. You take one. She says _thank you_ and points at a door: _this is my house_.

She did not say her name. One tiny word did all the work — and English has seven of them.

::: propriete
A **possessive adjective** says **whose** something is. It stands **before** the noun, and the **owner** chooses it:

| The owner is… | Possessive | Example            |
| ------------- | ---------- | ------------------ |
| I             | **my**     | my homework        |
| you           | **your**   | your street        |
| he            | **his**    | his dishes         |
| she           | **her**    | her bag            |
| it            | **its**    | its water          |
| we            | **our**    | our classroom      |
| they          | **their**  | their neighbours   |

:::

::: exemple
Write the sentence about Mrs Leila, your neighbour, step by step:

1. Mrs Leila is one woman → she is **she**, so the possessive is **her**.
2. The possessive stands **before** the noun → _her bags_.
3. Two bags or one, the word never changes → _I carry **her** bags._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _This is **she** bag._ ✗

It tempts you because _she_ is the word you have just used for Mrs Leila. But _she_ **does** things; it cannot own things.

The right sentence: _This is **her** bag._ ✓ — same person, and the owning word is a different word.

:::

::: verifie
Complete: "We clean ___ classroom every Friday, and the little pupils clean ___ classroom too."

---

_We clean **our** classroom every Friday, and the little pupils clean **their** classroom too._ — the first owner is _we_, so the word is **our**; the second owner is _they_, so the word is **their**.

:::

::: retenir
my, your, his, her, its, our, their come **before** the noun, and the **owner** chooses the word.

:::

## 🏆 The best of all — the superlative

Three streets meet at the market. One of them is clean, one is cleaner, and one of them beats both.

You already know how to compare **two** things: _cleaner than_. Now there are three, and you need the word that puts one at the top.

::: propriete
The **superlative** puts one thing **above all the others**. With a short adjective:

**the + short adjective + est** — _clean_ → _**the cleanest**_.

Three spellings to watch:

| The adjective…           | You add… | Example                            |
| ------------------------ | -------- | ---------------------------------- |
| ends in a consonant      | **-est** | old → the old**est**, young → the young**est** |
| ends in one short vowel + one consonant | double the consonant + **-est** | big → the big**gest** |
| ends in **-y**           | **y** becomes **i** + **-est** | happy → the happ**iest** |

:::

The three trees say the rule without a word: two trees are compared with **taller**, and only when the third one joins them does one tree become **the tallest**.

::: exemple
Your street, Amine's street and Sarra's street: yours is the cleanest of the three. Build the sentence, step by step:

1. _clean_ is a short adjective ending in a consonant → add **-est** → _cleanest_.
2. A superlative always carries **the** in front of it → _the cleanest_.
3. Name the group you compare inside → _My street is **the cleanest** street in the city._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _My street is **the cleaner** street in the city._ ✗

It tempts you because you learnt _cleaner_ first, and both words feel like "very clean". They are not the same job: **-er** compares **two** things (_cleaner **than** yours_), **the …-est** puts one **above all the others**.

The right sentence: _My street is **the cleanest** street in the city._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Amine is 10, Sarra is 11 and Karim is 12. Write one sentence about Amine with a superlative.

---

_Amine is **the youngest**._ — _young_ is a short adjective, so the superlative is **the** + young + **est**, and Amine is younger than both of the others, not just one.

:::

::: retenir
**the + short adjective + est** beats the whole group: the cleanest, the oldest, the youngest, the biggest, the happiest.

:::

## 🛠️ Verb + er = the person who does it

In the village, everybody works. One man grows wheat, one man builds houses, one woman teaches the children.

You know the three verbs already: _farm_, _build_, _teach_. English gets the three job names out of them with one ending.

::: propriete
Add **-er** to a verb and you get the **name of the person** who does that action:

**verb + er = noun** — _farm_ + _er_ = a **farmer**.

| The verb | The person       |
| -------- | ---------------- |
| farm     | a farm**er**     |
| build    | a build**er**    |
| teach    | a teach**er**    |
| clean    | a clean**er**    |
| garden   | a garden**er**   |
| play     | a play**er**     |

Two spellings: a verb that already ends in **-e** takes only **-r** (_drive_ → a drive**r**), and a short verb ending in one vowel + one consonant doubles it (_run_ → a run**ner**).

:::

Look at the four names: three of them are a verb you know plus **-er**, and the fourth, _driver_, is _drive_ with its final **e** eaten by the ending.

::: exemple
Find the person for the verb _build_, step by step:

1. The action is _build_ — the man **builds** houses.
2. Add the ending that names the person → build + **er**.
3. The verb does not end in _e_ and is not short with one vowel, so nothing else changes → _He is a **builder**._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: making **every** job name with -er, and inventing a verb for it — _a **carpent**_ ✗, _a **doct**_ ✗.

A **carpenter**, a **doctor** and a **vet** are job names too, but there is no verb _carpent_ and no verb _doct_ behind them: you learn those three as whole words.

The rule works the other way round only: from a **real verb** (farm, build, teach) you may build the person. ✓

:::

::: verifie
A woman drives the school bus every morning. Name her job in English.

---

_She is a **driver**._ — the verb is _drive_, and the person is the verb + the ending; _drive_ already ends in **e**, so it takes only **-r**, never _driveer_.

:::

::: retenir
**verb + er = the person**: farmer, builder, teacher, cleaner, gardener, player, driver — but a carpenter, a doctor and a vet come from no verb at all.

:::

## 🔮 Promises — the future with will and won't

Monday morning. Your class writes its charter on a big sheet of paper, and every line is a promise about **tomorrow**, not about today.

_I clean the blackboard_ is what you do every day. A promise needs another form.

::: propriete
To speak about the **future**, put **will** before the bare verb — and **will** never changes, whoever the subject is:

_I **will help**._ · _She **will help**._ · _They **will help**._

To say the future in the negative, use **will not**, or its short form **won't**:

_He **will not forget**._ = _He **won't forget**._

The short form of the affirmative glues to the subject: _I**'ll** tidy my desk._

:::

::: exemple
Turn "we tidy the classroom" into a promise for tomorrow, step by step:

1. Keep the subject and put **will** right after it → _We will…_
2. The verb stays **bare** — no _to_, no _-ing_, no _-s_ → _tidy_.
3. Finish with the moment → _We **will tidy** the classroom tomorrow._

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _She **will cleans** the board._ ✗

It tempts you because you learnt that _she_ makes the verb take an **-s**. That **-s** belongs to the present only. After **will**, the verb is always bare.

The right sentence: _She **will clean** the board._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Write the negative promise: Karim, walk in with dirty shoes. Use the short form.

---

_Karim **won't walk in** with dirty shoes._ — the negative future is _will not_, shortened to **won't**, and the verb after it stays bare (_walk_, never _walks_).

:::

::: retenir
**will + bare verb** for every subject · **will not = won't** · short form _I'll, we'll_.

:::

## 🐱 Orders — the imperative and Don't + verb

You carry your cat to the vet. She looks at it, then she gives you five short sentences, and each one begins with a verb.

She never says your name once. That is not rudeness — it is a grammar, and it is the fastest one in English.

::: propriete
The **imperative** gives an **order**, an instruction or a piece of advice. It has **no subject** and it uses the **bare verb**:

_**Close** the door._ · _**Give** your dog water twice a day._ · _**Wash** your hands._

To **forbid** something — an order not to do it, or a danger — put **Don't** in front:

**Don't + verb** — _**Don't hit** your cat._ · _**Don't give** chocolate to your dog._

:::

::: exemple
The vet wants two things from you. Write her two sentences, step by step:

1. Something to do: start straight with the bare verb, no _you_ → _**Take** your cat to the vet twice a year._
2. Something not to do: put **Don't** in front of the bare verb → _**Don't forget** its water._
3. Neither sentence names you: the person in front of you is the person who must do it.

:::

::: piege
The classic mistake: _**Not** close the door._ ✗ and _**You** close the door._ ✗

The first copies the negative word of your own language; the second adds a subject that an order never carries — with _you_ in front, the sentence stops being an order and becomes a simple statement about what you do.

The right sentences: _**Don't close** the door._ ✓ and _**Close** the door._ ✓

:::

::: verifie
Your little brother wants to give a big bone to your sick puppy. Tell him not to, in English.

---

_**Don't give** the bone to the puppy._ — the prohibition is **Don't** plus the bare verb, with no subject in front of it.

:::

::: retenir
**Imperative** = bare verb, no subject (_Close the door._) · **Prohibition** = **Don't + verb** (_Don't hit your cat._).

:::

## 🔊 Let's pronounce — the h you hear and the h you don't

Say _help_ out loud. Now say _hour_. Both words are written with an **h** at the very beginning, and your mouth does two completely different things.

In one of them the air comes out of your throat. In the other, the letter is there and the sound is not.

::: propriete
The letter **h** is written far more often than it is heard:

| You **hear** the /h/                      | You **don't hear** any /h/                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **h**ouse · **h**ome · **h**elp · **h**omework · **h**opscotch | **h**our · w**h**ere · dis**h**es · sc**h**ool · s**h**ould |

Two families hide the sound: a word where the first **h** is simply silent (_hour_), and a word where **h** sits behind another letter and the pair makes a new sound altogether (_where_, _dishes_, _school_, _should_).

:::

::: exemple
Sort two words with your ear, step by step:

1. Say _homework_ slowly: the air pushes out at the start → you **hear** the /h/.
2. Say _hour_ slowly: the word starts like _our_, with no push at all → you **don't hear** it.
3. Both are written with the same letter, so it is your ear that decides, never the spelling.

:::

::: verifie
In one of these three words the letter h is not heard at all: _help_, _school_, _home_. Which one?

---

**school** — its h hides behind the c and the two letters make one sound, so no /h/ comes out. In _help_ and in _home_ the h opens the word and you hear it clearly.

:::

::: retenir
You **hear** the h in help, house, home, homework · you **don't** in hour, where, dishes, school, should.

:::
